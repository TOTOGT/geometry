Bundling

Why ten, and what changes if it is not ten. Ten is a fact about hands; this is the fact underneath it.

You bundle at ten because you have ten fingers. Nothing else about arithmetic depends on it. Chapter 1 showed that a numeral never has two readings as long as no digit reaches ten. This chapter shows that the same is true for any bundle size, and then asks what each size costs you and what it buys.

§ 1

Any bundle works

On the table: make bundles of five instead of ten. Seven stones become one bundle and two loose, written 12 in fives. Nothing goes wrong, provided you never leave five loose stones unbundled. Five loose would be a bundle you forgot to tie.

For the teacher: Bundling.lean proves this for numerals of any length, in any base. If two digit lists of the same length, with every digit below the base, name the same number, they are the same list (unique_same_length). The one-line version for two digits was already in Numerals.lean; this is the general one. The script also checks it by brute force: 9,083 numerals in bases 2 to 12, and no two collide COMPUTED. And it keeps Chapter 1's break in view: let a digit reach the base, and [10, 1] and [0, 2] both name twenty.

§ 2

What a bundle size costs

Small bundles mean few symbols to learn but long numerals. Big bundles mean short numerals but many symbols, and a multiplication table that grows as the square.

One standard way to weigh the trade is symbols times length. Measured as b / ln b, it is smallest at three, with two close behind and ten well back (2.73, 2.89, 4.34) COMPUTED. By that measure the cheapest bundle is small. Ten is a compromise that hands happened to make.

§ 3

What a bundle size buys: fractions that end

A fraction 1/n ends, instead of repeating forever, exactly when every prime in n divides the base. So the base decides which shares are clean. Of the nineteen fractions 1/2 to 1/20:

In tens a third never ends. In twelves it is 0;4, four twelfths COMPUTED. That is the honest case for twelve, and a stronger one for sixty, which handles fifths as well.

This series argued the case for twelve in WP-45 · Dividing Unity. The script checks two of that paper's claims and both hold: twelve is the smallest number with six divisors, and the smallest that divides evenly into halves, thirds, quarters and sixths. It also records what the paper's title, “minimal sufficient arithmetic”, leaves out. Twelve is sufficient for those four cuts, not for fifths, and by this chapter's count sixty ends more fractions still.

§ 4

What to teach from this

  1. The rule is “never leave a full bundle loose”, not “count in tens”. Practise it in fives and in twelves, and ten stops looking like a law.
  2. Every choice of bundle trades symbols against length. Nobody's choice is free.
  3. The base decides which shares are clean. That is why dozens and sixties show up on clocks and in shops.
What is not claimed Which peoples counted in which bases, and when. No source on the history of numeral systems is held on this desk, so none is told here. WANTED Ifrah, The Universal History of Numbers; or Menninger, Number Words and Number Symbols.
