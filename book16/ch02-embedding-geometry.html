Embeddings: words as points, and the circle of the months

A shape inside a model that comes from a symmetry in the text it read.

When researchers look inside language models they find that the twelve months sit on a circle, years on a smooth curve, and cities on something like a map. A 2026 paper explains the circle from a symmetry in word statistics, and shows it appears even in small word-embedding models. This chapter builds the smallest version of that argument and prints every number from a script. It covers one thread of embeddings. It does not yet cover how embeddings are trained.

What was observed, and what the paper claims

The observation quoted: Karkada et al. 2026, abstract: calendar months arrange into a circle, historical years into a smooth one-dimensional curve with ripples, and the latitude and longitude of cities can be read off with a linear probe.

The claim of the paper quoted: abstract and introduction: co-occurrence statistics of words have translation symmetry. The frequency with which two months appear near each other depends only on the time interval between them. The authors prove that this symmetry makes embedding models learn Fourier representations, that is, sines and cosines. The circle is the longest wave, and the ripples are higher harmonics. They report the same geometry in shallow word-embedding models of the word2vec family and in large language models, and they report that it persists at intermediate embedding dimension even when all co-occurrences between months are removed.

1 · The smallest version: twelve months

Put the months in a ring, numbered 0 to 11, and let Cij = f(i − j), where the gap is measured around the ring. That is the symmetry: shifting every month by one place leaves C unchanged. For the toy I take f(d) = exp(−dc / 2.5), with dc the shorter way around the ring. This kernel is my choice, not measured from text assumption.

Cij = f(i − j mod 12)   ⇒   λk = Σd f(d) cos(2πkd/12),   eigenvectors cos(2πkj/12), sin(2πkj/12)

A matrix that depends only on the gap is called circulant, and its eigenvectors are the Fourier modes (proved in Book XXIV, Chapter 13). The script checks this directly: for each k from 0 to 11 it multiplies C by the Fourier vector and compares with λk times the vector. The largest discrepancy is below 10−12 computed.

An embedding gives each month a vector, and the Gram matrix of the vectors, W WT, is meant to reproduce C. Taking W from the eigendecomposition makes this exact (the script finds a maximum error below 10−7). Then I center the vectors and take the top two principal components, as the paper does for its plots. The result:

All twelve months land at the same distance from the center, to within 10−7, in calendar order, with equal angular steps. The top two principal components have equal variance (0.1736 each) because the k = 1 cosine and sine share one eigenvalue. That degeneracy is why the picture is a circle and not an ellipse. The k = 2 mode (variance 0.0536) is the first higher harmonic, the kind of component the paper identifies with the ripples.

A first attempt that failedThe largest eigenvalue of C (4.6069) belongs to the constant mode, which says only that every month co-occurs with every other. Taking the top two uncentered components gives the constant plus one cosine, and the points do not form a circle: the circular-agreement score is 0.370, against 1.000 after centering. Centering removes the constant mode before the circle appears.

2 · Years: open ends instead of a ring

Years do not wrap around, so the kernel depends on the gap but the matrix is no longer circulant. I use Kij = exp(−|i − j| / 6) on 30 points. Its top eigenvalues are 9.958, 6.326, 3.749, 2.310, 1.519, 1.062. The eigenvectors oscillate with 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 sign changes, in that order computed. They behave like sine and cosine waves fitted into a segment, with slightly different boundary behavior. That is the open-end counterpart of the ring's Fourier modes, and the paper's Figure 1 labels months periodic and years open for this reason.

A negative resultI also tested whether the first centered principal axis runs monotonically through the 30 years. It does not: the test returns false. So the toy does not by itself reproduce a clean single curve. I have not reproduced the paper's rippled-curve figure, and I have not worked out why this simple version differs.

3 · Why deleting the months does not delete the circle

The paper reports that if every co-occurrence between two months is removed, the circle still forms at moderate embedding dimension. Its explanation is that many other words are tied to the season (ski, beach, hurricane), so the time of year acts as a hidden variable that shapes the statistics of the whole vocabulary. The months get placed by their relations to those words.

Here is a toy of that mechanism, not the paper's experiment. Take the 12 months at phases 2πm/12, plus S seasonal words at random phases, plus 20 unrelated words. The association between two time-linked words is the product of their strengths times the cosine of their phase difference. Add a little noise. Then zero every month-to-month entry, keep the top 6 eigen-directions, center, and ask how well the top two principal components put the months in calendar order. The score is 1 for a perfect circle in order and near 0 for scatter computed; seed 7.

4 · What the paper does not claim

Scope

The theory is derived for word-embedding algorithms. Agreement with large language models is empirical, and the paper’s limitations paragraph notes that language models can adapt to context. (In my copy of the PDF that sentence breaks off at the end of a page, so I cannot say what the authors conclude from it.) The circle persists after removing month–month co-occurrences at intermediate embedding dimension (about 103 for a vocabulary of about 2.5×104 in its Figure 4), not at every size. The paper makes no claim about theories of linguistic competence, and it does not show that large models build no internal world model. It shows that this particular geometry has a simpler statistical origin.

Not done here

I have not run any of this on a real corpus. I have not reproduced the paper’s experiments or re-derived its proofs; I read the abstract, introduction, the Figure 4 caption, the limitations paragraph and one appendix derivation. I have not tested any language model. There is no Lean file for this chapter. The notes on word2vec training and on the Mikolov et al. analogy results, which this slot of the book is meant to cover, are still unwritten.

Open question open: do cyclic and ordered word classes in languages other than English, such as Portuguese, Nepali or Tibetan, show the same translation symmetry in their co-occurrence statistics? The paper tests English. A design for the check is noted in Book XXV.
