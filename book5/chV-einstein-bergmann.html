Einstein Returns, 1938–1943

Letting the fifth direction wiggle: a small circle with room for matter

Einstein did not leave the Kaluza idea alone for good. After about five years away, he and Peter Bergmann returned to it in 1938, and he kept working on it until 1943.

What he wanted

Einstein's lifelong target was a pure field theory in which particles are not extra ingredients but smooth lumps of the field, solutions without singularities. In their paper he and Bergmann note that many attempts to build such a particle in Kaluza's theory had failed, and that they were convinced no such solution exists in the vacuum version.

The change

They relaxed the cylinder condition. Fields could now depend on the fifth coordinate, but only periodically, on a circle of small circumference b. Their own picture is a strip curved into a tube. If b is small compared with how quickly things change along the other directions, then a function on the tube looks almost the same all around the circle and can be treated as a function of the ordinary coordinates alone. That is the answer they gave to why we see four dimensions.

Allowing the fifth-direction wiggle brought matter back in. A field that varies around the circle carries momentum in the fifth direction, which is charge, so charged matter could arise from the geometry itself, which is what Einstein had wanted. They broke the field into Fourier modes around the circle and assumed, without proving it, that the higher modes are small compared with the constant one.

What they could not do

Klein had fixed the size of the circle by quantizing, using Planck's constant and the de Broglie relation. Einstein and Bergmann worked classically, with no ħ, and so could not compute b from the electron's charge. The scale of the extra dimension stayed a free parameter. They also did not find the non-singular particle they sought. Van Dongen discusses how Einstein hoped a classical field theory could eventually account for quantum behaviour rather than start from it.

[STATUS]Everything on this page about the 1938–43 work is taken from van Dongen's paper (§4). I have not read the original Einstein–Bergmann paper (Ann. Math. 39, 1938); a reader who quotes it should read it first.

For a general reader the lesson is not that Einstein was wrong. A small compact direction is what many later higher-dimensional theories use. He reached it from a different starting point than Klein, and without the quantum step that makes its size computable.
