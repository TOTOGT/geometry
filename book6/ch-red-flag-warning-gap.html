The Red Flag Gap

When the air is red and the warning is not: a school-level reading of the 16 November 2026 North Charleston record

Pablo Nogueira Grossi · G6 LLC, Newark NJ · 2026

Mechanism: WP39 (Smoke as a Planetary Operator)

Abstract

On the morning of 16 November 2026 the AirNow NowCast for the North Charleston, South Carolina monitor climbed from the Good range into the red (Unhealthy) range overnight, while aggregator summaries read in the same session reported Good air and no alerts. This chapter does three things. It states what a school flag means. It gives the one piece of mechanism we can support, that surface concentration scales as 1/h with mixing depth h, which is why a cold, calm night can turn red without a smoky day before it. And it sets out a rule for raising a red flag and a log that would show whether flags are raised and whether they change decisions. It does not send alerts, it is not an official warning, and it does not show that any agency failed.

[DATA] One AirNow screenshot supplied by the author, a pasted EPA AQI table, one 2009 news report. [MODEL] C = Q/h and the flag rule. [OPEN] Whether an alert was issued; the cause of the overnight rise; anything about New York or New Jersey schools.

1. What a flag is

The AQI scale used by AirNow runs from 0 to 500 in six bands: Good 0–50 (green), Moderate 51–100 (yellow), Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups 101–150 (orange), Unhealthy 151–200 (red), Very Unhealthy 201–300 (purple), Hazardous 301–500 (maroon). The AirNow advisory text for red is that children, older adults, pregnant people and people with respiratory disease such as asthma should avoid physical activity outdoors, and everyone else should limit prolonged physical activity outdoors. [DATA] Source: the AirNow table supplied by the author on 2026-11-16; the table itself was read from a screenshot.

The EPA School Flag Program uses flag colors that follow the local air-quality forecast category. [DATA] This rests on a 2012 EPA fact sheet read only as a search summary; the current program pages were not retrieved. A flag is a posted signal at the school, not an alert sent to a person.

2. Why the same air can turn red overnight: C = Q/h

WP39 proves, in a three-layer box model, that at fixed column burden Q the surface concentration is C = Q/h. A layer that shrinks from 600 m to 50 m raises surface concentration twelvefold with no change in how much material is above the city. [MODEL] The arithmetic is checked by book6/ch-red-flag-verify.py; the identity itself is a definition of dilution, not a finding about any episode.

On a clear, calm night the ground cools and the lowest air becomes a shallow, stable layer, and fog forms when that air reaches its dew point. These are the textbook conditions for surface build-up. [MODEL] General atmospheric reasoning, not checked against any Charleston sounding. The overnight rise in the screenshot is consistent with this pattern. [OPEN] It is not shown to be caused by it: no mixing-depth, wind or dew-point data for North Charleston were obtained.

The WP39 mixed-layer figures (565 m to 53 m and the correlations) did not reproduce; see the correction box in section 2 of that paper. Nothing here depends on them.

3. The 16 November 2026 record

The AirNow chart for the North Charleston monitor, data updated Monday 16 November 2026 at 05:00 EST, shows hourly NowCast AQI green through the afternoon, yellow into the evening, orange near 03:00 and red from about 04:00, with the last bars at roughly 155 to 165. The pollutant table beside it lists PM2.5 at AQI 157 with a concentration of 57.2 µg/m³ and PM10 at AQI 68 with 93 µg/m³. [DATA] Read from one screenshot.

The AQI beside 57.2 µg/m³ is 157. Applying the EPA PM2.5 breakpoints as I recall them after the 2024 revision gives 152, a difference of 5; both lie in the red band. The likely reason is that the table pairs a NowCast-weighted AQI with a raw concentration, which is not verified, and the breakpoints were written from memory and not fetched. [DATA] Checked in ch-red-flag-verify.py.

Aggregator pages read in the same session gave US AQI of about 24 to 34 for North Charleston and listed no active alerts. The two readings cannot both describe the same hour at that monitor. Possible reasons include a stale feed, a different sensor, and a different averaging window. [OPEN] Which is right was not resolved, and whether any official alert was issued was not established: the alert sources were blocked or returned titles only.

4. The institutional gap

A 2009 Post and Courier report records the Charleston County school district considering air testing at five North Charleston schools after a USA Today analysis, built from EPA data and simulations rather than measurements, ranked the air around several of them among the worst in the country. District officials said they were unsure what they could do with results because regulating air pollution was the state agency's responsibility, and community groups wanted the agency to lead. No test results were reported. [DATA] The pollutants discussed there were industrial metals, a different problem from the PM2.5 reading above; the reports of staff cancers and children's asthma in that article were statements at a news conference, not findings.

The author's own contact with the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection (10 September 2026) found that the state does not track whether schools raise the air-quality flag. [DATA] From the author's account; not independently verified here. Together these describe one shape of gap: a signal exists, and nobody records which signal a school used or whether it changed the decision.

5. A red-flag rule, and what it does not do

Rule. Raise the red flag when the nearest regulatory monitor's NowCast AQI, or the agency forecast for the day, is 151 or higher. Then record, for each day: date, time, source and value read, flag raised, and the outdoor activity decision made. [MODEL] A proposal in the EPA flag convention, not EPA guidance.

It does not send anything to anyone, replace an agency alert, or decide which monitor represents a school. Two feeds can disagree, as in section 3, so the log records the source, and a school should prefer the regulatory reading over an aggregator summary when they differ. [OPEN] Whether the rule changes decisions is exactly what the log is meant to measure.

6. What would make this evidence

(a) The AirNow archive for the monitor, to time the gap between the AQI first crossing 101 and the first alert on any channel. (b) Hourly temperature, dew point and wind from the nearest weather station for the same night. (c) A mixing-depth estimate where a sounding or lidar profile exists. (d) The same record for a New Jersey monitor such as Carteret, which was not read. Until then the chapter claims a disagreement between sources and an unmeasured gap, not a failure.

Limitations and Status
