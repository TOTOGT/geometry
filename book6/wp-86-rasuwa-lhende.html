The Rasuwa Interval

Two floods, one stream, thirteen months apart. The triggers differed. The failure mode did not. This work package is about the gap between them.

Status — open, and the event is live.
Search and recovery on the Lhende and Trishuli was still under way when this page was opened on 29 August 2026. Casualty figures are moving daily and the sources disagree. This page carries dated, attributed ranges and no total of its own. Any single number printed here would be the most confident thing on the page and the least supported.

The record

8 July 2025

The Lhende stream flooded in Rasuwa district, Nepal. The cause was heavy rainfall on the Chinese side of the border. The flood swept away the Miteripul bridge connecting Nepal and China, damaged the Rasuwagadhi hydropower dam, and left fifteen people missing.

26–27 August 2026

The Lhende flooded again. A glacier collapsed at roughly 5,200 m on the Nepal–Tibet border; the rock-ice avalanche entered the river about 20 km northeast of the crossing, dammed it, and the dam failed. The Trishuli rose about nine metres in thirty minutes. Rasuwa was worst hit, then Nuwakot and Dhading; on the Tibetan side, Gyirong Port. At least nineteen bridges, some forty kilometres of road and two hydroelectric schemes were destroyed.

Same stream. Same district. Same border. Thirteen months apart. The trigger differed — rainfall in 2025, a glacier in 2026 — but the failure mode did not: something originates on the Chinese side, the Lhende carries it, and the crossing and the hydropower go.

The claim

A flood that raises a river nine metres in thirty minutes admits no evacuation order, no engineering and no decision. Everything that could matter had to exist beforehand. The event is not the failure — it is a fact about that valley and it will happen there again. The failure is the unused interval before it, and the interval is the only term anyone controls. On the Lhende that term was measurable: thirteen months.

What the interval was for

Between the two floods there were thirteen months in which a standing Nepal–China mountain-hazard mechanism could have been built:

  • contact points on both sides of the border, staffed around the clock;
  • agreed alert thresholds, written down in advance rather than negotiated during an event;
  • rapid satellite sharing scoped to river blockage, abnormal flow, glacial-lake instability and ice-rock avalanche — not weather alone, which is what the 2025 event trained everyone to watch for and is precisely what the 2026 event was not.

In August 2026 no such mechanism existed. Information about impounded water upstream reached Nepal’s disaster authority after the flood was already downstream.

What this work package does not claim

No operator chain, no contact geometry, no dm³. Nothing here is offered as an instance of anything else in this series, and the argument is stronger without it: two events on one stream, a shared failure mode, a measurable interval, and a specification for what that interval is for. That claim stands on the record alone and can be checked against it by anyone.

Open items

  • Establish whether any cross-border mechanism was proposed after July 2025, by whom, and what became of it. If one was proposed and not built, that is a different and sharper finding than one that was never proposed.
