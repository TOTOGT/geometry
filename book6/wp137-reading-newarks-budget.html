Reading Newark's Budget

One-time money in a $900 million general budget. A close reading of the City of Newark's 2025 and 2026 State-form budgets: which columns foot, which do not, how much of the general budget the city itself flags as non-recurring, and what it would cost an average home to replace it. Arithmetic, not an audit.

Principia Orthogona · Book VI · WP137 · 2026

Status. Independent working paper, not peer-reviewed, and not an audit, a rating or a finding that Newark is in fiscal stress. Every figure comes from the text of two State of New Jersey Municipal User Friendly Budget PDFs (named in Sources), copied by hand into wp137-verify.py, which was run before these sentences were written. An earlier read of the same PDFs through a text-summary tool scrambled several lines; nothing here relies on that read. What is computed is labelled computed; what the sheets print and this page cannot explain is labelled unexplained.

§1 · What the form is

New Jersey municipalities file a standard workbook, the Municipal User Friendly Budget (UFB), with the Division of Local Government Services. It is the city's own summary of its budget: revenue and appropriations by fund, the tax levy and rate, personnel, debt, and a list of revenues "that may not be available in subsequent years". Newark's 2026 file does not say whether it is the introduced or the adopted budget. The 2025 file is named INTRODUCED BUDGET, so its figures may differ from what the city finally adopted; the 2026 file's prior-year columns are the better guide to 2025 outcomes where the two disagree.

The form does not contain the one number a runway estimate needs: year-end fund balance. That sits in the audit; §5 uses the 2021 audit and the 2023 budget's surplus sheet, the only ones provided, and nothing later.

The balance is refilled each year: about $27.8 million of operating excess in 2021 (audited) and an implied $49.5 million added in 2022. That changes how the §4 draws should be read: a draw is spending from a balance the previous year's results had restocked. Going further is inference, not reading, because no balance sheet after 2022 was provided. The draws are $25.0M (2022), $60.0M (2023), $43.0M (2024, read from the 2025 file's prior-year column; the 2024 budget itself was not read), $23.85M (2025) and $4.106M (2026): $155,956,000 over 2022 to 2026. Against the $53,375,084.83 available at the end of 2021, at least $102,580,915.17 had to be added to the balance during 2022 to 2025 for those draws to be fundable, if each was paid from the balance. To fund the 2024 draw alone, at least $25,140,057.77 had to be added during 2023. These are lower bounds from the printed draws, not estimates of what was added.

The 2026 introduced legal-line budget also has an appendix with a 31 December 2025 balance sheet; the scan's figures were not legible by OCR, so no end-2025 balance is read here.

Context from an earlier audit, which is a different kind of audit and not a balance review. The State Comptroller's December 2013 report on Newark's payroll, timekeeping and operating practices (covering January 2010 to September 2013, a performance audit under the Comptroller's own statute) states in its background section that Newark "historically has been a financially distressed city." It also records special State aid of $32 million in 2011 and $10 million in 2012 (the City had asked for $24 million), none in 2013, and a Memorandum of Understanding with the Department of Community Affairs that put a Fiscal Monitor over hiring and budget practices. This paper quotes that history; it does not extend it to 2026, and nothing in the 2025 and 2026 files read here says whether a monitor or special aid is in place today.

§7 · What replacing it from the levy would cost

This is arithmetic at constant valuation, not a forecast. The city could cut spending, sell assets again, or get state aid; the table prices one option so the scale is visible. Each $1,000,000 of levy, grossed up by the 96.24% collection rate the sheet uses for the reserve, costs the average residential assessment of $191,563 $15.57 a year (computed).

§8 · A toy simulation, and what it is for

Treat each of the ten flagged general-side lines as repeating independently with probability p, and price whatever does not repeat from the levy. With a fixed seed and 20,000 draws, at p = 0.25, 0.50, 0.75 the mean added cost per average home is about $1,112, $742 and $371, with 5th-to-95th percentile ranges of $561 to $1,486, $138 to $1,340 and $0 to $925. The mean was already given by the arithmetic in §7; the simulation adds only a spread, and the spread is mostly the question of whether the four asset sales happen. The value of p is an assumption, not data, and the model contains nothing about Newark beyond ten printed lines. It is included to show the method, not to predict anything.

§9 · Debt arithmetic

The debt sheet foots. Gross debt $759,701,874.59 less deductions $500,565,790.65 is net debt $259,136,083.94, which is 0.96% of the three-year average valuation of $26,999,413,197.67. Gross debt per head is $2,438.47 on the 2020 census population of 311,549. Current-year debt service is $43,995,319 principal plus $18,666,700 interest, $62,662,019, which is 5.65% of the $1,108,746,903.34 all-funds budget (all computed). The ratings printed on the sheet are Baa1 (year of last rating 2024) and AA- (year of last rating 2017); the sheet's layout puts them under Moody's and Standard & Poor's respectively.

§10 · What would test it

First, the adopted 2026 budget and the 2025 adopted budget, to see which of the §3 differences survive. Second, the audits for 2022 to 2025, so the surplus draw can be set against balances that are read, not implied. Third, a mapping from each UFB-4 line to its revenue line on UFB-2, so the flagged share is a share of identified lines. Fourth, other New Jersey cities' forms, so Newark's 10.60% has a comparison. This paper uses no state stress score and no peer group; none was used here.

§12 · One public body in between: the Parking Authority

The 2019 lease-back reported by the press (a $27 million, 30-year lease of space the city had sold for $1 in 2017) raises a structural question: why would a city route a transaction through an authority? This section reads what the Authority’s own audit says, and stops where the documents stop. It does not read the Authority’s finances into the city’s numbers, and it does not say why the city chose this structure; no document read here states a reason.

What follows is reading, not a finding. The Authority is a public body the city created, and the city appoints its board. Its 2023 bonds are secured by the city’s rent. So the debt is the Authority’s, and the city’s payment toward it appears in the city’s budget as rent. Whether the city also guaranteed any Authority bonds, which would change how the debt counts under New Jersey law, is not stated in the audit and is not known here. The Parking Authority Law says authority bonds are generally not debts of the municipality unless the municipality guarantees them by ordinance. The 2017 sale ordinance (file 17-1221, adopted 12 July 2017) was read only as a council record page, without its text, so the sale price is still the press’s figure.

Two cautions. The Authority’s board approved this audit in 2025 and its subsequent-events note runs through 23 July 2025, about nineteen months after year-end; the audit itself says the 150-day covenant was missed. And the Authority’s income statement shows a “City of Newark lease” revenue line of $479,061 for 2023, which this paper has not reconciled to the monthly rent in Note 7. Neither point is read as a conclusion about the city.
