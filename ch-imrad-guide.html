Writing a Scientific Article

IMRaD, one section at a time

A study of one published guide for beginners: what each section of a research paper has to do, which tense to use where, how to build an abstract and a title, and when to cite. Written in plain language for readers of English as a second language, with four exercises.

Before you start

What this chapter is, and is not

This chapter retells the advice of one paper: Ecarnot, Seronde, Chopard, Schiele and Meneveau, “Writing a scientific article: A step-by-step guide for beginners”, European Geriatric Medicine, 2015 (doi 10.1016/j.eurger.2015.08.005). The wording here is the author’s own, not the guide’s, and the guide should be read in full for the original.

Where the advice comes fromThe guide is written by physicians for early-career clinical researchers, and its examples come from cardiology and medicine. Two parts of it, such as ethics-committee approval and trial registration, apply to human-subject studies and may not apply to your field. The structure and the writing advice are not specific to medicine in the guide’s own account, but this chapter has not tested them in other fields.

It does not show that following the advice gets a paper accepted. The guide does not claim that either. It offers what experienced authors recommend.

Part 1

The map

Most journals use the IMRaD order: Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion. The guide adds that you should always check the target journal’s instructions for authors, because some use another format. Around the four sections sit the title, the abstract, the reference list, the tables and the figure legends, and optional parts such as acknowledgements.

PartIts one job
IntroductionSay why the study exists: what is known, what is not, and what this study will add.
MethodsSay exactly what you did, so a reader with the same resources could repeat it.
ResultsSay what you observed, without comment.
DiscussionSay what it means, how it fits earlier work, and what it cannot show.
AbstractStand alone as a short summary of the four sections.
TitleCarry the key terms, so the right readers can find the paper.

Before writing a word, the guide suggests choosing your target journal, because it decides the style, the length and who your readers are. It also suggests collecting your notes and references as you read, so you do not have to search for them later.

Where it fits: this is a reading to do before the course begins, so the vocabulary of a research paper is familiar when you start. Weeks 9 to 11 of the course return to the same material when you write a full paper. That placement is the course’s, not the guide’s.
Part 2

The Introduction: from what is known to what you will do

The guide describes a funnel with five steps. First, a short account of what is already known, with references. Second, narrow down to what is still uncertain, citing any earlier or conflicting data. Third, name the specific gap in knowledge your study fills. Fourth, state your hypothesis and your objective. Fifth, say very briefly how you will pursue it.

Select the most relevant references and do not cite everything. Do not state facts so obvious that a reader in your field would find them simple. Keep every sentence useful. The guide says that, without a journal limit, about one to one and a half pages is a reasonable length.

State the objective once, in exact terms: the measure you will assess and how. Then use the same wording in the introduction, results, discussion, abstract and, in part, the title. The guide says repeating yourself here is not a fault, because it shows the reader that you command your subject and keeps your terms from drifting.

Part 3

Which tense? A table for each job

This is the part of the guide that matters most for readers of English as a second language. The guide recommends the following choices.

What you are writingTense
the current state of knowledgepresent
what other authors observedpast
a process begun earlier and not yet finishedpresent perfect
something that has not been settled yetpresent perfect
your hypothesispast for the first verb, present for the second
your objectivepast
your methodspast; past perfect for events before your study
your resultspast

If English is not your first language, the guide suggests using any translation or writing help you can reach, and studying how papers in good journals phrase these sections.

Exercise 1 · Choose the tense

The sentences below are invented practice sentences about an imaginary study, not findings. Fill in each blank with the verb in the right tense for its job.

  1. Planned forest burns (be) ________ known events, since their dates are set in advance. (current knowledge)
  2. Earlier studies (measure) ________ canopy height after fires. (what others observed)
  3. It (not be) ________ determined yet whether a satellite lidar can see the change. (not yet settled)
  4. We (hypothesize) ________ that burned plots (lose) ________ canopy height. (hypothesis)
  5. We (aim) ________ to compare canopy height before and after each burn. (objective)

Check: 1 are (present). 2 measured (past). 3 has not been (present perfect). 4 hypothesized (past) and lose (present). 5 aimed (past).

Part 4

Methods and Results

Methods. Describe exactly what you did. Every result in the results section needs a method in the methods section, and you cannot report a test you never described. If a procedure was published before, a short summary and a reference are enough. Begin with the study design and the population or material studied, then the measures, then, in a last paragraph, the statistics. The guide recommends a separate paragraph that says how data will be presented, which tests were used for which variables, and which software and significance level. If you plan sub-group analyses, say so there, so they are not later questioned as after-the-fact choices. Subtitles help the reader.

The guide also mentions human-subject details: a short statement of ethics approval and consent, and the registration of randomized trials. These depend on your field.

Results. State what you observed and nothing else. Do not describe the method again, and avoid comment words such as “surprisingly” here. Give a result for every method, in the same order as in the methods, and use the same subtitles. As a rough rule from the guide, a result that fits in one or two lines can go in the text. Tables suit the same variables across two or more groups, and figures suit trends and relationships that are hard to read as numbers. Do not repeat in the text what a table or figure already shows.

Exercise 2 · Strip the comment

Rewrite this invented sentence for a results section: “Interestingly, canopy height fell by a surprisingly large amount in the burned plots.” Keep the observation and remove the comment. One answer: “Canopy height fell in the burned plots (Table 2).” The interpretation belongs in the discussion.

Part 5

The Discussion

Begin with a short recap of the main finding, in the same words as the objective in the introduction. Then interpret, without repeating the results and without over-interpreting them. The guide gives a clear warning: if 20 of 25 patients bled after a drug, you may not write that 80 per cent of patients given the drug bleed, because that turns a count into a general claim. Say instead that your results indicate the drug may have serious adverse effects.

Compare your results with other reports. If they differ, offer a plausible reason, such as a different population or method. Say whether your findings can be applied to other settings, and if not, why not. Say what is new and whether the gap you named in the introduction was filled. The guide says negative results are also worth reporting if the study was well made.

On criticizing others, the guide recommends tact. Instead of saying that another study was underpowered, say that it may have been, or better, state the strength of your own study. A short paragraph on the strengths and limits of your work lets you show you know its weak points and answer them first.

Exercise 3 · Soften the criticism

Rewrite this invented sentence in a more careful tone: “Smith’s sample was too small to mean anything.” Two answers: “Smith’s sample may have been too small to detect the effect.” Or: “Our sample was large enough to detect an effect of this size.”

Part 6

The abstract, the title and the references

Abstract. It must make sense on its own. The guide asks for four short parts: background with the main objective, methods in the briefest form, results with the primary result first, and a one-line conclusion tied to the main objective. It asks for no references, no figures or tables, and no judging remarks in the abstract. Check that every method you mention has a result.

Title. Put the key terms in it, because readers find papers through searches. Name the main factor studied, the setting, the design and the main finding, with the most important element first. Skip vague openings, for example “A report of” or “The effects of”; name the effect itself. Use subtitles sparingly and check the journal’s length limit.

References. Cite any idea or fact that comes from someone else, except plain, widely known facts. Cite the original source of an idea, not a paper that repeats it, and check every reference yourself, even ones copied from other papers. The guide prefers peer-reviewed articles in English, original research over reviews where possible, and advises avoiding web pages, personal communications and unpublished data. Follow the journal’s reference style and consider reference-manager software.

Exercise 4 · One objective, four places

Write one sentence stating the objective of a study you could do. Then place the same wording in a draft title, in the last sentence of an introduction, in the first sentence of a discussion and in the conclusion line of an abstract. Check that the same measure and the same words appear each time.

Limits

What this chapter does not show

  • That the advice works. The guide recommends practices and does not report a test of them.
  • That it fits every field. It was written by physicians for clinical research, and its examples are medical.
  • The journals’ own rules. The guide says to follow each journal’s instructions for authors, and this chapter did not read any.
  • The guide itself in full. This is a retelling in other words. The original tables and its figure of section guidelines are not reproduced.
