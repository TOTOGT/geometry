Professional English for physicians: pediatric oncology pathways and the hardest conversations

Output 2, revised · advanced (about B2 to C1) · for physicians who already hold a medical degree, some with specialty training · two sessions of 90 minutes · all cases and letters fictional · compared with ophthalmology throughout

Before you teach this. The second session is about telling parents that their child may die. It is a language session, not therapy and not a clinical protocol. Any student may step out, pass, or watch without speaking, at any point and without explaining. Use invented cases only, no real names, no statistics. Keep ten minutes at the end for a quiet debrief, and have students write one source of support available to them at their own institution. Teachers are not counsellors; refer on.
Rule for every task: never write that you are licensed, certified or qualified to practise somewhere unless that is true. Use [placeholders] for facts you do not know.

Session 1 · Part 1. Reading the pathway: oncology compared with ophthalmology (30 min)

Ophthalmology is a specialty entered directly after medical school in the US (ACGME, 2026: 48 months integrated, or a preliminary year plus 36 months, from a machine summary). Pediatric oncology is different: it is a subspecialty built on top of a core pediatrics training. There is no direct route from medical school to oncology. Compare with ophthalmology. In both specialties a foreign doctor needs recognised training, a licence, and a place. Ophthalmology's difficulty is the number of places (18 of 109 "International Grads" registered matched in 2026) and procedural privileging. Pediatric oncology adds a layer: a pediatrics residency first, a smaller subspecialty, and daily conversations about life-limiting illness. Adult medical oncology (a different route) and the Brazilian and German recognition of foreign oncology training: OPEN.

Questions. (1) Why can a physician not move "straight to oncology"? (2) Find the sentence that proves a fellowship is not the same as permission to practise. Session 1 · Part 3. A credential inquiry for a subspecialty (30 min)

Fictional: Dr. [Name] completed medical school and a [number]-year pediatrics residency in [country]. Write 120 to 160 words to a state board or program office asking how foreign pediatrics training would be assessed before a hematology-oncology fellowship, and what licence the ABP needs for certification. Session 2 · Part 4. How the profession teaches difficult news (20 min)

Status: language exercises, not a clinical protocol. SPIKES: original paper read (body and six steps; end of the reference list not read). ASCO 2017 guideline: original read, pages 1 to 13 of 17 (the remaining pages are references; the online data supplement and appendix were not available). The ASCO guideline is written for clinicians who care for adults with cancer, so neither source is a pediatric disclosure protocol. The phrases below are illustrative English, not validated scripts, and nothing here tells a student what to say to a real family, to a child, or to an adolescent.

SPIKES (Baile, Buckman and colleagues, The Oncologist, 2000) VERIFIED

The paper presents six tasks for giving unfavourable news to people with cancer: setting up the interview; assessing the patient's perception; obtaining the patient's invitation; giving knowledge and information; addressing emotions with empathic responses; and strategy and summary. Its own advice includes asking before telling, warning that bad news is coming, giving information in small pieces and checking understanding, and naming the emotion you see. The authors also say not every encounter needs all six steps.

What the paper does not establish. It says the protocol is "not completely derived from empirical data", and that whether patients find it useful is an open question. The support it cites is a 1998 ASCO symposium survey and workshop questionnaires about physician confidence, not patient outcomes. In the text I read, it does not discuss children or paediatrics, and it does not discuss interpreters; its only mention of age is that bad news is "particularly stressful" when the patient is young. Involving family is described as the patient's choice, which is adult-patient framing.

ASCO communication guideline (Gilligan and colleagues, JCO, 2017) VERIFIED

Scope. The guideline's target population is "clinicians who care for adults with cancer", and its literature review was limited to communication between clinicians and adults with cancer. A search of the full text found no discussion of children or adolescents. Pediatric oncology is therefore outside its stated scope.

Evidence status. The systematic review found 47 publications. The authors say evidence was limited for most questions, so nearly all recommendations are formal consensus (strength: strong), reached by a modified Delphi process with a 75% agreement threshold. Only the three recommendations on clinician communication-skills training (9.1 to 9.3) are rated evidence based, with intermediate quality of evidence. The authors also say it is unclear how far studies from other patient populations generalise to oncology, and unclear which communication behaviours lead to better outcomes. The disclaimer says the guideline does not mandate a standard of care.

What it recommends on the topics this activity touches (adult patients). Goals of care and prognosis (2.1 to 2.4): tailor information, reassess goals when care changes significantly, use simple direct terms, and take extra steps with bad news. Interpreters (6.1): use a medical interpreter rather than a family interpreter. End-of-life care (4.1 to 4.5): use an organised framework (it lists SPIKES among published frameworks), and recognise and respond empathetically to grief and loss among patients, families, and clinicians themselves. Training (9.1 to 9.3): practice with role-play and feedback; lectures alone are not adequate. Its stated purpose includes clinician well-being.

Pediatric applicability remains a separate, unresolved question. An adult-oncology guideline, now verified, is not a rule for what parents must tell a child, how adolescent confidentiality works, or when a child should receive prognostic information. Those need pediatric-specific evidence and the institution's own policy. Ask students what their own hospital's guidance says.

Session 2 · Part 5. The hard questions (discussion and role-play, 45 min)

Choose one scenario per group. All are fictional. In each, name the sentence that is hardest to say in English, then say it aloud in three versions: too blunt, too vague, clear and kind.

  1. A mother asks, before the results are in: "Is my daughter going to die?" What can you honestly say?
  2. A father asks you not to tell his son the diagnosis. The boy then asks you directly what is wrong.
  3. The family wants to continue treatment that you believe is no longer helping. How do you talk about goals of care?
  4. A fifteen-year-old asks you to tell her the truth and not to tell her parents yet.
  5. The only available interpreter is the patient's uncle.
  6. You must tell your team that a child they cared for has died, and then talk to the parents again the next morning in your second language.
  7. A parent asks: "Did we wait too long?" You do not know. What do you say?

Same news, four listeners

Take one fictional message, for example "The treatment has not worked, and we need to talk about what comes next." Say it four ways, then compare what changed: to a parent, to an adolescent patient, to another clinician in a handover, and through an interpreter. Record for each: vocabulary, sentence length, what is left out, what is checked back, and who you look at. Discuss: which changes are linguistic, and which would be clinical or policy decisions that belong to the institution? Leave that second question open.

Observer checklist (language only). Did the speaker use a warning phrase once, then stop? Short sentences? Plain words ("died", not only "passed")? Space for silence? A question about what the family understands before information? A clear next step? Nobody grades emotion; grade clarity.

Session 2 · Part 5A. Teacher-led: how policies are worded (15 min)

A language exercise about interpreting professional and regulatory English. It does not evaluate medical practice, and the class does not decide which policy is better or correct.

Two safeguards. (1) Participation must not require access to internal hospital policies. Use publicly available policies, or the fictional teaching extracts below. (2) No confidential information: no patient details, no internal documents, and no identifiable clinical cases are brought into class.

Fictional teaching extracts. Invented wording on a non-clinical topic, written only to compare modal verbs. They are not real policies and not models for practice.

Extract A. "Staff must submit a leave request at least 14 days before the planned date. The department head may approve shorter notice in exceptional circumstances. Staff should keep a copy of the confirmation."

Extract B. "Employees are expected to request leave in advance. Where possible, requests should be submitted two weeks ahead. Shorter notice can be considered by the manager at their discretion."

  1. Underline every modal verb and expression of expectation. Sort them into obligation, recommendation and permission.
  2. For each sentence, who is responsible: the employee or the manager?
  3. Which extract leaves more room for discretion? Point to the words that show it.
  4. Optional: students who wish to may bring a short extract from a publicly available policy of their own institution (any topic), and compare it in pairs by the same four steps.

Closing language question. "How would you explain this policy accurately in English to a colleague trained in another country?" Each student says it aloud in two or three sentences without adding anything the text does not say.

Session 2 · Part 6. Writing (15 min)

Write a short follow-up message (60 to 90 words) that a clinical team could send after a difficult family meeting, for example through a patient portal or as a call summary. Fictional case. It states, in plain English: what was discussed in a sentence, the next step, who the family can contact and when, and that questions are welcome. No cliches, no promises, no medical detail beyond what was said in the meeting. Then write one sentence you would never put in such a message, and say why.

Optional discussion. Whether a hospital or clinician sends anything after a child's death varies by institution and country, and I have not verified how common it is. Ask students what their own hospitals do and whether anyone is expected or allowed to write. Do not assume a practice exists.

Session 2 · Part 7. Debrief and care (10 min)

Ask: which sentence was hardest, and why? What would help you prepare? ACGME's training text says programs must promote well-being, and ASCO's adult-oncology guideline names clinician well-being among its purposes and recommends recognising grief and loss among clinicians themselves (recommendation 4.4). Each student writes one named source of support at their own workplace or school. If a student is distressed, stop the activity and offer to talk privately; do not press anyone to continue.

Teacher notes and key

Part 1. (1) Pediatric oncology is a subspecialty that follows a core pediatrics residency; the ACGME text says the fellowship is part of a core pediatric program. (2) The ABP page says temporary or training licences are not accepted and a non-US licence no longer counts for certification. (3) Rows marked OPEN: ask the ABP, the state chamber (Germany), the Brazilian Pediatric Society and the CFM. (4) Model: "In the US you complete pediatrics first, then about 36 months of fellowship, and you still need an unrestricted US licence for board certification."

Part 5A. Extract A: "must" is an obligation on staff; "may approve" is the department head's permission; "should keep" is a recommendation. Extract B: "are expected to" and "where possible ... should" are softer expectations; "can be considered ... at their discretion" leaves the decision to the manager. B leaves more room for discretion. A model explanation to a colleague: "In A, you have to give 14 days' notice, but the head can allow less. In B, two weeks is the aim, and the manager decides about shorter notice." Differences between real policies are recorded as differences, not ranked.

Part 3 model features. Names the requesting role, gives placeholders for unknown numbers, asks two or three numbered questions (which body assesses foreign pediatrics training; what the ABP counts), lists enclosures, closes by asking for the rule.

Part 5 notes. Q1: honesty without false certainty ("I do not know yet, and I will tell you as soon as I do"). Q2: parents and clinicians share responsibility to the child, so explore why before refusing; discuss assent as a concept, not as a legal answer. Q5: ASCO's adult guideline recommends a medical interpreter rather than a family interpreter (6.1, consensus). Pediatric and institutional policy remain OPEN. Q7: avoid blame; "Nothing you did caused this" is a medical claim, so say it only if it is true for the case. There is no single correct script.

Before classroom use. (1) Decide, with each student's institution, which pediatric disclosure, adolescent confidentiality, interpreter and documentation policies apply; neither SPIKES nor the ASCO guideline supplies them. (2) Present Parts 4 and 5 as language practice; no model answer is validated. (3) Optional: read ASCO's online Data Supplement for the evidence tables. Accuracy reminders. Nothing here is clinical or legal advice. The pathway rows use only what the sources marked above say.
