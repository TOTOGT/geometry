Heating, cooling and mechanical services: what the numbers look like, and the five that drive the plan.

What this is built from

The framework below (three operating engines, five numbers) came from an AI-generated overview supplied by the author. That overview cited a job posting, an advisory firm’s marketing page, two videos and a LinkedIn profile; none was read for this page, and the advisory page could not be fetched. So the framework is given as definitions that a reader can check by arithmetic, not as findings. The only facts about the industry on this page come from two sources that were read: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and a trade survey reported by ACHR News. Both were read through a fetch tool’s summary of the page and are not independently checked. Claims in the overview about private-equity multiples, quality of earnings and software were left out because nothing read supports them.

The three engines

A framework for splitting an HVAC firm’s plan, from the overview described above. It is a way to organize the model, not a finding.

The five numbers

These are definitions. Each can be checked by hand, and the page’s code is tested against hand-worked cases.

  1. Revenue per truck-day = daily service revenue divided by trucks on the road.
  2. Labor burden per billable hour = (wages + payroll taxes and benefits + truck insurance + fuel, for the same period) divided by billable hours in that period.
  3. Quoted-vs-actual margin bridge = quoted gross margin in dollars, minus the extra material cost, minus the extra labor hours priced at the labor rate, equals actual gross margin in dollars. One labor rate is assumed, so a change in the rate itself is not separated out.
  4. Agreement churn = agreements lost divided by agreements at the start. Attach rate = agreements sold divided by eligible visits.
  5. Cash floor = fixed cost of a slow month divided by the monthly contribution of one agreement, where the contribution is the annual fee times (1 minus the cost to serve), divided by 12. It is the number of agreements at which recurring contribution covers that month’s fixed cost; it says nothing about how many a given firm has.

Seasonality

The BLS says peak heating and cooling seasons bring overtime and irregular schedules, and the trade survey says 19% of respondents adjust pricing by season. Beyond that, how deep the spring and autumn dip runs, and how much a maintenance base cushions it, is not in either source. The cash floor above is a break-even identity, so the reader supplies the firm’s own costs and fees.

What this does not do

  • It does not forecast or value any real HVAC firm, and the worked example is invented arithmetic.
  • It gives no gross-margin or churn benchmark, because the sources read report none.
  • It does not claim that investors favor HVAC businesses, or recommend software; the overview made both claims with no source read.
  • It does not show that the five numbers are the right ones for every firm. They are a starting definition.
