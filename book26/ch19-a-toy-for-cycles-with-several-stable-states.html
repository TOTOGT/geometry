A Toy for Cycles With Several Stable States

A work sample: a two-node model in which a smaller economy orbits a larger one, a test that tells a system with two stable states from one with a common shock, and a measured limit on what a single national time series can show. It uses no real data and claims no finding about any real place.

IntroductionThe question

If economic and political cycles are coupled, can they have several stable states, and could a time series show it?

Macro series often move together. One reading is that a shared shock moves them. Another is that the system has more than one stable state and pushes between them. Both give series that are correlated, so correlation cannot tell them apart. This chapter builds the smallest model that has both readings, asks what test could separate them, and measures how often that test works on a series of the length macroeconomics usually has. The second half of the chapter is the honest part: at that length, the test often cannot answer.

Section 1The model

Two nodes, one larger and one smaller, each with its own stable states, and a pull between them.

Each node has a state that sits near one of two values, high or low. Node A is the larger economy. Node B is the smaller one and has shallower wells, which means it is easier to push from one state to the other. In the model, each node is pulled toward the other with strengths k_AB (A on B) and k_BA (B on A), and both are shaken by noise:

dxA = ( xA - xA^3 + k_BA (xB - xA) ) dt + s dW
dxB = ( mB xB - xB^3 + k_AB (xA - xB) ) dt + s dW        mB = 0.6 (shallower wells)

The only part derived by hand is the symmetric case, with both nodes equal (mB = 1) and equal pulls k. A state with the nodes on opposite sides, xA = −xB = x, needs x² = 1 − 2k, so it exists only when k < 1/2. The two eigenvalues of the linearization there are −2 + 6k and −2 + 4k, so it is stable only when k < 1/3. The states with both nodes on the same side are stable for every k. Stated plainly: enough coupling removes the several stable states. A strong pull makes the pair behave as one.

The script checks these thresholds against a numerical Jacobian and prints PASS or FAIL. What the model has is a pull, not care or investment: nothing in it says why a larger economy would pull a smaller one, or whether it should.

Section 2The test, and how it can fail

Stated before the numbers were run. The thresholds are the author’s and can be changed by anyone who would run it on real data, before they run it.

The test asks whether a series is better described by two hidden states than by one. It fits one Gaussian and a mixture of two, and calls the series bimodal only if both conditions hold: the Bayesian information criterion favors two by more than 10, and the two components are well separated (Ashman’s D above 2). The second condition exists because a series whose spread changes over time, with one mean throughout, can fool the first alone.

M1. The hand-derived thresholds are right.The numerical Jacobian gives the same stable and unstable ranges for k.It does not. The derivation is then wrong and Section 1 is withdrawn.
M2. Correlation cannot separate the two readings.A bistable pair and a linear pair with a common shock can be made to have the same correlation and the same spread.They cannot be matched. Then the premise of the chapter fails.
M3. The test gets three known cases right.It calls the bistable pair bimodal, and the linear pair with a common shock and the volatility cycle not bimodal.Any of the three verdicts is wrong. The test is then not used.
M4. The test has enough power at macro length.At about 250 points per series, it calls a truly bistable pair bimodal in at least 80% of series.Below 80%. Then a “not bimodal” verdict on one real series of that length is not evidence against several stable states, and the chapter says so.

Section 3What the toy does

Correlation cannot tell them apart (M2)

A bistable pair and a linear pair with a common shock were tuned to the same correlation, 0.828, and the same spread, 0.879. Nothing in those two numbers separates them.

The volatility cycle shows why the second condition is there: its BIC gain of 3996.1 is far above 10, and BIC alone would call it bimodal. Ashman’s D of 0.00 is what rules it out. The BIC gains are inflated because the points are not independent, so only the verdicts, not the sizes, mean anything.

Does a pull from the larger node change the smaller one’s fate?

A pull of A on B (k_AB > 0) reduces B’s flips and the time it spends opposite A; the flips of A do not change when B has no pull on A. When B pulls on A (k_BA > 0), A flips more often: 1077 flips with only B pulling, against 492 with none. That is the whole of the toy’s answer to “why would the larger one gain from keeping the smaller one steady”: it matters only if the smaller one’s pull on the larger one is not zero. That is a condition inside the model, not a finding about Newark, New York or anywhere else.

The controls are never called bimodal, which is good. But when the pair switches rarely, as real regimes do, the test finds the several stable states in only 17% of series at 0.2 flips per series and in 2% at none. M4 fails for those cases. A single national series can neither confirm nor rule out several stable states. A negative result on one series of that length means almost nothing.

Section 5What a real test would need

Not run. A design, with its failure conditions stated first.

The way out of the power problem is many units, not a longer series: a panel of cities, regions or countries, each observed over the same period, so that the number of possible switches is the number of units times the number of periods. The test would be applied as follows, and none of it has been done.

  • Before any run, compute the test’s power at the real panel’s size, as Section 4 does for a single series. If the power is below 80%, the data cannot answer and the result is reported as unable to answer, not as a negative.
  • Controls first. The two non-bistable constructions must be called bimodal in no more than 5% of series at that size. If they are called more often, the test is not used.
  • Choose the series before looking at them. The panel, the period and the measure are fixed in writing first, so the outcome cannot decide the choice.
  • The satellite question is a separate test and needs a measure of how strongly the small unit depends on the large one, such as commuting or fiscal flows. It is open, and the model above supplies a condition, not evidence.

A reader in public finance would know which regional series of that kind are held and in what form. That is the one thing this chapter asks of anyone: whether such a panel exists and is usable. It asks nothing else of any reader.

What would make this the wrong approach

Two-state models fit a series well when the series is bounded and noisy, which most macro series are not once trends are present. The method assumes the trend has been removed, and how it is removed can create or erase two peaks. The toy has no trend at all, so it cannot say how much this matters.

LimitsWhat this does not show

  • Any finding about a real economy. No real series was used. The model is a toy with chosen numbers.
  • That coupled cycles in markets, economics or geopolitics have several stable states. The model shows what such a system would do if they did.
  • That the test works on real data. It was run on simulated series with the known answer, and its power at the real data’s length is poor when regimes switch rarely.
  • Care, investment or any motive. The model has a pull and nothing else. Why a larger economy would support a smaller one is not in it.
  • Anything about Newark or New York. They appear here only as the motivating example.
  • Any experience in dynamical systems. The author claims none; the derivation is one page and the script checks it.
  • Any agreement, interest or review. No one at the university has seen this page.
  • Anything after the date written. 13 November 2026.
