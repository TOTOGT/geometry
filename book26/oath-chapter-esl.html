The oath is not enough

Source essay with full references

Instructional edition · advanced reading unit · preprint

Before you read

  1. Have you ever made a promise that was hard to keep? What helped you keep it?
  2. Do you ask your doctor questions? Why or why not?
  3. Predict: why might a promise not be enough to make people trust a professional?

Part 1: The idea in four words

A doctor often makes an oath, a serious public promise, at the start of a career. The Hippocratic Oath is the most famous one. People say it contains the words "first, do no harm", but those exact words are not in the ancient text. A modern pledge, the World Medical Association's Declaration of Geneva (adopted in 1948, last changed in 2017), says: "the health and well-being of my patient will be my first consideration."

A promise has value. It says what a profession is for. But a promise cannot check itself. It tells us what someone intends, not what they did. This essay uses four ideas:

  • Oath: the promise.
  • Ethics: thinking carefully about what is right, including in situations the promise did not predict.
  • Righteous conduct: acting on those reasons, even when it is difficult or costly, and even when nobody is watching.
  • Accountability: making it possible for other people to examine what happened, question decisions and find mistakes.

Part 2: When promises were not enough

History shows serious harm inside professions that had formal codes of conduct. These cases show failures of professional and institutional safeguards.

  • The Nuremberg Code. After the trial of Nazi-era doctors (1946 to 1947), a court judgment stated: "The voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential." It was never passed as a separate law, but it became an important reference for research ethics.
  • The Tuskegee study (1932 to 1972). Researchers followed Black men with untreated syphilis and did not inform them, long after treatment existed. Public anger led to new laws and to the Belmont Report (1979), which names three principles: respect for persons, beneficence (doing good) and justice.
  • Farid Fata (sentenced in 2015). A Michigan cancer doctor was sentenced to 45 years in prison for health-care fraud after giving unnecessary chemotherapy. CBS News reported that a former employee told a superior, and he was arrested days later.

The lesson is not that doctors cannot be trusted. It is that trust needs more than a promise. In the last case, an employee willing to speak up made the difference.

Part 3: A story about disclosure

Dr Leana Wen, an emergency physician, told this story at a TEDMED talk in September 2014. Her mother had advanced breast cancer and was receiving chemotherapy. Looking online for her oncologist's phone number, her mother found him listed as a paid speaker for a drug company, often promoting the same chemotherapy he had prescribed her. She called her daughter in a panic. Wen, a doctor herself, did not know whether the treatment was right for her mother.

Wen does not say the oncologist did anything wrong. Her point is that the patient found out by accident, and nobody had told her.

In the United States, drug and device companies must report certain payments to doctors, and the government publishes them in a public database called Open Payments. It does not cover every financial relationship, and it does not oblige a doctor to tell patients in the consulting room.

Does money change treatment? The evidence is mixed. A 2026 study in the Journal of Clinical Oncology, based on Medicare records, found that payments were associated with slightly more use of the drug being promoted, whether or not guidelines preferred it. The differences were small, and an association does not prove that payments caused the choices. Whatever the answer, patients can only weigh a conflict of interest if they know about it.

Part 4: What a patient can do

This is not medical advice and not a reason to refuse treatment. It is a way to join the checking.

  • Consider a second opinion. It is generally a legitimate part of informed decision-making. Ask your clinician how long you can safely take to decide, and do not delay urgent treatment without medical guidance.
  • Ask what the alternatives are, what the evidence is for each, and what the risks of waiting are.
  • Ask the doctor to explain unfamiliar medical words in plain language, and repeat the explanation in your own words to check your understanding.
  • Ask about conflicts directly. Payments from companies to doctors can be looked up in Open Payments, though searching may be difficult or inconclusive. A payment is a reason to ask more questions, not proof of bad advice.

Why do professionals sometimes explain things badly? Sometimes money plays a part, but there are ordinary reasons too: short appointments, paperwork, caution about liability, and the difficulty of explaining uncertainty. None of this excuses a poor explanation. It means patients are better served by asking for what they need than by guessing at motives.

Part 5: Conclusion

An oath states a commitment. Ethics gives us reasons to examine and debate. Righteous conduct means acting on those reasons, even when doing so is difficult or costly. Accountability allows others to examine our actions, question our decisions, and identify mistakes.

Professional promises matter, but they cannot establish trust on their own. Trust becomes more defensible when promises are supported by responsible conduct, when mistakes can be acknowledged and corrected, and when patients have the information they need to participate in decisions about their care.

Comprehension questions

  2. What are the four ideas the essay uses? Explain each in one sentence.
  3. In the Fata case, what brought the problem to light?
  4. Does Dr Wen say her mother's oncologist did something wrong? What is her actual point?

Discussion and writing

  1. "A promise tells us what someone intends, not what they did." Do you agree? Give an example from any profession.
  2. Which of the four ideas is hardest to put into practice? Why?
  3. Should doctors be required to tell patients about payments from companies? Argue for one position, then state the strongest objection to it.
  4. Writing task (150 to 200 words): choose a profession (teacher, banker, engineer). Describe one way it could make its promises easier to check.
