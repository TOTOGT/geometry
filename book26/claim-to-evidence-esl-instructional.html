From claim to evidence

Instructional edition · advanced reading and checking task · preprint

Before you read

  1. You read: "This AI system is as good as a doctor." What would you want to know before you believe it?
  2. Have you ever checked a number in a news story or a paper? What did you do?

Part 1: The idea

New medical AI systems appear all the time, and papers and companies make claims about them: for example, "the system finds pneumonia as well as radiologists". A good reader does not ask "Can we trust this AI?" That question is too big. A better question is: What exactly does the evidence support?

A good reader also keeps three kinds of checking apart:

  • Arithmetic check: can you get the same number from the numbers printed in the paper?
  • Statistical check: do the reported comparisons really support the conclusion? This often needs data that are not printed.
  • Clinical check: would the system work safely and well with real patients? Repeating a paper's calculations cannot answer this.

Part 2: Five steps

  1. Extract the claim. Copy the exact sentence. Note the group of patients, the data, the measure used, and the limits the authors name.
  2. Trace the evidence. Find the paper, any extra files, any code and any data that are public. Note what is not public.
  3. Recompute. Redo the calculations you can. Never invent a missing number.
  4. Test the logic. A correct calculation is not the same as proof of the conclusion. Ask: does this number really show what the claim says?
  5. Record the limit. Give each finding one label: verified, partly supported, contradicted, or cannot be checked independently.

There is one more idea. Sometimes two numbers agree only because they came from the same source or the same assumptions. This is agreement by construction. It shows the numbers are consistent with each other. It does not show that either one is right.

Part 3: An example, CheXNet

A well-known paper (Rajpurkar and colleagues, arXiv:1711.05225, version 3) tested an AI system called CheXNet on 420 chest X-rays. Four radiologists (doctors who read X-rays) also labelled the images. The abstract says CheXNet "exceeds average radiologist performance on the F1 metric". F1 is a score from 0 to 1 that combines two kinds of accuracy. Higher is better.

CheXNet0.435
Radiologist 10.383
Radiologist 20.356
Radiologist 30.365
Radiologist 40.442
Radiologist average0.387

The paper also prints a difference between CheXNet and the average: 0.051, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.005 to 0.084. (A confidence interval is a range that shows how uncertain an estimate is.)

Part 4: Your task, check the numbers

Use a calculator. Write your answers and say which of the four labels each finding gets.

  1. The average. Add the four radiologists' scores and divide by 4. Is the result the same as the printed average, 0.387? (The printed numbers have three decimals, so allow a difference of about 0.0005 in each.)
  2. The difference. Subtract the printed average from CheXNet's score: 0.435 − 0.387. Is the result the same as the printed difference, 0.051? What do you notice?
  3. Every radiologist? The claim says CheXNet beats the average radiologist. Does it also beat every radiologist? Look at the table.
  4. What cannot be checked. The paper says it used a method called bootstrap (computing the score again on many random samples of the same data) to get the confidence intervals. Can you repeat it with only the numbers in the table? Why or why not?
  5. The claim. The abstract is about the F1 score on 420 images. Does that prove the system would work well in a hospital? Explain in two sentences.

Check yourself

Your answers should show: (1) the average of the four scores is 0.3865, which matches 0.387; (2) the subtraction gives 0.048, not 0.051, and the paper does not explain the gap in the text we read, so this is an unexplained difference, not a proven mistake (the paper may compute the difference in a different way); (3) no, radiologist 4 scored 0.442, which is higher than CheXNet's 0.435; (4) no, the bootstrap needs the image-level data, which are not printed; (5) no, a score on one set of 420 images does not show safe or effective use in clinical practice.

Part 5: Rules for the checker

  • Do not say a paper is wrong when you only found something you cannot explain.
  • Do not count two numbers that agree by construction as two checks.
  • Say what you did not check. "I could not find this" is not the same as "this is false."
  • You are checking how a claim is supported. You are not giving medical or statistical advice.

Discussion

  1. Why is "Can we trust this AI?" a weak question? What is a stronger one?
  2. Find one claim about health technology in the news. Write the exact sentence and list what you would need to check it.
  3. Why is it useful for a checker to write "I could not check this"?
