From claim to evidence: an auditable method for evaluating medical AI

Pablo Nogueira Grossi · Newark, NJ

The problem
Medical-AI papers and product claims arrive faster than most clinicians and researchers can check them. The useful question is not "is this AI trustworthy?" but "exactly which claims does the available evidence support?"
What this chapter gives you
A five-step procedure, a vocabulary of findings that includes a category for agreement that proves nothing, and one worked example on a public paper, with the code that produces each recomputation.
Skills shown
Reading a technical paper for its exact claims, recomputing what the printed numbers allow, separating a correct calculation from evidence for a conclusion, and recording limits in a form others can challenge.
How it was made
The method and the worked example were produced with AI assistance (Claude) under my direction. The paper's numbers were extracted from the PDF by a tool. A reviewer then confirmed the key figures against the PDF (see the boundaries note in section 3); I have not, and not every number was in that confirmation. No statistician or clinician has reviewed this chapter.

Scope: A method for checking claims, not a clinical product. It uses no patient records, makes no claim of clinical validation, and does not replace medical or statistical peer review. The worked example does not conclude that the paper is right or wrong.

1. What question this answers

A medical-AI claim can fail in many ways, and most are not mistakes of arithmetic. A correct number can sit on a test set too small to bear it, a reference standard that is itself uncertain, or a metric that does not measure what the headline implies. This method does not rate a system as trustworthy or untrustworthy. It records, claim by claim, what the available evidence supports and where it stops.

2. The five steps

  1. Extract the claim. Copy the exact performance statement, with its population, dataset, metric and stated limitations.
  2. Trace the evidence. Find the paper, supplementary material, code and underlying data that are publicly available, and note which are not.
  3. Recompute what is reproducible. Check arithmetic, denominators, confidence intervals and reported comparisons. Never fill a gap with an assumed value.
  4. Test the logical connection. Ask whether an internally correct calculation actually supports the clinical conclusion drawn from it.
  5. Record the boundary. Give each finding one of four statuses: verified, partially supported, contradicted, or not independently checkable.

Three kinds of verification, kept apart

Each ledger row is tagged with the kind of check it is, because they answer different questions and one does not stand in for another:

  • Arithmetic verification: can a number be reproduced from the figures printed in the paper?
  • Statistical verification: do the reported comparisons support the stated performance conclusion? This often needs data that are not printed.
  • Clinical validation: does the evidence show the system would perform safely and effectively in clinical practice? Reproducing a paper's calculations cannot establish this.

One extra label: agreement by construction

Two calculations that agree are not independent confirmation if they share the same assumptions, inputs or code. A total that matches the sum of the parts it was built from, or a rebuilt number that only restates the same inputs, tells you the numbers are consistent with each other, not that either is correct. An interval that sits near its estimate is an even weaker signal, since percentile-bootstrap intervals can be asymmetric. The ledger marks these cases so that a reader does not count them as checks.

Choosing a paper: the aim is not to find a flawed one. A successful verification is as informative as a failed one, so the example below was chosen because it is public, widely cited, and prints enough numbers to recompute something, not because its claims look doubtful.

3. Worked example: CheXNet

Source: Rajpurkar et al., CheXNet: Radiologist-Level Pneumonia Detection on Chest X-Rays with Deep Learning, arXiv:1711.05225, version v3 (25 December 2017). Claim (abstract): "We find that CheXNet exceeds average radiologist performance on the F1 metric."

What the paper prints

A test set of 420 frontal chest X-rays from 389 patients, labelled by four radiologists. F1 for CheXNet 0.435 (95% CI 0.387 to 0.481); for the radiologist average 0.387 (0.330 to 0.442); individual radiologists 0.383, 0.356, 0.365 and 0.442; a difference of 0.051 (0.005 to 0.084). Intervals are bootstrap percentiles from 10,000 resamples. The text I read does not give the number of pneumonia-positive images. Note that the highest individual radiologist score (0.442) is above CheXNet's 0.435, so "exceeds the average radiologist" is not "exceeds every radiologist".

A check that can fail

negative_control.py changes the printed radiologist average to a wrong value and reruns the checks. C1 then reports Contradicted, so the arithmetic check is capable of failing. When the difference is changed to match the altered average, C2 passes, which is correct: that check tests consistency between printed numbers, not their truth.

What the example does and does not show

The printed radiologist average does follow from the four individual values. The printed difference of 0.051 does not follow from the printed point estimates (0.048 after rounding allowance), and the text I read does not say why. That is an unexplained discrepancy and not a demonstrated error: it may reflect a different statistic computed inside the bootstrap. The F1 values, the bootstrap intervals, and the claim itself cannot be rechecked without per-image data. The headline claim is about F1 on one 420-image set, and a reader should keep that qualifier attached.

Boundaries of this example. I did not read the paper's methods section for the exact definition of the reference standard, and my text extraction may have dropped part of the limitations section. I did not check later versions of the paper, a published journal version, or any follow-up work. A reviewer confirmed against the PDF the main figures: Table 1 on page 2 (CheXNet 0.435, radiologist average 0.387, radiologist 4 0.442) and Section 4.1 on page 3 (difference 0.051, 95% CI 0.005 to 0.084, computed on the same bootstrap samples). I have not compared them with the PDF myself, and the other printed values were not part of that confirmation. Anyone relying on the remaining numbers should check them against the PDF.

Two points of precision. First, the paper describes its comparison as against the radiologist average, so row C7 contradicts an overgeneralized reading of the claim, not the authors' actual comparison. Second, a negative control shows only that the checker catches the alteration that was tried. It does not show that the checker would catch every possible inconsistency.

4. How this connects to my other work

The same habit runs through the rest of this book: recompute quoted numbers, state what was and was not checked, and keep verified, partly supported and unchecked claims apart. The oath chapter applies the idea to professional promises. This chapter applies it to performance claims. The common principle is that accountability needs records others can inspect, not an assurance that someone checked.
