A Preprint Against a Kernel

A 247-page theory cites five Lean-verified theorems. What the five say, what is still unchecked, and what a full check would cost.
What this is

A working record, partly done. The preprint is cited as literature (J. L. Nielsen, “A Complete and Unified Gauge Theory with Gravity on the Complex Hopf Fibration”, preprint dated 14 Aug 2026). This chapter is about its claims and its Lean file, not its author. Five of the fourteen checklist items below are open. A checked box means a step was done and can be re-run, not that the theory is right or wrong.

What was received

The preprint claims that charge quantization and unification force one fibre bundle, and that the Standard Model gauge group, three generations, the particle masses and the cosmological constant then follow as theorems with no free parameter. Its abstract says five structural theorems are checked in Lean 4. It has about 300 lines containing the word “Theorem”; a count of distinctly numbered statements gives 140.

A full kernel check of everything cannot be done here or soon. Most of the statements concern objects the Lean library does not yet have: bundles with connections, spectra of the Beltrami operator, Ray–Singer torsion. So the work is sorted by what a kernel can reach.

The checklist

Five steps are done. Seven are open.

The audit lists 7 theorems, all within the three standard axioms: five depend on none, one on propext, one on propext and Quot.sound. This matches what the paper reports. Report: tools/verify-audit/2026-10-26/.
  • C2Read each of the five kernels for what it says. A reading, not a proof, with two of the points confirmed by short examples that compiled (see section 3). None of the five is about physics.
  • C3Census of the numbered statements. 140 in all: 83 theorems, 22 corollaries, 10 propositions, 10 lemmas, 13 definitions, 2 axioms. The tier labels (about 22 arithmetic or algebra, about 99 needing objects the Lean library lacks) come from keyword matching and are a first sort, not a reading of each statement. File: book26/nielsen-census.tsv.
  • C4Check the numbers in the abstract. Recomputed in Python (book26/nielsen-abstract-arithmetic.py) and checked in Lean (book26/NielsenAbstractArithmetic.lean; 6 theorems, standard axioms only, run 2026-10-26). Reproduced: 3/(4π) = 0.238732, which is 0.39σ from the Thomson-limit value the paper compares with; the neutrino masses sum to 59.282 meV; the boson masses sit within 0.23σ of PDG 2025; the cosmological-constant formula gives 2.940×10−122. Not reproduced as a prediction: at the Z pole, the abstract’s own masses give 1 − (mW/mZ)2 = 0.2232, which differs from 3/(4π) by more than 0.015, and the paper bridges the gap with a “dressing” equal to the residual. The larger neutrino splitting is compared with 2.453×10−3 eV2, which I believe, from memory and not yet verified, is the 3–2 splitting; against the 3–1 splitting from the one NuFit-6.0 block I could read it is −3.2σ. The H0 value is the cosmological-constant formula rearranged with the measured dark-energy fraction.
  • C5Check the algebra statements (about 6).
  • C6Hypothesis audit of the remaining statements (about 99). For each: what is assumed, what is derived, and what a kernel would need that does not exist yet.
  • C7Read the full 247 pages. Only the front matter, the contents, one table and the Lean appendix have been read.
  • C8Reconcile the stated inputs. Checked against the source with book26/ch08-inputs-verify.py (8 checks, 3 controls). The item’s suspicion holds, and the contradiction is between two pages of the same paper. p. 12, under the heading “Proof of zero dimensionless free parameters”: “The only dimensionful input is the electroweak VEV, v = 246 220 MeV (Axiom 1)”, and the paper “contains no fitting routine, no optimization loop, and no adjustable coefficient”. p. 216, Table 16 — whose stated purpose is to “distinguish what TUFT assumes from what it predicts” — puts under Predictions: “SM gauge group + GR; c, ℏ, e, α, G, v”. The same v is Axiom 1 on one page and an output on the other, and the Inputs row of that table lists only topological normalisations. A second mismatch on the same page. Table 16’s own reproducibility note sets masses by mj = (ℏ/c)(λj/reff) “with v/R fixed once across sectors”. Fixing a ratio once so that sectors agree is a calibration, and the formula consumes ℏ and c, which that same table calls predictions. A quantity cannot be an output of the calculation that uses it as an input. The p. 11 table is a third labelling. There v is marked “Input (v)”, while α = 1/137.036, G, c and e are all “Derived”. So the paper gives three accounts of its own inputs in eleven, twelve and two hundred sixteen pages, and they do not agree. Not established: that v cannot be derived, or that Theorem 68 (G) and Theorem 66 (α) fail — neither was read. What would settle C8: one table, stating for each of c, ℏ, e, α, G and v whether it is assumed or computed, and if computed, from what. The claim of zero free parameters is only as strong as that list.
  • C9Check the sin²θW claim. Checked against the source, p. 123, eq. (100), with book26/ch08-sin2w-verify.py (6 checks, 4 controls). The scheme and the uncertainty. The 0.4σ is against the PDG Thomson-limit value sin²θW(0) = 0.23867 ± 0.00016; recomputed, 0.39σ. Against the MS-bar value at MZ, 0.23122 ± 0.00006, the same number is 125σ. The paper does not report that as a failure: it writes that the PDG value “implies” a spectral correction Δspec ≈ −0.0075, which is back-solved from the discrepancy rather than computed from the Hopf-shell spectrum. The same unquantified mechanism then absorbs a 5.0% offset in g and 3.0% in g′. The derivation against its own definition. Two lines above eq. (100) the paper writes sin²θW = g′²/(g²+g′²), where g′ is the hypercharge coupling; eq. (100) then identifies the numerator as the weight “carried by the SU(2) sector” and puts dim SU(2) = 3 on top. By the paper’s own formula the SU(2) fraction is cos²θW. And a generator count over a Gauss–Bonnet integral ∫K dA = 4π is not a ratio of gauge weights: carried out as the prose describes, the degree-of-freedom ratio is 1/4 or 3/4, neither of which is 0.2387. Two derived values of one symbol. The same page derives it again from the paper’s own Table 6 masses: 1 − mW²/mZ² = 0.223200, which reproduces exactly and matches the PDG on-shell value to 10−5. So the theory yields 0.238732 and 0.223200 for one quantity — 6.96% apart, 97σ in the very uncertainty used to claim 0.4σ — and each is compared to whichever PDG scheme value it lands on. Not reproduced: eq. (101) prints g ≈ 0.6205; 4π√(α/3) with α = 1/137.036 gives 0.6198. Their α comes from Theorem 66, unread, so this may be internal. What would settle it: a value of Δspec computed from the shell spectrum without reference to the measured number, and a statement of which scheme eq. (100) is a prediction of. Neither is in the paper. This is a finding about internal consistency, not a claim that 3/(4π) is wrong physics or that no derivation could exist.
  • C10Confirm the product-splitting reading of kernel 2. The reading in section 3 is a hand derivation and has not been tested.
  • C11Check the remaining arithmetic claims. Quark and boson masses, couplings and the anomalous moments, each tabulated from the paper’s own script as the lepton sector now is.
  • C12Lepton sector: formula, constants and outputs in one table. From the paper’s own appendix script, transcribed by hand and confirmed against what that script prints (book26/nielsen-lepton-table.py, run). The three masses come out 0.23% high, a uniform offset the script itself prints, which is more than 106, 105 and 40 standard deviations for the electron, muon and tau, so the abstract’s “within PDG uncertainties” does not describe them. The mass ratios agree to 0.002% and 0.006%, which is a one-parameter result anchored on one mass. The exponent has five terms; the one that distinguishes the tau, a trefoil “regularization”, acts on that lepton alone and moves it by about 1.1%. A Lean version of the deviation comparison is written (NielsenLeptonDeviation.lean) and was run on 2026-10-26: 363 s, three declarations, axioms within the permitted set, no sorryAx (receipt: tools/verify-audit/2026-10-26/geometry__book26__NielsenLeptonDeviation.axioms.txt). A clean axiom report is not a reading of the statement — per R20 a theorem can assume its conclusion and still report clean — so what this establishes is that three named declarations elaborate, not that they say the deviations exceed the quoted uncertainties.
  • C13The lens-space determinant: is the Gaussian exponent ζ(3) or ζ(3)/(4π²)? Computed with book26/nielsen-lens-determinant.py (6 checks). The preprint’s Sector Determinant Lemma asserts ln det′ Bn = L(n) − ζ(3)·n² + O(1) and uses exp(−ζ(3)n²) in the mass formula. The coefficient is ζ(3)/(4π²) = 0.030448, not bare ζ(3) = 1.202 — smaller by 4π² = 39.478 in the exponent. Method. On the unit S³ the coexact 1-form eigenvalues of ★d are ±N for N ≥ 2 in the SU(2)L×SU(2)R representations (N/2,(N−2)/2) and its swap; the ℤn is the Hopf U(1) in SU(2)L, so the invariant sector keeps weights 2mL ≡ 0 (mod n). The resulting multiplicity is quasi-polynomial of period 2n, so the spectral zeta is a finite sum of Hurwitz zetas and ln det′ = −ζ′(0) is exact: no cutoff, no truncation. Why the number holds, three ways. (i) At n = 1 the sector is all of S³, ζ(s) = 2[ζR(s−2) − ζR(s)], and the code reproduces the closed form ζ(3)/(2π²) − ln 2π = −1.776980152292558942 to 31 digits. This is also where the 4π² comes from: ζR′(−2) = −ζ(3)/(4π²) exactly, and the n² term inherits it. (ii) The quasi-polynomial multiplicities match a direct enumeration of SU(2) weights for n ∈ {1,2,3,5,7,8} and every N < 200. (iii) The coefficient was not fitted against a target: it converges as the window moves up (6×10−7 at n = 8–64, 5×10−8 at 32–64), and a local three-point solve with the target absent from the basis gives −0.030448471 at n = 48. Consequence. With the correct coefficient the Gaussian factor in m2/m1 is 0.913, not 0.027. It is then inert, and whatever generates the lepton hierarchy is not this term. Two further defects in the lemma as stated. The “+ O(1)” is too strong — there is a growing ln n term, fitted at ≈ 4/3. And Cheeger–Müller cannot confirm this coefficient: torsion is an alternating combination of determinants across all form degrees, in which a metric-dependent n² term cancels, so the second citation offered as support cannot bear on it. Not established: that the asymptotic follows from the preprint’s cited route through Nash–O’Connor; the ln n coefficient analytically; the non-trivial χn sectors; or sensitivity to radius and zeta normalisation. Worth separating out: the corrected statement is a small standalone result in spectral topology, refereeable without any physics.
  • C14The fork: run the mass formula with the coefficient C13 derived. book26/nielsen-exponent-fork.py (6 checks, 2 controls). C12 established that the preprint’s own appendix script reproduces its lepton table using exp(−ζ(3)n²); C13 established that the lens-space determinant gives ζ(3)/(4π²). Substituting the second for the first, holding Λ, a, β, α and the trefoil factor fixed: me +223%, mμ +1.08×104%, mτ +3.81×106%. The spectrum does not shift; it is destroyed. And the escape route is closed by the paper’s own argument. The failure is in the ratios — μ/e comes out 6949.7 against the observed 206.77, a factor of 33.6; τ/μ comes out 5886.5 against 16.82, a factor of 350. p. 12 argues that v is a unit conversion because “changing v multiplies every mass by the same constant” and “leaves invariant every mass ratio”. That is correct, and it means the single empirical input cannot repair a ratio failure. Λshell cancels in m2/m1 and m3/m2. So exactly one of these holds, and both contradict “zero dimensionless free parameters”: (A) the Sector Determinant Lemma is right, and the mass formula is wrong by factors of 34 and 350 in the ratios; or (B) the mass formula is right, and the exponent ζ(3) is not the lens-space determinant coefficient — in which case its stated derivation does not support it, and the value that fits the data is precisely the one the derivation does not give. Not established: which branch holds; whether the lemma was ever meant to apply to this formula; or whether a and φn are right — both remain undefined in the text.

What the five kernels say

KernelWhat the Lean provesWhat it does not
1. Charge quantization forces holonomyIf some charge is inadmissible, the holonomy is not trivial. A contrapositive in a setup where Charge, Phase and Loop are arbitrary types.Nothing about U(1), a charge lattice or a connection.
2. A domain has no product splitA ring with no zero divisors has only the idempotents 0 and 1, and a direct product of two nonzero rings has zero divisors.A direct product of rings corresponds to a disjoint union of spaces (connectedness). A Cartesian product of spaces corresponds to a tensor product (Künneth). My reading, untested: the Lean does not prove “no product splitting of the bundle”.
3. Completeness forces universalityAny two classifying objects for the same bundle functor are equivalent. The paper’s U1_forcing is this theorem renamed; an example that closes by rfl shows it.That the complex projective space classifies anything. That is a hypothesis.
4. Completeness as a theoremEvery bundle is a pullback of the universal one. This restates a field of the hypothesis Classifies, closed by that field.That nature’s fields satisfy the hypothesis.
5. Affine absorptionAdding a constant and a linear term does not change a second difference. Linear integer arithmetic.Anything about spectra or masses.

The Lean compiles and the axioms are standard, so these are real theorems. The gap is between what they state and what their names and the abstract claim.

What would make a claim like this refereeable

This section is about method, and applies to any large claim, including this project’s own. A kernel checks that conclusions follow from stated hypotheses. It cannot check that nature satisfies them. So the useful question is where each claim sits relative to the kernel.

  1. Split the work into pieces a referee can read. One paper on what the premises force, one on a fixed formula and its numbers, one on cosmology and neutrinos. A referee can check twenty-five pages; no one can referee 247.
  2. Freeze the formula before comparing. Publish the exact formula, every constant and every discrete choice, with a commit hash, and only then compare with the data. Count each discrete choice as a parameter and say how many there are.
  3. Add negative controls. Put the formula on a wrong case, such as a wrong lens space or a shuffled level, and show the fit gets worse. Without that, close agreement is not informative.
  4. Use the kernel where it is strong. Check that each number in a results table is exactly the output of the stated formula and constants. Give each Lean theorem a mutation test: negate a hypothesis and confirm it stops holding. Where the library lacks an object, state a named hypothesis and list it in a ledger.
  5. Reconcile the arithmetic with the prose. Compare each quantity with the matching reference quantity and report both uncertainties. Say which statements are predictions and which are rearrangements.
  6. Write at the strength of the evidence. Vary the strength of claims, let headings ask questions, and define terms where they first appear.

One claim in this preprint is pure mathematics and independent of the physics: that a lens-space determinant yields a ζ(3)n² term. A topologist can referee it as it stands, which makes it the natural first piece to submit.

What this does not show
