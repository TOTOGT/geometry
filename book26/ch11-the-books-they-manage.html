The Books They Manage

What the New York State Insurance Fund’s own public documents show about the bond portfolio an assistant portfolio manager would help run. Newark, NJ · 28 October 2026
Note on naming the employer

Unlike chapters 1 to 9, this chapter names the organization, as chapter 10 does, because it reads only that organization’s published documents and is a work sample for one posting (vacancy 227844). Every figure below is from the 2023 annual report or the 2020 investment policy, except where the posting is cited. Nothing here comes from anyone inside the Fund. It is not a statement by the Fund, and it is not advice.

The posting

The Fund posted an Assistant Corporate Bond Portfolio Manager role on 27 October 2026, with applications due 1 December 2026. The posted salary is $179,150 to $229,150 a year, full time and permanent, at 199 Church Street, New York. The posting says the Fund manages more than $22 billion and that the corporate bond exposure is more than $9 billion. The official civil-service title is Special Investment Officer. Candidates send a résumé and cover letter to the Fund’s recruiting address.

The question this chapter asks is narrower than whether the role is a good one: what is actually in the books the role touches, according to the Fund’s own filings?

What is in the books (31 December 2023)

The Workers’ Compensation Fund reported $21.0 billion of cash and invested assets in its 2023 annual report. Bonds were $18.48 billion of that, or 87.9 percent; common stocks were $1.83 billion (8.7 percent); cash and short-term investments were $463 million (2.2 percent). Within the bonds, corporates and public utilities are the largest category, about half the bond book, which agrees with the posting’s “more than $9 billion.”

The rules the books run under

The Fund’s investment policy statement, in the version amended on 18 November 2020, sets the frame for the main Workers’ Compensation Fund. Investment-grade fixed income must be between 78 and 100 percent of market value, with a target of 85. The other targets are 4 percent high yield, 5 percent public equities, 4 percent alternatives and 2 percent cash. The fixed income portfolio’s weighted average rating must be at least mid-single A (A2), and its duration target is 8 years, plus or minus 1. The policy asks for enough U.S. government securities to cover 75 percent of liabilities due within a year, allows securities lending under the Workers’ Compensation Law, and allows derivatives only with surplus funds. The policy statement itself contains no issuer or sector limits; any such limits would sit in manager guidelines or other documents not read here.

The policy has moved since

The Fund’s board minutes of 30 October 2025 record that the board approved an updated policy, replacing the resolution of 18 December 2024, and that its strategic asset allocation was amended “to reflect the current market environment and risks.” The minutes give no figures. The minutes also say the board reviews and adopts the policy once a year, so the 2020 version read here is several revisions old, and the targets above are not necessarily today’s.

What the numbers say, and what they do not

The book was $1.49 billion below its cost at the end of 2023, about 8 percent. Most of that is not in corporates. U.S. Government and agency bonds, whose credit risk is small, were down about 14 percent of book; their prices fall when rates rise. Corporates were down about 4.6 percent. A reader who looks for the losses in the corporate bonds would find less than a third of them there at that date. The report’s maturity table points to interest rates as the main cause: bonds due after ten years were $1.03 billion below cost, 69 percent of the whole gap, while bonds due within a year were within 1 percent of cost. The table does not split the corporate loss into its rate and credit parts, so how much of that loss is credit is not measured here.

These are unrealized losses on a book run against long-dated liabilities. They are not realized losses. The report does show $85.3 million of net realized losses and impairments on bonds in 2023 (p. 26), so some bonds were sold or written down at a loss; it does not say which. They do show why the policy’s liquidity rule and 8-year duration target matter, and why the person working beside the portfolio manager would spend time on relative value and cash planning, not only on credit selection.

What a daily monitor would watch

These are the author’s proposals, not the Fund’s plans, and the monitor does not exist yet. The 2020 policy benchmarks the main fund’s investment-grade bonds against a “NYSIF Custom Benchmark” whose make-up it does not give; ICE BofA indices appear only for cash and for two smaller funds. A daily note could still track the Treasury curve (especially the 7 to 10 year point and the long end), the option-adjusted spread on investment-grade corporates by rating (A and BBB), and the change in each since the prior day, all from public data. The first test is whether it reproduces a known past move correctly; the second is whether it tells a person something the morning market summary does not.

The strongest opposing case

A critic would say this chapter reads a 2023 report and a 2020 policy to describe a 2026 book, that the allocation has been changed since, that unrealized losses on a liability-matched portfolio tell an outsider very little, and that no outsider can say how the desk actually trades. All four are fair. The chapter claims only what the filings show for the dates they cover.

What this does not show

Not established here
  • The Fund’s current holdings, ratings mix, duration or allocation. The latest figures read here are for 31 December 2023.
  • That the author meets the posting’s experience requirement. The author’s background is corporate credit and securities finance, not a bond trading book; the application says so.
  • The credit-rating distribution. It was not found in the 2023 report’s notes for this fund (searched for NAIC designations, letter ratings and “investment grade”).
  • Anything from the 2024 annual report, which is published and was not read for this version.
