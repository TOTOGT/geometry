The oath is not enough: ethics, righteousness and the records that show them

Pablo Nogueira Grossi · Independent research

The question
A professional oath is a promise made once, in words, before anyone can check it. What do ethics and righteous conduct add, and how can a reader tell that they are present?
What this chapter gives you
A short argument in four parts (oath, ethics, righteous conduct, accountability), three historical examples, two contemporary accounts about disclosure, practical questions for patients, and a record of how I handled corrections in my own work.
Skills shown
Ethical analysis and plain-language writing for a non-specialist reader. The section on my own corrections gives one checkable example: the 19 September 2026 withdrawal and its public record.
How it was made
The argument and structure are mine. The drafting and the source checks were done with AI assistance (Claude) under my direction. Facts about real cases come from the sources listed at the end; I have not independently re-verified them.

Scope: An essay, not medical, legal or ethics-committee advice. It makes no claim about the motives of any named person. It does not say oaths are worthless.

1. What an oath does

An oath is a promise. It is spoken once, usually at the start of a career, and it states an intention before there is any chance to test it. That has real value. It tells the person taking it, and everyone watching, what the profession says it is for. The Hippocratic Oath is the best-known example. It is ancient, it survives in several versions, and the phrase "first, do no harm" that people attach to it is not in the classical text in those words. Modern professions use newer pledges, such as the World Medical Association's Declaration of Geneva, adopted in 1948 and last amended in 2017. It includes the pledges that "the health and well-being of my patient will be my first consideration" and "I will not use my medical knowledge to violate human rights and civil liberties, even under threat."

What an oath cannot do is check itself. It says what someone intends. It does not show what they did, what they were paid, what they hid, or what they corrected.

2. Where the promise was not enough

The record of medicine and research shows serious harm inside professions that had formal codes of conduct. These cases show failures of professional and institutional safeguards, not a finding about which individuals took which oath. Several led to new rules or oversight, and in each case the promise had not been enough.

  • Nazi-era human experiments. The Nuremberg Code came out of the 1946 to 1947 trial of physicians. Its first principle reads: "The voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential." It was set out in a court judgment, not a statute, and became a reference point for later research ethics.
  • The Tuskegee syphilis study (1932 to 1972). Researchers in a public-health programme followed Black men with untreated syphilis, without informing them, long after treatment existed. The public outcry led to the National Research Act (1974) and then the Belmont Report (1979), which names three principles: respect for persons, beneficence and justice. Research on people is now subject to human-subjects oversight requirements, including review boards.
  • A modern single-practitioner case. In 2015 a Michigan oncologist, Farid Fata, was sentenced to 45 years in prison for health-care fraud after giving unnecessary chemotherapy to patients, some of whom did not have cancer. CBS News reported that a former employee alerted a superior, and Fata was arrested days later.

The lesson in the third case is not that doctors are untrustworthy. It is that trust was placed in a person and a promise, and, as CBS reported it, what brought the case to light was a former employee willing to report.

3. Oath, ethics, righteous conduct, accountability

The oath is the promise. It is necessary as a statement of purpose, and it is the cheapest of the four to give.

Ethics is reasoning about what is right, applied to cases the oath did not foresee. It asks who is affected, who consented, who benefits, who bears the risk, and whether the answer would survive being shown to the people involved. The Belmont principles are a good example because they are reasons that can be argued with and applied, not a pledge.

Righteous conduct, as I use the phrase here, is acting on those reasons, including when it costs something: correcting a claim that favours you, reporting a colleague's fraud, declining a result you wanted. It does not need an audience. Someone can do the right thing privately, without recognition, and it is right all the same.

Accountability is what lets other people tell. Conduct that leaves no trace cannot be evaluated by anyone else, so a profession that wants to be trusted needs records that others can inspect: informed consent forms, review boards, conflict-of-interest disclosure, audit trails, and protection for people who report. An oath alone provides no evidence that its promises were kept. Ethics is tested by argument, and conduct by whatever evidence exists of what was done. Accountability is the work of making that evidence exist.

4. A story about disclosure

The story that prompted this chapter is told by Dr Leana Wen, an emergency physician, in a talk called "What your doctor won't disclose", given at TEDMED in September 2014. Her mother had stage IV breast cancer and was on her third round of chemotherapy. When her mother went online to look up her oncologist's phone number, she found him listed on several websites as a paid speaker for a drug company, often promoting the same chemotherapy regimen he had prescribed her. Her mother called her daughter in a panic. Wen, a doctor herself, did not know whether the regimen was right for her mother or not.

Wen does not name the oncologist and does not say that he did anything wrong. Her point is narrower and harder to answer: the patient found out by accident, and nobody had told her.

What the record says now. As I understand current US practice, drug and device makers must report certain payments to physicians and other covered recipients, and CMS publishes them in a public database called Open Payments. It covers reportable transactions, not necessarily every financial relationship. It is reporting by the payer. It is not a rule that a doctor must tell the patient in the consulting room, so a patient who does not look it up may never learn of it.

The evidence on whether such payments change treatment is mixed. A retrospective study of Medicare fee-for-service patients aged 66 or older (diagnosed 2014 to 2020; 15,607 patients and 8,419 oncologists), published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology on 15 September 2026, linked Open Payments records for the year before diagnosis to treatment choice. As the abstract reports, payments for a guideline-preferred drug were associated with a slightly higher chance of receiving a preferred treatment (adjusted risk ratio 1.06, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.13). Payments for a non-preferred drug were associated with a slightly lower chance of receiving a guideline-preferred treatment (adjusted risk ratio 0.93, 95% CI 0.89 to 0.98). As the study reports it, payments were associated with more use of the promoted drug, whether or not guidelines preferred it, though the differences were small. An association of this kind does not show that the payments caused the treatment choices.

That is an observational association in claims data, a commentator in the coverage called its size minimal, and it is not proof about any one doctor. It does not settle the question of trust that Wen raises: patients can only weigh a conflict of interest if they know about it.

This is the oath's limit in one case. A promise to put the patient first does not tell the patient who else is paying the doctor.

5. A cautionary tale: the patient as part of the checking

Hypothetical, not a report about any real person. Wen's story shows a patient finding a conflict by accident. The question it leaves open is what a patient can do before the accident. Imagine someone given a serious diagnosis by a confident specialist who is in a hurry. She does not ask about alternatives, the strength of the evidence, or whether anyone else would recommend the same thing, because the explanation was short and she was frightened. Nothing here says the advice was wrong, and a payment is not proof of bad advice. The point is that she had only the oath and his word, and nothing she could check.

What the patient can do. None of this is medical advice, and it is not a reason to refuse treatment on your own. It is a way to take part in the checking:

  • Consider a second opinion, and a third if the first two differ. Seeking another opinion is generally a legitimate part of informed decision-making. If the answer is the same from an independent source, that is real evidence. If it differs, you have learned something that matters. Ask your clinician how long you can safely take to decide.
  • Ask what the alternatives are, and what the evidence is for each. Ask what the risks of waiting are and what the treatment can and cannot do. Do not delay urgent treatment without medical guidance.
  • Ask about conflicts directly, and look them up. In the United States, payments from drug and device makers to physicians are published in the Open Payments database. Searching it may be difficult or inconclusive. Treat a payment as a reason to ask more questions, not as a verdict.
  • Check the recommendation against published guidance for your condition, for example the patient versions of professional guidelines, and bring any difference back to the clinician as a question.
  • Write things down and bring another person. People forget most of what they are told in the worst hour of their year.
  • Use AI tools to prepare, not to decide. They can translate a term, list questions to ask, and explain what a test measures. They can also be wrong, so anything that matters goes back to a clinician and a second source. Be careful about pasting identifying medical records into a general-purpose tool.

Why do professionals communicate so badly, when the tools exist? It is tempting to conclude that it must be deliberate, that if clinicians do not use the whole apparatus of the internet and AI to explain things, there has to be a reason. Sometimes the reason is financial, and that is why the disclosure question is worth asking. But there are ordinary explanations too, and a patient who assumes bad faith will ask worse questions than one who assumes a harried person with incentives she cannot see. Appointments are short, and documentation can take more time than the conversation. Liability makes clinicians cautious about what they promise, and privacy rules limit what patient information can go into consumer AI tools. Uncertainty is hard to communicate, and some people use a confident tone to cover it. None of this excuses a poor explanation. It means the patient is better served by asking for what she needs than by guessing at motives.

That is where the oath, ethics and righteousness meet again. The oath says the clinician will put the patient first. Ethics asks what the patient has a right to know. Righteousness is the clinician who volunteers the conflict, explains the alternatives and welcomes the second opinion, without being asked.

6. How I try to apply this to my own work

I do independent research, and a reader has no reason to trust me because I say I am careful. So I make the record do the work:

  • AI use is stated. Where AI tools did the reading or checking, the page says so, and says that no second person re-ran the checks.
  • Limits come first. Each work sample states what was not checked, not only what was.
  • Corrections are dated, with their status stated. The three items below have different standing, and I keep them apart. (1) Published: on 19 September 2026 I withdrew a claim in one of my own chapters, that six systems were related by explicit morphisms in one category, after a test I had not previously run showed no two of them were similar (that is, no pair satisfied the mathematical test of similarity used in that chapter). (2) Checked in a Lean file, reported not independently rebuilt: Lean is a proof-checking program that verifies whether a mathematical argument follows from its stated definitions, assumptions and accepted axioms. A file dated 25 September proves that non-similarity from the chapter's own model; its axiom report lists only the three standard axioms, and I have not seen it rebuilt by anyone else. (3) Draft, not published: in November I drafted an erratum marking the "verified" labels on a related bundle as superseded pending review.
  • Labels mean one thing each. "Formally checked", "mathematically established" and "empirically validated" are kept separate, because a proof checker cannot validate an experiment.

None of this makes me right. It makes my errors findable, which is what the oath cannot do.

7. What I am not saying

  • That oaths are empty. They state a purpose worth stating, and many people keep them.
  • That any doctor named here acted from any particular motive.
  • That review boards and disclosure rules are enough on their own. They fail too, and they too need people who will speak up.

8. Where this leaves the argument

An oath states a commitment. Ethics gives us reasons to examine and debate. Righteous conduct means acting on those reasons, even when doing so is difficult or costly. Accountability allows others to examine our actions, question our decisions, and identify mistakes.

Professional promises matter, but they cannot establish trust on their own. Trust becomes more defensible when promises are supported by responsible conduct, when mistakes can be acknowledged and corrected, and when patients have the information they need to participate in decisions about their care.
