A Proposal With a Test That Can Fail

One decision rule from a research proposal, written down before any data, and made runnable so it can be checked.
What this is

A work sample from a research proposal. The proposal is about whether one pore-size law explains why two zeolites, HZSM-5 and HMCM-22, give different products from ethanol. This page does not contain the proposal. It shows how the proposal’s test is defined, and runs it on invented numbers. It is not peer-reviewed, I do not hold a PhD, and no real data are shown here.

The claim, in plain words

The claim is that the product selectivity σ of a zeolite with pore radius r follows σ = 1 − (J/λ)(a/r)², where a, J and λ are the same for both materials. Rearranged, (1 − σ)r² = a²J/λ, a quantity that must come out equal for both zeolites. So the test needs no fitted lattice spacing. It needs one ratio.

The rule, runnable

Call the ratio R = (1 − σZ) rZ² / [(1 − σM) rM²]. The law predicts R = 1. The rule passes if |R − 1| ≤ tol, where the tolerance is set from replicate-run repeatability before the confirmatory runs. A blank tolerance asks for one, and a σ outside 0 to 1, or a radius that is not positive, is invalid. The numbers below are invented for illustration.

How I work, from this project’s own record

The rule above was written the way everything in this series is: by hand first, then as code, then run on a damaged copy to confirm the check can fail. This chapter has its own verifier, book26/ch05-verify.py. It ran failing with this page absent, then passing, and it rejects five damaged copies, including one with the decision rule reversed.

The reason for that habit is a record of things that went wrong, all in the public audit log: formal theorems that compiled but proved something other than their names claimed, an inverted scaling law in a published prediction, a backwards sign in a selectivity claim, a sign error in a numerical stepper, and a prediction (a “helical phase”) that was cut because its premise was false. A tool that proves what it says is only useful if the failures are written down next to it.

What this page does not show

Not established here
  • That the law is true. A first, exploratory look at real data, made before the definitions were fixed, did not support a single shared radius, and it is not a test. That is why the proposal asks for new runs.
  • Any real measurement. The collaborator’s tables are not reproduced here, pending her permission. The numbers on this page are invented.
  • That the proposal will be funded, or that the experimental collaborators have committed in writing.
  • A PhD or peer review. The work is public and audited, not peer-reviewed.
