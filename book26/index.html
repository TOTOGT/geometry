The 2026 Applications

One year of job applications, listed by industry. No employer is named.
This book is a list. It records what was prepared and sent in 2026 so that the year is on the page and not only in memory. It makes no claim about why any application did or did not succeed.

Chapters

  • Chapter 1. Two Conversations · what a year of applications produced so far, stated as counts
  • Chapter 2. FP&A for HVAC · an FP&A work sample, second in a series of one industry at a time; sourced industry figures and a calculator that starts empty
  • Chapter 3. The Computer Room · a cooling failure at a bank branch, told from the customer’s side; an account, not a finding
  • Chapter 4. A Policy Brief on the Response Gap · a credit-governance working paper read as a policy analyst would: four proposed controls, each with a test, and one run
  • Chapter 5. A Proposal With a Test That Can Fail · one decision rule from a research proposal, written before any data and made runnable, with the project’s record of what failed
  • Chapter 6. What a Collaborator Found · what a catalysis researcher studied, what one exploratory pass over her data showed, and what her review changed
  • Chapter 7. Two Postings, One Employer? · two recruiting-coordinator postings read side by side: what they share, where they differ, and why the record cannot yet call them one role
  • Chapter 8. A Preprint Against a Kernel · a 247-page theory that cites five Lean-verified theorems: what the five say, a checklist of what is checked and what is not, and what a full check would cost
  • Chapter 9. A Licence Before a Student · what New Jersey requires before a one-person company may sell instruction: the statutory test, the three exclusions, and the fees payable before a first student exists

What this record is

Each row is one application, or one set of application materials, grouped by the industry of the employer and the type of role. Employers are left out on purpose. The point of the record is the shape of a search: how wide it was, how the roles moved between fields, and how many there were. Naming a company would add risk for the people who work there and nothing to that picture.

What the first draft is built from

This draft was assembled from the file names of the resumes and cover letters saved during the year, not from submission receipts. Three consequences follow. A row means materials were prepared, not that they were sent. The month is the month the file was saved, not the date of submission. And applications made with one click on a job board leave no file at all, so they are missing. Industry labels are inferred from the file names and need to be confirmed before this page is relied on.

What this record does not show

  • It does not show how many applications were rejected, ignored or still open. Outcomes are recorded only where the employer has written back: one outcome is recorded so far, a form letter saying the application would not progress, and a blank is not a rejection.
  • It leaves out applications made through job boards with a single click, so the true total is higher than the number of rows.
  • It does not explain any outcome. A search of this width, across this many fields, in this labor market, cannot be read as a judgment on the person applying.

How it gets completed

Each row needs three confirmed facts: whether the application was sent, the date it was sent, and what happened. Until those are filled in, the table above stays marked as unconfirmed. Applications from 2027, if the search continues, go in Book XXVII on the same terms.
