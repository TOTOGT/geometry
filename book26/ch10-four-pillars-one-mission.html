Four Pillars, One Mission

A vision for the New Jersey Institute for Social Justice, written for its search for a President & Chief Executive Officer.

An exception to this book’s rule

This chapter is part of Book XXVI, 2026, the author’s published record of his applications. The other chapters leave employers unnamed. This one names the Institute and its search firm, because it is written for a live application and its point is the Institute’s own work. Everything about the Institute is taken from its position profile and its public pages, and is described in its own terms.

IntroductionOne mission, four pillars

The Institute’s mission is to empower people of color by building reparative systems that “create wealth, transform justice and harness democratic power” from the ground up in New Jersey.

The position profile lists four program areas: economic justice, criminal justice reform, democracy & justice, and environmental justice. One way to read the mission is that each phrase names a pillar, and that the fourth pillar is the ground the others stand on.

PillarMission phraseThe question it answers
Economic Justicecreate wealthWho gets to build wealth in New Jersey, and what repairs the gap?
Criminal Justice Reformtransform justiceWhat does public safety look like when it is built on investment, not punishment?
Democracy & Justiceharness democratic powerWho holds power, and is the ballot equally open to everyone?
Environmental Justicefrom the ground upWho lives next to the harm, and who decides what gets built there?

Reparations runs through all four. The Institute’s 2026–2027 agenda includes confronting New Jersey’s history of slavery and systemic racism through reparations, and the New Jersey Reparations Council’s Blueprint for Repair is part of its record.

The chapters share an operating reality. The Institute runs on an annual budget of about $6.1 million, is roughly 90% funded by institutions and foundations, and has never solicited or accepted government funding, which protects its independence as an advocacy organization. The next leader arrives at the start of a strategic planning process, with a charge to deepen the Institute’s presence in Central and South New Jersey and to make its work replicable in other states. A small organization with four pillars has to choose where its research, law and community work add something no one else supplies.

Each pillar below covers where the Institute stands, how the pillar serves the mission, a first-two-years agenda, how to tell whether it is working, and the strongest case against the Institute’s position, so the leader is ready for it.

Where the Institute stands

The Institute’s research on the racial wealth gap is its signature work. It says the roughly $300,000 gap it measured earlier has since doubled, to about $640,000, and it identifies homeownership as the main driver. The portfolio runs from Black Homeownership Matters and the baby bonds campaign to testimony on housing, student debt and college access. The position profile lists passage of the Fair Price Protection Act among its recent successes.

Why it is the mission

“Create wealth” is the mission’s first verb, and “reparative” has its most concrete meaning here: a gap built by redlining and exclusion from credit has to be closed by deliberate investment, not left to markets.

A first-two-years agenda

  • Make the wealth research the spine of the strategic plan. Turn the statewide numbers into county-level scorecards, built with partners in Central and South New Jersey, so the story is no longer told mainly from Newark.
  • Finish the baby bonds campaign and pair it with first-generation homebuyer and fair-lending measures, so that a child’s account and a family’s access to a mortgage point the same way.
  • Package the model for replication: a short toolkit that another state’s advocates could use to produce their own wealth-gap analysis and baby bonds design. That serves the national-profile goal and is a natural basis for multi-year national funding.
  • Keep the independence rule intact. The research is credible partly because the Institute takes no government money, so partnerships with lenders, community development institutions and public agencies should be framed as information-sharing, not funding.

How to tell it is working

The Black–white homeownership gap, enactment and funding of baby bonds, the number of counties with active local partners, and the number of states using the Institute’s methods.

The strongest opposing case

Critics argue that race-conscious wealth programs face legal vulnerability, that universal programs reach more people at lower cost, and that baby bonds are a large long-term fiscal commitment. The leader should arrive with program designs that are durable in court and in budget negotiations, and with evidence for why targeting is justified by the size of the gap.

Pillar 2 · Transform justiceCriminal Justice Reform

Real public safety comes from real community investment.

Where the Institute stands

The Institute’s youth justice work starts from that disparity. It campaigns to close the state’s three youth prisons, and From Shackles to Scholarship proposes a different future for the Johnstone Campus in Bordentown, which houses the state’s only youth prison for girls. On policing, its report on the Newark consent decree is titled A Solid Foundation For a Work in Progress; the federal court ended that decree in November 2025, after nine years, so the report now describes the first months after oversight rather than during it. Its site also lists a sign-on letter asking the U.S. Department of Justice to investigate the Paterson Police Department, and testimony in support of a minimum age of 14 for juvenile delinquency. The position profile adds protecting youth due process rights and supporting investment in community-based public safety options.

Why it is the mission

“Transform justice” asks for more than reform at the margins. The Institute’s claim is that safety comes from investment in communities, so the pillar is about building the alternative while dismantling the old system.

A first-two-years agenda

  • Define the end state for youth facilities in concrete terms: how many beds close, on what schedule, and what community-based capacity replaces them in which counties. A closure campaign without a landing place invites the objection that it leaves young people and neighborhoods without options.
  • Use Newark as the test of whether reform outlasts oversight. The decree has ended, so the question is no longer compliance but whether stops, searches and use of force stay where the decree put them. Publish measurable indicators on a fixed schedule, then carry the method to Paterson and other departments, which is also a route into the Institute’s Central and North Jersey expansion.
  • Build public-safety credibility. Invest in evidence on community-based safety options, and work with victims’ advocates and local officials wherever interests overlap, so legislators are not forced into a binary choice.
  • Integrate litigation and policy. The law and policy director’s team should decide, case by case, when a legal action advances the legislative agenda and when it risks hardening opposition.

How to tell it is working

The number and racial composition of youth in secure custody, dollars reinvested and where they went, whether the reforms Newark made under its decree hold after oversight ended, and legislation enacted.

The strongest opposing case

Opponents argue that secure facilities protect the public and give structure to youth who have committed serious offenses, that raising the minimum age or limiting detention can leave serious harm unaddressed, that police unions and some community members see oversight as a barrier to effective policing, and that consent decrees are costly. The Newark decree ended with the Justice Department supporting its termination, which critics of the Institute can cite as proof that the reforms worked. The leader should engage these concerns with data on outcomes and costs, and acknowledge victims’ interests directly.

Where the Institute stands

The record also includes online and automatic voter registration and lowering the voting age to 16 in Newark school board elections. On 2 July 2026 Governor Mikie Sherrill signed the John R. Lewis Voter Empowerment Act, listed by the Governor’s office as ACS for A-1715 / SCS for S-282. The Governor’s release says New Jersey is the first state to enact voting rights reform following the U.S. Supreme Court’s decision in Louisiana v. Callais; the count of New Jersey as the tenth state to pass its own voting rights act is the Institute’s. Unfinished items include same-day voter registration and full funding for the 2030 Census, to support an accurate count and fair redistricting. The Institute’s site also lists a motion to intervene in a federal voter-roll lawsuit against New Jersey, a legal brief on ending the county line on ballots, and a sign-on letter against the SAVE Act.

Why it is the mission

“Harness democratic power” is the pillar’s description of itself. Wealth and justice policy are only durable if the communities affected hold the power to defend them.

A first-two-years agenda

  • Move from passage to implementation. A voting rights act protects no one until it is used: language access, local election structures and redistricting practices have to be monitored and, where needed, enforced. That work has to happen in Central and South New Jersey jurisdictions where the Institute has had the least presence.
  • Finish the unfinished list: same-day registration and funding for the 2030 Census.
  • Keep the legal posture disciplined. Federal litigation raises the Institute’s profile and its exposure, so the board and leader should agree in advance on selection criteria, protect the organization’s nonpartisan and independent identity, and respect the rules that govern a charitable advocacy organization in an election year.
  • Link power to the other pillars. The people who vote on baby bonds, youth prisons and data center siting are the same constituents, so voter engagement should be planned with the other three pillars.

How to tell it is working

Registration and turnout gaps in the jurisdictions where the Voter Empowerment Act applies, the number of local governments engaged, litigation outcomes, and the pace of the remaining legislative items.

The strongest opposing case

Opponents argue that voter-roll maintenance, identification rules and earlier registration deadlines protect election integrity and give election officials time to administer a clean election, and that expanded access without safeguards risks public confidence. Others dispute how far state voting rights acts and federal intervention should reach. The leader should be able to explain what the Institute’s proposals do for administrability and integrity, not only for access.

Pillar 4 · From the ground upEnvironmental Justice

The ground beneath the rest.

60+organizations in a coalition letter of 13 May 2026 asking the Governor for a moratorium on new data centers of 20 megawatts or more
4parts to the Governor’s data center plan, announced 27 May 2026
2data center laws signed since: 7 July 2026 (rate class and tariffs) and 27 August 2026 (self-reported energy and water use)

Where the Institute stands

This is the Institute’s newest area of work. The position profile describes it as fighting for vulnerable communities, primarily communities of color, that are disproportionately and repeatedly exposed to environmental harm from pollution, climate impacts, infrastructure and public works projects, and proximity to toxic facilities, including large-scale data centers. The setting has moved quickly. The Governor’s office stated a plan in four parts on 27 May 2026: fair-share rules so that data centers bring new clean energy online; transparency, starting with reporting on energy and water use; statewide standards for Community Benefits Agreements; and good-paying jobs, by having these centers use local trades and pay prevailing wages. Two bills have since been signed. S-731/A-796, signed on 7 July 2026, places large-load data centers in their own rate class with tariffs so they cover their own costs. S3379, signed on 27 August 2026, requires data center owners to report energy and water use to the Board of Public Utilities every six months for three years. Critics, among them the Pinelands Alliance, say self-reporting without independent verification or limits on use comes after the harm and call for a moratorium. The coalition letter cites electricity costs, noise and environmental effects. New Jersey already has established environmental justice organizations, including the New Jersey Environmental Justice Alliance and the Sierra Club’s New Jersey chapter.

Why it is the mission

The mission speaks of repairing the “cracks of structural racism in our foundation” that erupt into earthquakes in communities of color. Air, water, land and infrastructure are literal foundations. Environmental harm also feeds the other pillars: utility costs and flood risk reduce household wealth, siting decisions are made by processes that communities of color have often been left out of, and enforcement falls on the same neighborhoods that bear the burden.

A first-two-years agenda

  • Choose the niche before choosing the campaigns. With a $6.1 million budget, a new pillar cannot try to cover all of environmental policy. The Institute’s distinct assets are a racial equity analysis, legal capacity and research on wealth, so the best fit is likely siting and cumulative-impact decisions about large facilities, including data centers, and infrastructure and public works projects. This should be tested with the board and staff.
  • Take the gap the new law leaves. The reporting law has no independent verification, and its critics say so. Checking the reported figures against the communities that live beside the facilities is research and legal analysis of the kind the Institute already does, and nobody else may be positioned to do it.
  • Partner instead of duplicating. Work under the established environmental organizations’ leadership on technical environmental questions and bring the legal and racial equity analysis they may lack.
  • Fund the pillar on its own terms. Seek dedicated multi-year funding so it does not draw down the existing pillars, and set a deadline for judging whether it has found its place.
  • Use the pillar to connect the others: household cost and property value effects for Economic Justice, participation in siting decisions for Democracy & Justice, and enforcement burden for Criminal Justice Reform.

How to tell it is working

The number of siting or permitting proceedings where the Institute’s analysis is in the record, community partners engaged, dedicated funding secured, and enforceable commitments won.

The strongest opposing case

Supporters of data center development point to tax revenue, jobs, grid investment and competitiveness in the technology economy, and argue that heavy regulation or pauses push investment elsewhere. Some also argue that environmental review can delay public infrastructure that communities need. The leader should show that the Institute is asking for fair siting, transparency and cost accountability, not opposing development as such.

ConclusionThe architecture across the pillars

Each pillar follows the same sequence: research that quantifies the harm, law and policy that change the rules, community engagement that keeps the work grounded, and coalitions that carry it across the state and nation. The next leader’s job is to protect that sequence while the organization grows.

In the first year that means listening to staff, board, partners and funders; leading the strategic plan with explicit choices about where the Institute leads and where it supports others; building the Central and South New Jersey presence through partners before offices; and raising multi-year funding without compromising the independence that gives the Institute its credibility.

Reparations is the thread that ties the pillars into one argument: that New Jersey’s inequities were built by policy and can be repaired by policy.

Corrected after checking: the Institute’s page prints homeownership of 38.4% for Black and 75.9% for white households without a survey year; the 2024 Census tables give 42.6% and 76.7%, a gap of 34.1 points, not 37.5. Not found in any State source, and so the Institute’s or the profile’s claim only: median wealth of $662,500 and $20,000 (the State does not publish wealth by race; the April 2025 release says “less than $20,000” and a gap of about $640,000); $132,000 home equity; 59% of people denied the vote; 80,000 restored voting rights; 350,000 restored to jury service; the $10 million reinvestment; the coalition letter of 60 or more groups (news reports only).
