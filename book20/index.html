Reading Data

Book XIX asks what a proof checker entitles you to say. This volume asks the same question of data: what a pattern, an average or a trend actually licenses, and the three ways a careful person gets it wrong — counting only the survivors, trying many things and reporting the one that worked, and mistaking a return to the mean for an effect.

This corpus has a kernel for its proofs and nothing comparable for its patterns. A theorem that assumes its conclusion still kernel-checks (R20); a numerical coincidence found after a wide search still looks like a discovery. This volume is the instrument for the second case, and it is turned on the series before it is turned on anyone else.

1 · Why this is its own volume

The placement map says a piece of mathematics goes where its role is, and that XVI–XX stay empty until material arrives that fits nowhere else. This material has arrived, and it fits nowhere else in its register. Book XVII holds probability as a machine defines it — a variable with no second moment has variance zero, as a theorem (variance_of_not_memLp). Book XIII holds what a proof check establishes. Neither holds what a person may conclude from data. That is this volume, named after its material rather than the reverse.

Its shape is borrowed, and the debt is stated: Jordan Ellenberg's How Not to Be Wrong (Penguin, 2014) organises this ground into Linearity, Inference, Expectation, Regression and Existence. Those five parts are placed across the series, not all here:

Ellenberg is a guide to the ground, not an authority on it. Nothing on these pages is cited to him that a primary text can carry, and no passage of his is reproduced.

4 · What is not on this desk

Two founding papers are missing, and the volume says so before it starts

Wald, 1943 — the Statistical Research Group memoranda on estimating aircraft vulnerability from the damage on planes that returned. Chapter 1's argument is Wald's, and it needs Wald, not a retelling.

Galton, 1886 — “Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature.” The held statistics text treats regression as a model (§10.3) and never names the phenomenon: the script searches it for Galton, for “toward the mean”, “regression effect” and “mediocrity”, and finds none of them. Chapter 4 cannot be written from it.

Also wanted, for the chapters placed in Book X: Condorcet's 1785 Essai and Arrow's Social Choice and Individual Values (1951). None of the four was found by filename or by the ledger's first lines — which is a statement about that search, not proof of absence.

Chapters 2 and 3 can be written now, and chapter 5's method. Chapters 1 and 4 cannot until their founding papers have addresses. [OPEN]

5 · What this volume will not claim

  • New statistics. Everything here is textbook, and the textbook is named with its page.
  • That a coincidence in this corpus is meaningful, or meaningless, before its script has counted the search that found it. Chapter 2 audits; it does not pre-judge, in either direction.
  • That the hurricane record shows a trend, or shows none. The file is held; the history of how it was observed is not.
  • Anything on Ellenberg's authority. His book organised the ground; the claims rest on the texts in §2.
