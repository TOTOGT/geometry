Marguerite Porete

The Book That Outlived Its Author by Six Hundred Years

K · Seven States F · Anéantissement U · The Return

On 1 June 1310, in the Place de Grève in Paris, a woman was burned alive for a book. She had written it, and when it was condemned and burned in front of her some years earlier, she had gone on lending it to people. That was the offence. Not the doctrine — the persistence.

Her name was Marguerite Porete. The book was Le Mirouer des simples âmes anienties, the mirror of simple, annihilated souls. It was burned with its author's name attached to it, and then it went on being copied, translated, glossed, bound, recommended, and read devoutly in monasteries for the next six hundred and thirty-six years — with no author's name on it at all. In 1927 an English edition was printed with the byline By an Unknown French Mystic of the Thirteenth Century. In 1946 a scholar in Rome, reading Vatican manuscripts, worked out who the unknown mystic was.

This chapter belongs to Movement III because Porete is the hardest kind of witness: one who was silenced completely and won anyway, and who never knew it. The pattern of that win is worth understanding precisely, because it is not a consoling story about truth rising. It is a story about what a ban can and cannot reach.

The ban

DATA Sometime between 1296 and 1306, Guy of Cambrai — the bishop, Gui de Collemedio — had the Mirouer publicly burned at Valenciennes, in Porete's presence, and forbade anyone to hold or circulate it on pain of excommunication. This is the first fact and it is often skipped: the book was destroyed once and its author walked away from the fire alive. The ban was of the ordinary kind. It was meant to end the matter.

She kept circulating it. She sent it to churchmen. And she had, either before or after, obtained the approval of readers whose judgment carried weight — the book itself claims three, and one of them can be named from the manuscripts and from the scholarship: Godfrey of Fontaines, a regent master of theology at Paris, one of the most respected scholastics of his generation. A master of the same faculty that would later condemn her praised the book.

The shape of the offence

What made her a relapsed heretic was not that she held forbidden opinions. It was that she had been told to stop and did not stop. Under the procedure of the time this distinction is decisive: a first condemnation is a warning, and relapse is what carries the fire. She was killed by the second act, not the first.

Every writer who has ever been told that a piece of work is finished, withdrawn, or not to be spoken of again should notice how mild the original ban was, and how completely it was survivable.

The silence

DATA In 1308 she was arrested and brought before William of Paris — William Humbert, a Dominican, Inquisitor of France. She refused to take the oath the inquisitor required. She refused it again when summoned again. She was excommunicated and remained excommunicate for roughly a year and a half, and through all of it, according to the trial documents, she would not speak to her judges. Absolution was offered repeatedly on the condition that she cooperate. She never did.

Her silence was classified as contumacy and rebellion and the trial proceeded without her. This is the detail that gives the case its peculiar structure: the book was tried, and the author declined to attend. She would neither defend the text nor disown it. She let it speak alone.

A man named Guiard de Cressonessart was arrested at about the same time for coming to her defence, and was tried alongside her. He talked. He confessed, submitted, and was sentenced to life imprisonment. He is the control case, and he is worth keeping in view: submission bought him his life and cost him his voice, and almost nobody has heard of him.

The extract

DATA A commission of twenty-one theologians of the University of Paris was given fifteen propositions drawn from the book. Not the book. Fifteen sentences taken out of it. Of those fifteen, only three are securely identifiable today; two of them read:

That the annihilated soul gives licence to the virtues and is no more in their servitude.

— Article extracted from the Mirouer, Paris consultation, 1310

That such a soul does not care about the consolations of God nor does it care for His gifts.

— Article extracted from the Mirouer, Paris consultation, 1310

Read cold, on a page by themselves, these are alarming. A soul released from the virtues is a soul released from restraint; a soul indifferent to God's gifts is a soul indifferent to God. Twenty-one masters read them cold, on a page by themselves, and the verdict followed.

The Mirouer is not a treatise. It is a dialogue, and its principal speakers are Love, the Soul, and Reason — and Reason is a character who is corrected again and again by Love, who fails to follow what is being said, and who at length dies. The book kills Reason off partway through, as a literary act, to mark the point past which its argument no longer answers to Reason's questions. Sentences addressed to a character who is about to be shown inadequate do not carry the same force as sentences asserted flatly by an author. The soul that has "given licence to the virtues" is, in the book's own account, one that has passed through them so completely that it no longer needs to be commanded to do what it now does freely. Whether that is orthodox is a real question, argued seriously for seven hundred years. It is not the question the fifteen extracts asked.

Extraction changes truth value — and it cuts both ways

A proposition lifted out of a structure is a different proposition. This is less a defence of Porete than an observation about reading: an extract carries its own apparent meaning, and that meaning is generated by the extraction as much as by the source. A sentence that reads cleanly alone may be doing quite different work in place, and the reader of the extract has no way to tell.

The asymmetry is worth stating, because one direction is far more dangerous than the other. Extraction usually makes a claim look better than it is, not worse — a grand name detached from what it names will pass almost any inspection, since there is nothing left to inspect. Porete's case is the rarer inversion: extraction made a defensible book look indefensible. It is instructive because the machinery is identical, and only the direction differs.

The fire, and what a chronicler saw

DATA Condemned as a relapsed heretic, Porete was handed to the secular arm and burned the day after sentencing, on 1 June 1310. The chronicle of Guillaume de Nangis — a source with no sympathy for her, which is what makes it worth citing — records that the crowd was moved to tears by the calmness with which she faced her death.

That is the whole of the eyewitness record of her person: a hostile chronicler, noting against his own argument that the composure of the woman he was condemning made the onlookers weep. Everything else we have of her is the book.

The survival

DATA Written in Old French, the Mirouer was translated into Latin, into Middle English, into Italian, into Middle French, and circulated in France, Italy, Germany, England and Bohemia. A fifteenth-century English translator known only by the initials M.N. not only rendered it but added glosses defending its more difficult passages for English readers. Richard Methley, a Carthusian of Mount Grace in Yorkshire, made a Latin version late in the same century. It was read in orthodox religious houses as a devotional classic. Nobody stopped it, because nobody knew what it was.

In 1927 Clare Kirchberger edited M.N.'s translation from four surviving manuscripts and published it. The title page read: The Mirror of Simple Souls. By an Unknown French Mystic of the Thirteenth Century.

DATA In 1946 Romana Guarnieri, working on Vatican Latin manuscripts, matched the anonymous devotional text against the propositions recorded in the 1310 trial and identified the unknown mystic as the woman burned in the Place de Grève. Six hundred and thirty-six years after the sentence, the book got its author back.

What the ban actually achieved
It destroyed the author and separated her from the text. The text was portable; the name was the liability. Anonymity was not the book's misfortune — it was its passport.

It is worth being exact about the mechanism, because the consoling version of this story is wrong. Truth did not rise. A book survived because the thing that had been condemned — a named woman, in a jurisdiction, subject to a procedure — was no longer attached to it. The Inquisition of France could reach Marguerite Porete. It could not reach an anonymous manuscript circulating among Carthusians in Yorkshire two hundred years later, because there was no longer anyone to summon.

There is an object that is the exact inverse of Porete's book, and it is worth holding the two side by side. The Mirouer was an artifact with no name — and it survived, and it was read, and it did its work. A name with no artifact behind it is the opposite thing: it travels easily, it is cited, it accumulates authority, and it does no work whatever, because there is nothing there to do the work. Of the two, only one is a real loss.

Seven states

DATA The Mirouer sets out seven states through which the soul passes on the way to annihilation and union. CONJECTURE This chapter records the count and places it beside the other rung-counts collected in the Ladder of Ascent, and it declines to make anything more of it than that.

Why the seven is recorded and not used

Seven-stage schemata are extremely common in medieval spiritual writing. A count of seven appearing in Porete is close to what one would expect from the base rate alone, and a correspondence that a fair coin would produce is not evidence. The honest statement is: here is one more rung-count, logged, with the note that on its own it carries almost no weight, and that anyone assembling the ladder series should be counting how many schemata they had to pass over to find the ones that fit.

No constant of the operator chain is fitted to this number, and none should be. The interesting structural fact in Porete is not the seven; it is the sixth.

MODEL The seventh state, in Porete's account, is not attainable in this life — it belongs to glory. The sixth is the one the book is actually about: the soul that has been annihilated and is still here, living in the world, acting without being commanded to act. The transformation is not the terminus. There is a return.

This mapping is offered as a lens and nothing more. It is not a claim that Porete was describing a dynamical system, and there is no measurement here that could come out one way rather than another. What it is good for is a single structural observation, which the book makes better than the framework does: the fold is not the destination. Whatever happens at the threshold, something walks away from it and goes on living. Porete's soul is annihilated in the sixth state and then does the dishes.

Eckhart, and what is not claimed

OPEN Meister Eckhart came to Paris shortly after Porete's execution, and the resemblance between passages of the Mirouer and Eckhart's teaching on detachment and the ground of the soul has generated a substantial scholarly literature. Some of it argues influence; some argues a shared vernacular-theological milieu; the question is not settled. This chapter asserts neither. It notes only that ideas indistinguishable from the ones she was burned for were, within two decades, being preached by a Dominican master — who was himself posthumously condemned in 1329, and who has a chair in the history of philosophy while she has a date and a square.

What she is a witness to

The book stands beside Primo Levi and Graciliano Ramos in this movement for a reason that is worth naming exactly. Porete does not testify to survival; she did not survive. She testifies to something narrower and stranger: that a person can decline to participate in the destruction of their own work, at the cost of their life, and that this refusal can be the operative variable.

Consider what compliance would have produced. She was offered absolution repeatedly. Recanting would have meant certifying that the book was wrong. A recanted book does not get copied by Carthusians; it gets filed as an error and forgotten. Guiard de Cressonessart complied, lived, and vanished from history. It is at least arguable — CONJECTURE, and it is the kind of counterfactual that no record can settle — that her silence is precisely what kept the text in circulation, because she never gave anyone an authoritative statement that it was false.

She was not silent because she had nothing to say. She had written a hundred and forty chapters of what she had to say, and she declined to add a syllable to them under duress. The book was her whole testimony and she let it stand alone in the room.

Nobody knows anything about the sixth state except by hearing it from the seventh, which is not given in this life.

— the argument of the Mirouer, as the trial documents did not record it

She was burned on a Monday, and the crowd wept, and the book walked out of Paris without her.
