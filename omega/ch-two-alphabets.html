Mystics · Antiquity · Letter and Number

Two Alphabets, One Number

Hebrew gematria and Greek isopsephy — two traditions in one sea that turned letters into numbers — and the Pythagoreans, for whom number was the substance of the world.

"Let him that hath understanding count the number of the beast … and his number is six hundred threescore and six." — Revelation 13:18
§ I · The Shared Idea

When a letter was also a count

Before there were separate symbols for numbers, a great many peoples used the letters they already had. In both ancient Greek and Hebrew the alphabet did double duty: aleph and alpha stood for one, and every letter carried a value fixed by its place in the row. Add the letters of a word and you had a number; the word and the sum became, in a sense, the same thing. The Greeks called this isopsephy — "equal-pebble," from the counting stones — and the Jews called it gematria. Two names, one habit of mind, in one Mediterranean world.

It is easy to read that habit as mere play, and often it was. But to the people who took it seriously it was something closer to a conviction: that a name has a hidden weight, that meaning and quantity are not strangers, and that the world might be counted as well as read.

The Pythagoreans went furthest. For them number was not a description laid over the world but the world's own substance: the ratios of a plucked string, the concords of music, the order of the heavens — all of it number, and number all of it. Around Pythagoras grew a school half-mathematical and half-devotional, with initiates and silences and a founder remembered as almost more than a man. That the mathematics was real and the reverence was extravagant are both true at once; the two are not always easy to pull apart, and this whole gallery lives in the seam between them.

§ III · The Beast, Counted

Nero as 666

The most famous number in the West is a gematria. When the Greek form of the emperor's name, Nerōn Kaisar, is written in Hebrew letters — nrwn qsr — the values sum to 666. The Hebrew spelling is exactly the one attested in the Talmud and among the Dead Sea community at Qumran, which is why the identification is more than a coincidence of arithmetic. And there is a tell in the manuscripts: some early copies of Revelation, from Latin-speaking regions, give the number as 616 — precisely the total when the shorter Latin form, Nero Caesar, is transliterated instead. The variant is not an error; it is the same calculation run on a slightly different name.

The practice was ordinary enough to end up as graffiti. Suetonius records that after Nero had his mother killed, wits scrawled a line playing on isopsephy — in Greek, "Nero" and "killed his own mother" carry the same value, 1005. A joke, a slander, a devotional key, an apocalyptic cipher: the same tool served all of them, in the same centuries, in the same streets.

A name has a number, and a number can be read back as a name. It is the oldest way of saying that nothing is only what it appears — and, like every powerful idea, it was used by the honest and the crooked alike.
§ IV · What the Chapter Keeps

A shared inheritance, told plainly

The point of putting gematria and isopsephy side by side is not to trace a secret line from one to the other, and certainly not to make either tradition the keeper of a hidden power. It is the opposite: two peoples, in the same sea, independently arrived at the same beautiful and dangerous idea — that letters are numbers and numbers carry meaning — and the Pythagoreans, a third room of the same house, pushed it to its cosmological limit.

Number-mysticism has always drawn saints and charlatans in equal measure, in every tradition that ever practised it. That is the honest note to end on, and it is the note this whole gallery is tuned to: there are good and bad people everywhere. The inheritance is the idea, not the people who happened to carry it; and the idea — that the world might be counted, and that a count might mean something — is one this book has spent its whole length trying to earn honestly.
