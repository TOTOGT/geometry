Movement III · The Case for Us
Levi · Ramos · dos Anjos

The Witnesses

Three men who saw the worst and went on testifying for the species

Not decoration. Proof of concept — that a person can carry the worst thing that can happen and still hand something forward.

The case for hope cannot be made by argument alone, because the objection to it is not an argument. The objection is a feeling, and the feeling is: you have not seen what I have seen. There is no premise that answers that. There are only people who did see, and who are on the record afterwards.

Three of them stand behind this book. Two were named in the working paper that became Vilomah; the third belongs beside them and has been in the argument from the start without being written down. What they share is not optimism. Not one of them was an optimist. What they share is that they refused to let the worst thing be the last word, and each refused in a different way, so between them they cover most of the ground a reader might need.

Primo Levi, and the Refusal to Simplify

Levi was a chemist. He was deported to Auschwitz in 1944 and survived, and the fact he always attributed his survival to — among luck, timing, and an illness that kept him behind when the camp was evacuated — was the chemistry: a laboratory assignment, indoors, in the last winter. The training that made him useful to the machine is the training that produced the prose afterwards.

And the prose is the point. Se questo è un uomo is not a cry. It is a report: measured, specific, almost clinical, written by a man who understood that the only testimony the future would be unable to dismiss was one that could not be accused of exaggeration. He used the register of the laboratory on a subject that had every reason to demand the register of the scream, because he had worked out which one would survive contact with a sceptical reader in fifty years.

Forty years on he wrote I sommersi e i salvati, and did the hardest thing in the entire literature of the camps: he complicated his own side. The grey zone — the region where victims were made into instruments, where the line between the persecuted and the functionaries of persecution blurs and cannot be redrawn cleanly — is his. He introduced it knowing exactly what could be done with it by people acting in bad faith, and he introduced it anyway, because it was true and because a testimony that flattered its own side would eventually be found out and would take everything else down with it.

He kept the seam visible. He is the reason this book knows that keeping the seam visible is not a stylistic preference but the condition of being believed.

He died in Turin in 1987, in a fall at his home; whether it was suicide has been argued ever since and this book does not need to settle it. What matters here is what he did with the forty-two years he had after the camp, which was to spend them making the record harder to deny.

Graciliano Ramos, and the Register That Does Not Falsify

Ramos wrote the driest Portuguese in Brazilian literature, and the dryness is a moral position. He was a provincial mayor before he was a novelist, and his annual reports to the state were so exact and so unadorned that they were read as literature by people who had no business reading municipal accounts. He never afterwards wrote in any other way.

Vidas Secas follows a family through the drought of the sertão, and it gives one chapter to the dog. Not sentimentally — the dog gets the same unsparing attention as the people, which is a far stranger and more difficult act of respect than affection would have been. There is no rescue in the book. There is no redemptive turn. There is exact attention to what the drought did to each of them and what they then did to each other, and the refusal to console is what makes the book trustworthy about the thing it describes.

In 1936 he was arrested under the Vargas government and held without charge. He wrote it down afterwards, in Memórias do Cárcere, published after his death, and he wrote it in the same register: no self-heroising, no accusation raised past what the facts carried, an account so plain that the reader arrives at the outrage themselves and cannot suspect they were led there.

He is the proof that coldness of style and warmth of purpose are compatible — that the writer who refuses to soften is not the writer who does not care, but very often the only one who can be believed about what he cares for.

Augusto dos Anjos, and the Vocabulary That Was Adequate

One book, Eu, in 1912. Dead in 1914, at thirty. He wrote with the vocabulary of the sciences — decomposition, cells, energy, the mechanics of matter coming apart — and was mocked for it by a literary culture that thought poetry had a permitted vocabulary and that this was not it.

He was not decorating verse with laboratory words. He had found the only language adequate to his subject, which was that everything decays, including the observer, and that the decay is physical and can be named precisely. The precision is the feeling. Strip the scientific vocabulary out of those poems and you do not get a purer grief; you get no grief at all, because the grief was in the exactness.

That is the whole method of this series, arrived at a century earlier by a man with one book and two years to live. The mathematics here is not ornament laid over a human argument, and the human argument is not a softening laid over the mathematics. Each is the only adequate language for its own domain, and the work is holding them in the same volume without letting either dissolve the other.

What Three Witnesses Prove, and What They Do Not

They do not prove that things turn out well. Levi’s Europe murdered his family and most of the people he was deported with. Ramos’s sertão is still dry and his prison still existed. Dos Anjos died at thirty and the decay he wrote about took him on schedule. [OPEN] Nothing in these three lives licenses an inference that the catastrophe in the last chapter resolves.

What they prove is narrower, and it is the load this movement has to carry: the capacity exists. A human being can absorb the maximum available dose of what the world can do — industrial murder, drought, imprisonment, the certainty of one’s own dissolution — and come out the other side still speaking, still exact, still handing something forward to people not yet born. That capacity is not theoretical. It is documented, three times, in three languages, under three different kinds of catastrophe.

Which is what makes it evidence rather than encouragement. [MODEL] If the open variable in every projection is human response, then the question is what that variable can do at its limit, and the honest way to find out is to look at people who were taken there. They were. They testified. The variable’s range includes this.

[FAITH] The step from the capacity exists to the species will use it is a bet, and it is stated here as one. No reading of three lives closes that gap, and this book does not pretend that it does. What the witnesses do is make the bet a reasonable one to place, which is all any evidence ever does for any bet.

And there is one more thing, which is the reason these three and not some braver list. Each of them, at the moment when despair would have been the fully rational response, did the same specific thing: they went back to work. Levi wrote a report. Ramos wrote a memoir. Dos Anjos wrote the poems. Not a gesture, not a statement — the next real piece of work, done well, in the only register adequate to it.

That is not a mood. It is a method, and it is the subject of the movement that follows.
