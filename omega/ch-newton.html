Isaac Newton

1643 – 1727 · Woolsthorpe, Cambridge, London

The mathematician of the Principia, and the author of a very large body of alchemical and theological writing that most readers never meet.

Status of this page. The manuscripts

Newton wrote at length on alchemy and on theology, not only on mathematics and physics. A large part of his papers was sold at Sotheby's in 1936, and the economist John Maynard Keynes bought many of them. Keynes's essay “Newton, the Man”, prepared for the tercentenary of Newton's birth and published in 1947, called him “the last of the magicians”. Transcriptions of many of the manuscripts are published by the Newton Project. Historians of science, among them B. J. T. Dobbs, have argued that Newton's alchemy was a serious inquiry into matter and not a hobby.

Is “mystic” the right word?

This gallery uses the word as a category for mathematicians whose work sat beside religious or esoteric practice. For Newton the word is contested: some historians describe his alchemical and prophetic writing as part of one natural-philosophical programme with his optics and mechanics, and the label “mystic” is the series' own, not a settled description of him.

Theology

Historians generally agree that Newton rejected the doctrine of the Trinity and kept that view private (Richard Westfall's biography Never at Rest, 1980, is the standard account). In the General Scholium added to the second edition of the Principia (1713), Newton argues that the order of the planetary system points to a designing and ruling God.

The series, and a number you can check

Like Madhava's school (see Madhava of Sangamagrama), Newton, Gregory and Leibniz worked independently on infinite series for the circle in the seventeenth century. One of Newton's methods is the series for the inverse sine: $$\arcsin x = x + \frac{1}{2}\frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{1\cdot 3}{2\cdot 4}\frac{x^5}{5} + \frac{1\cdot 3\cdot 5}{2\cdot 4\cdot 6}\frac{x^7}{7} + \cdots$$ Since $\arcsin(1/2) = \pi/6$, setting $x = 1/2$ and multiplying by 6 gives $\pi$. This series is not the alternating series $\pi/4 = 1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - \cdots$: its terms shrink by about a factor of four each step, so it converges geometrically, where the alternating series converges only like $1/n$. Newton is reported in the literature to have computed $\pi$ to about fifteen decimal places by a method of this kind.

Checked here. With 60-digit arithmetic, six times the sum of the first 22 terms of the arcsine series at $x = 1/2$ agrees with $\pi$ to within $10^{-15}$, and the error shrinks by a factor close to 4 per term (4.1 at term 41). Four times the alternating series needs about $10^{15}$ terms for the same accuracy, because after $n$ terms its error is about $1/n$ (within 10% from $n = 100$ on). omega/ch-newton-verify.py computes $\pi$ by Machin's formula, not from a library constant. That Newton did this computation, and its year and digit count, are reported by historians and are not checked here.
