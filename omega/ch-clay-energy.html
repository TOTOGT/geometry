The tablets are already digitized.
Almost no one has read them.

two fragments, photographed decades apart — a join, waiting to be noticed

An estimated half a million cuneiform tablets survive in museum and university collections around the world — administrative records, omens, letters, myths, grain loans, school exercises. Most have been photographed and catalogued in the last twenty-five years. Far fewer have been fully transliterated, joined to their missing fragments, or read by anyone since the scribe who wrote them. That gap is the project. Here Comes Everybody, for the oldest writing there is.

Why this works now

Three things converged that weren't true a generation ago, and together they turn a closed specialist backlog into open work.

Digitized

The images already exist

Major collections — the British Museum, the Vorderasiatisches Museum, Penn, the Hermitage and more — have been photographed and catalogued online over the past two decades, largely through the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative.

Joinable

Fragments can be matched from home

A large share of tablets survive only as broken pieces. 3D viewers now let anyone rotate, compare, and attempt a physical join between fragments online — the same act a curator does in a storeroom, done in a browser.

Machine-assisted

First-pass reading is faster

Computer-vision and translation models trained on annotated tablets (UChicago's DeepScribe is one example) now offer rough first drafts of sign identification and translation — a triage tool, not a replacement for the philologist who checks it.

The toolkit — real, open, live today

CDLI

Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative. The central catalogue — search by museum, period, or text type, and see the actual photograph alongside any existing transliteration.

cdli.earth →

ORACC

Open Richly Annotated Cuneiform Corpus. Dozens of curated sub-projects with full annotation — royal inscriptions, letters, lexical lists — each maintained by a specialist.

oracc.museum.upenn.edu →

Electronic Babylonian Library

The Fragmentarium — transliterated Babylonian fragments with a public API and an active algorithmic join effort that has already recovered new lines of Gilgamesh.

ebl.lmu.de →

ETCSL

Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature. Nearly 400 literary compositions in transliteration and English translation. Funding ended in 2006; the archive remains fully online and citable.

etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk →
The one rule

Read what's there, not what you hope is there. A join, a transliteration, a corrected sign reading — these are claims about the world, and they face a real filter: an Assyriologist can tell you if you're wrong, and being correctable is the whole point. The moment a recovered text gets read as secretly encoding something it doesn't say — confirming a theory, hiding a message — the work stops being archaeology and starts being wishful reading. The Sumerians mostly wrote about grain, gods, kings, and floods. Recovering what they actually said is the gift. That's the entire standard this project holds itself to.

This is also why the credit is real: a claim that survives expert review is worth something precisely because it could have failed to.

How to actually start

Pick one small corpus

Not "cuneiform" — one genre, one collection, one period. Sumerian proverbs on ETCSL. Neo-Assyrian letters on ORACC. One shelf, not the library.

Learn to see a sign, not read a language

You don't need fluent Akkadian to start. Learn to recognize wedge groups and tablet layout — enough to compare two images and notice whether they share a hand, a ductus, a break line.

Attempt one real thing

One join attempt. One flagged sign that looks misread. One metadata gap filled in. Small and checkable beats broad and speculative, every time.

Submit it to the actual archive

CDLI, ORACC and eBL all take corrections and contributions from outside their core teams. The submission is the point — it's what makes the discovery real instead of private.

Log it, whatever the answer

Accepted, rejected, or "already known" — all three are useful outcomes. A rejected join with a documented reason is still a real result: it closes a possibility for the next person.

For classrooms & Hour House

Here Comes Everybody, applied to a clay tablet

The coordination problem used to be the whole obstacle: too many tablets, too few trained readers, no way for anyone outside the field to help. That's gone. What's left is a pipeline problem — getting a classroom a real task and routing what they find back into the actual record, with their name on it.

student attempts a join→ logged & submitted→ checked by a specialist→ accepted into CDLI / eBL→ credited, permanently

The credit is not a certificate of participation — it's a citable line in a real archive, findable by anyone, forever. That's a different thing to hand a student than a grade. It's purpose with a paper trail: a fifteen-year-old's join sitting in the same catalogue as a Yale curator's, because the tablet doesn't know or care who found it.

Keep the same discipline in the classroom as everywhere else in this project: the task is "what does this say," never "what does this prove." That boundary is what makes the credit worth having.
