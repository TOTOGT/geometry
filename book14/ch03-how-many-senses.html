How Many Senses a Word Has

A dictionary template, a proof checker and a tagged corpus
Warrantbook14/ch03-verify.py, 0 FAIL · book14/Polysemy.lean, kernel-checked
ManuscriptIJL v3 → v4
AuthorPablo Nogueira Grossi
A dictionary has to decide how many senses a word has. This chapter takes a manuscript written for the International Journal of Lexicography, which proposes a four-step template for that decision, and checks its two counting rules. A proof checker and a sense-tagged corpus each found one thing the manuscript had wrong. v4 of the manuscript corrects both.
1 · The template

Four questions for every entry

The manuscript, by Pablo Nogueira Grossi, maps the series' operator chain C → K → F → U onto four questions a lexicographer can ask of any word:

  1. What is the minimal reading the others derive from? (C, the head sense)
  2. Which constructions does each sense allow? (K)
  3. How many senses are there? (F)
  4. What context does each sense need? (U)

The answer to the third question is a co-predication test. Take "The book took three years to write and is now on the shelf": one noun carries two readings in one clause, so they are one sense. Now take "The charge was fifteen dollars and carried a five-year sentence": the result is a pun (a zeugma), so those readings are separate senses. MODEL

2 · What the kernel found

A test that has to be transitive

If "same sense" is to mean exactly "can be said together", then being-said-together must be transitive. Polysemy.lean proves this in general (classes_force_transitive). It then proves that one triple with a∼b, b∼c and a≀c defeats the rule completely: no assignment to senses, of any size, fits it (three_readings_no_division). KERNEL-CHECKED

The dot-object literature reports exactly that triple for newspaper, whose three readings are the object, its content and the institution. The object and content readings can be said together, and so can the content and institution readings, but the object and institution readings resist it. The judgement table is an input to the proof, not a corpus result. MODEL

The repair, now in v4 §7.1Read the test over chains: readings joined by a chain of co-predications form one sense, and each non-transitive triple goes into a usage note. The count is then well defined.

The second rule said "one fold produces two senses, two folds up to four". That holds for binary folds (leaves_le_pow, the bound 2k), and a binary fold of depth one gives exactly two (depth_one_two). But the manuscript's own book has three branches from one fold (formal, agentive, telic), so the rule must read "at least two". The bound then applies only to binary folds. KERNEL-CHECKED

3 · What the corpus found

The head is not the most frequent

v3 predicted that the head sense would also be the most frequent one, while §3.1 of the same text said it need not be. The script checks five nouns against WordNet 3.0 and the SemCor sense-tagged counts: COMPUTED

It fails twice. A chicken is eaten (16) more often than it walks (10), and a book is read (46) more often than it is shelved (10). v4 withdraws the prediction and keeps §3.1: derivational priority and frequency come apart. For charge, WordNet has 15 noun senses, 7 of them in SemCor. The most frequent is the cavalry charge (16), not the price (4), so v4 adds it as example (9d). COMPUTED

4 · Everything else v4 fixes
  • Petersen & Potts (2023) was wrong three ways: the first author is Erika Petersen, the venue is Findings of EACL 2023, and the pages are 490–511. The paper was also cited for a claim it does not make. v4 cites what it does say: the causative alternation depends on "the verb in combination with its theme argument" (§2), which is the unit the K gate acts on.
  • Bond et al. (2024) has four authors, not two, and runs to p. 225.
  • Sense counts for LDOCE5 were removed, because no copy is held to check them against.
