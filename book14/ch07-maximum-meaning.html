Maximum Meaning

What the NASA Sanskrit paper actually says

A widely shared story says NASA called Sanskrit the best language for computers. The paper behind it says something narrower and more interesting. Sanskrit grammarians built a way of analysing sentences that matches, triple for triple, what artificial intelligence was calling semantic networks in 1985. They wrote those analyses in a Sanskrit so compressed that it sometimes needed its own commentary.
1 · What the paper is

Briggs, NASA Ames, 1985

Rick Briggs wrote "Knowledge Representation in Sanskrit and Artificial Intelligence" at RIACS, a research institute at NASA Ames. It appeared in AI Magazine 6(1), Spring 1985, pp. 32–39. Its thesis is in the abstract: "a natural language can serve as an artificial language also", and "much work in AI has been reinventing a wheel millenia old" (p.32). Nowhere in its eight pages does it call Sanskrit the best language for computers. CITED

2 · Maximum meaning

Compressed, and then unfolded

Briggs describes the language of the grammarians as "a terse, very condensed form of Sanskrit, which paradoxically at times becomes so abstruse that a commentary is necessary to clarify it" (p.34). He says they wrote and talked "in a remarkably concise way" (p.36). Pāṇini's rules were "brief, algebraic formulations" (p.34). CITED

The same page shows the cost. Three words, graamam gacchati caitra ("Caitra goes to the village"), receive from the eighteenth-century grammarian Nāgeśa a paraphrase of 38 words: "There is an activity which leads to a connection-activity which has as Agent no one other than Caitra…". That is about thirteen times the length, and the paraphrase is where the meaning becomes explicit enough to reason with. COMPUTED

The series' reading This is the C → U pair of Chapter 3 in its oldest form. The sentence is the compressed core; the grammarian's paraphrase unfolds it until every role is named. Briggs names the trade-off himself: compression past a point needs a commentary. He also names the floor of decomposition: "The name ‘action’ cannot be applied to the solitary point reached by extreme subdivision" (p.35), which is a fold depth with a stopping rule. The mapping to C and U is ours (Pablo Nogueira Grossi's), not Briggs'. MODEL
3 · Why word order can be free

5,040 orders, one meaning

Briggs' central example is "Out of friendship, Maitra cooks rice for Devadatta in a pot, over a fire", in Sanskrit Maitraḥ sauhārdyāt Devadattāya odanam ghaṭe agninā pacati (p.38). Each noun carries its role in its ending, and the six roles are "Agent, Object, Instrument, Recipient, Point of Departure, Locality" (p.36). Word order, he says, "has usually no more than stylistic significance" (p.36). CITED

The script puts the seven words in all 5,040 possible orders. It reads each role from the word's ending, and every order returns the same six triples. English cannot do this: "Devadatta cooks rice for Maitra" swaps two roles without changing a single word form. The analyser is a toy that knows the endings of this one sentence and nothing else. 4 · Precise, not only short

Briggs does not claim that shortness alone made the language useful. Both traditions, he writes, encode "in a very clear, often apparently redundant way, in order to make the analysis accessible to inference" (p.38). The Sanskrit sentence is compressed, and the analysis of it is deliberately redundant. That is the same split this series draws between a statement and its proof. CITED
