How Mathematical Prose Parses

Imperatives, collective predicates and ambiguity, measured on this series

Mathematical prose is English (or Portuguese) bent into a special shape. It gives orders ("Let x be…"), it uses adjectives that apply to groups and not to individuals ("these are coprime"), and its words carry more than one sense. Six recent studies each measure one of these features. This chapter runs their measurements on this series' own prose, and the prose turns out to break the pattern in a way worth knowing.
1 · Proofs are recipes, and this prose is not

Thirteen imperatives, counted

Tanswell and Inglis (2023) searched arXiv for imperatives inside proofs and concluded "in favour of the recipe model of proofs: that proofs are like recipes, giving instructions for mathematical actions to be carried out". Their thirteen common imperatives run at 9,406 per million words inside proofs and 6,854 outside (Table 3). CITED

The script counts the same thirteen verbs at the start of sentences in this series' prose: 832 pages and 1,392,322 words, with formulas and code removed. COMPUTED

Where the recipes wentThe recipes are in the Lean. Every tactic line is an imperative in the grammatical sense: have, intro, rw, exact. Book XIX ch 2 counted 2,482 of them. This series splits what an arXiv proof does in one place across two: the prose makes assertions, and the Lean carries out the instructions. Tanswell and Inglis's recipe model fits the formal half exactly and the prose hardly at all. MODEL
2 · Words that apply to groups

Coprime, and the series' own word, orthogonal

Arambillete and de Groote (2025) open with a sophism: "Every set of prime numbers is a fortiori a set of coprime numbers. Therefore, every prime number is a coprime number." The trouble is that "coprime is a collective predicate". It is said of a set, not of one number. CITED

Collective.lean makes this exact. The premise is true: any set of primes is pairwise coprime (primes_pairwise_coprime). The conclusion is not false but empty: read about one number, "coprime" is true of every number, because a one-element set is vacuously pairwise anything. So 4 is "a coprime number", and 4 is not prime (four_is_coprime_distributively). KERNEL-CHECKED

This series' title word has the same grammar. "u, v, w are orthogonal" means every pair, and cannot be read along a chain: (1,0) ⊥ (0,1) and (0,1) ⊥ (2,0), but (1,0)·(2,0) = 2 (orthogonal_not_transitive). This is the non-transitivity Chapter 3 found in co-predication, now inside one word. KERNEL-CHECKED

In the pages, the collective form "are orthogonal" appears 11 times. The explicit forms appear less often: "mutually orthogonal" 6 times and "pairwise orthogonal" never. The singular "is orthogonal" appears 35 times. The series mostly writes the ambiguous collective form and leaves the pairwise reading to the reader. COMPUTED

3 · Ambiguity as a number

How many bits is a word worth?

Balkır (2014) models word meaning with density matrices from quantum mechanics: "words are represented by mixed states and each eigenstate represents a sense of the word". An unambiguous word is a pure state, and ambiguity is measured by entropy. CITED

Applied to Chapter 4's operator keys, and reading each gloss as a separate pure state, the two ways of counting disagree: COMPUTED

Over glosses, K is the most ambiguous key: threshold, curvature and gate. But the pages join those three into one sense, so at the level of senses K carries no ambiguity at all. Only U keeps entropy once senses are counted, because union and unification are not joined to unfolding. That is the linguist's distinction between polysemy, one meaning with shades (K), and homonymy, separate meanings under one form (U), measured in bits. The quantum reading is ours. MODEL

4 · Speaking mathematics

Two further sources place this in practice. Orth (2013) argues that "much like learning a foreign language, learning mathematics has been based mostly on oral tradition", and that literacy comes second but cannot be skipped. Corneli, Martin, Pease and colleagues (2017) model mathematicians' dialogues, the arguments people actually have, as "a higher-order nested semantic network". That is the representation Chapter 7 found in the Sanskrit grammarians. CITED

A number to read carefullyFIRMA (Fatima) translates between Lean and informal mathematics. It reports "a 6307.5% improvement over REAL-Prover", with an overall score of 0.304. Turned back into a baseline, that percentage means REAL-Prover scored about 0.005, and even the stronger baseline scored 0.080. The percentage is large because the baselines are near zero, not because 0.304 is high. The scores are the author's own and are not replicated here. OPEN
