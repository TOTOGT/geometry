Reading the Navier–Stokes blowup

A guide to OpenAI, Finite time blowup for Navier–Stokes, preprint of 8 September 2026, 166 pages (PDF). Prepared by Pablo Nogueira Grossi with the assistance of Claude (Anthropic). Section, theorem and equation numbers are the paper's.

OPEN Where things stand

  • The paper is a preprint. It has not completed peer review. The Clay Mathematics Institute has said the problem "has apparently been settled" and that it will act only after peer-reviewed publication and the community's checking.
  • OpenAI says the proof was produced by its AI agents and checked with the Lean proof assistant. We have not found a public description of what that formalization covers.
  • There is a priority dispute. Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge announced their own smooth-forcing results for the Euler, porous-media and Boussinesq equations about twelve hours before OpenAI's announcement, and said their unpublished work may have reached OpenAI's system; OpenAI says it did not. The paper itself builds on Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa's amplification strategy (§1.1).
  • This guide explains the mathematics as the paper presents it. It does not referee the proof.

1The claim, exactly

Theorem 1.1. For every viscosity ν > 0 there are a smooth force f, compactly supported in space and time, a compact set K, and smooth u, p on ℝ³ × [0, 1) solving the Navier–Stokes equations with u(·, 0) = 0, supported in K, with kinetic energy bounded for all t < 1 and with supx|u(x, t)| → ∞ as t ↑ 1.

So the fluid starts at rest. A smooth push, switched on and off inside a fixed region, drives it to infinite speed at time 1, while its total energy stays bounded. No smooth bounded-energy solution can continue past that moment. Clay's problem statement (Fefferman, 2000) asks for a proof of any one of four alternatives. This is alternative (C), breakdown on ℝ³ with a smooth, decaying force allowed. Because everything has compact support, the same flow also gives (D), breakdown on the torus (Corollary 10.6). CITED

What it says

  • Forced, three-dimensional Navier–Stokes can blow up from smooth data in finite time.
  • It happens for every viscosity, with finite energy.
  • The blowup is explicit: you can describe the flow that does it.

What it does not say

  • Nothing about the unforced equations (f = 0), which is the physically central question and remains open.
  • Nothing about turbulence or real fluids: the force is engineered, cycle by cycle, to cancel what the flow cannot supply.
  • Not yet peer reviewed.

2Why a force does not make it easy

With a force allowed, any flow at all "solves" the equations: pick u and p, and call whatever is left over the force, f = ∂tu + (u·∇)u − νΔu + ∇p. The paper calls this the momentum residual R(u, p). The whole difficulty moves into one requirement: f must stay smooth, with every derivative bounded, right through the blowup time, even though u and its derivatives become infinite there. The individual terms of R diverge. Their sum must not.

So the paper is a cancellation problem. It builds a flow whose singular terms cancel to every order at the singular point, and then checks that what is left over is a perfectly ordinary smooth force (§2, §3).

3The picture

The flow has four parts (§2):

  • The inner core. An axisymmetric vortex centred at the origin. Fluid spirals toward the axis, carrying angular momentum inward, so it spins faster, the way a skater pulls in their arms. Incompressibility pushes it out along the axis, so the spiral can keep going. Radial inflow and viscous diffusion stay in balance; the shape is self-similar, with radius ∼ τ1/2 and height ∼ τ1/2−h for a small fixed h (0 < h < 1/100). A slight up–down asymmetry is built in on purpose, so that shear never vanishes where it is needed (§2.1).
  • The annulus. Where the core is joined to the slower outside flow, the equations are not satisfied: a residual remains, and it blows up. Left alone, it would need an unbounded force (§2.2).
  • The pulses. Rings of rapidly oscillating velocity are placed in the annulus. Each is seeded by an exponentially small force and then amplified by the shear of the core, grows, and is finally damped by viscosity. A wave's velocity averages to zero, but its momentum flux does not; the pulses are arranged so that this flux is exactly the missing force. in the paper's words, "the fluid's own motion supplies the missing momentum transport" (§2.2).
  • The heat exterior. Outside, a purely swirling flow that solves the radial heat equation exactly, so it needs no force at all, and stays smooth at every fixed radius up to t = 1. That is what allows the flow to be cut off in space (§2.3).

5The proof in six moves

  1. Build the leading profile.

    Similarity coordinates X = r²/(2q), η = z/qD, with τ = q(1 − η²), turn the shrinking core into fixed profiles E, U, Π for swirl, axial velocity and pressure. The profiles are built near the axis (analytic), joined to the heat exterior, and five "radial moments" are matched so that the leftover residual lives only in the annulus and can be written as the divergence of a stress T.

    Theorem 4.6; Appendices A (heat exterior, moment matching) and B (profiles near the axis).

  2. Make the stress realizable by waves.

    A wave family produces momentum flux only in certain directions. With two families, fluxes v₁, v₂, the target must satisfy T = c₁v₁ + c₂v₂ with c₁, c₂ > 0: it must lie inside the cone they span. The profile is tuned (a small oscillation with phase N log X) so that this "admissible stress cone" condition holds everywhere in the annulus.

    Lemma 4.5; Proposition C.2, C.3 (Appendix C).

  3. Correct the background to every order.

    Corrections in powers q2nh absorb the radial momentum equation, axial viscosity and lower-order terms. The result is an axisymmetric background whose residual is exactly the annular stress divergence plus a remainder that is "flat": every derivative is O(qN) for every N.

    Proposition 5.5.

  4. Add the pulses.

    Divergence-free oscillations w = ∇ × A, written as waves a cos(ξ·x + φ) with a ⟂ ξ, grow by drawing energy from the shear and then decay as the shear tilts their wavevector and viscosity takes over. Their averaged products ⟨wrwθ⟩, ⟨wrwz⟩ equal T to leading order, so their flux cancels the background's leading residual. An auxiliary torus variable keeps different pulses from interacting.

    Lemmas 7.1, 7.4, 7.7; Propositions 7.5, 9.5; Section 6 (auxiliary torus).

  5. Iterate the corrections.

    Every correction creates new errors (linear terms, wave–wave interactions, cutoff errors). A fixed cycle of four operations removes them and improves the decay exponent by 1/10 each time, σj+1 = σj + 1/10 from σ0 = 1/5. Summing with shrinking cutoffs gives a local flow whose residual is flat at the singular point.

    Propositions 9.1, 9.6, 9.9; Section 8 (mean corrections).

  6. Localize and conclude.

    Cut off in space and time, define f as the residual, and show every derivative of f has a limit at t = 1, so f extends to a smooth compactly supported force. Bounded energy follows from the scalings. A uniqueness lemma then shows any smooth bounded-energy solution with the same force and data must equal this one before t = 1, so none survives past it.

    Proposition 10.1; Lemmas 10.2–10.5; Theorem 1.1, Corollary 10.6.

The local result behind moves 1–5 is stated as Theorem 3.1: a divergence-free field, smooth at the axis, with a flat residual near the singular point, zero residual outside a fixed radius, and swirl growing like τ−1/2−h. CITED

7Words you will meet

Residual R(u, p)
What is left when a chosen u, p are put into the equations. The force is defined to be it.
Flat
A quantity whose every derivative is smaller than any power qN near the singular point. Flat errors extend smoothly through t = 1.
τ, q, X, η
Remaining time; concentration scale (q ≍ τ near the core); radial and axial similarity variables.
Swirl
The azimuthal velocity uθ, the speed around the axis. It is what blows up.
Stress, covariance
Averaged products of wave velocities, such as ⟨wrwθ⟩: the momentum the waves carry. Their divergence acts like a force on the mean flow.
Admissible stress cone
The set of stresses two wave families can produce with positive weights. The target stress must lie inside it.
Auxiliary torus
An extra variable Y on which the pulses depend, so that overlapping pulses can be given disjoint supports and do not interact.
Heat exterior
The outer swirling flow, an exact solution of the radial heat equation. It needs no force.
Reynolds numbers
Reθ ∼ τ−h grows (the fluid turns many times per diffusion time); Rer stays bounded (viscosity keeps competing with inflow).
Alternatives (A)–(D)
Fefferman's four statements in Clay's problem: smoothness on ℝ³ or the torus without force, or breakdown on ℝ³ or the torus with a smooth force. Proving any one solves the prize problem.

8What a careful reader should check

These are the places where an argument of this kind can fail. They are a reading list, not a report of errors; we have not found any.

  • The cone condition. Moves 2 and 4 need the target stress strictly inside the cone, uniformly. Appendix C does this by perturbing the profile; check that the perturbation keeps the five moments and the inner solution exactly (Proposition C.3).
  • Every derivative, uniformly. Flatness must hold for all Cartesian space-time derivatives, uniformly in the similarity variables (Theorem 3.1(iii), Proposition 9.9). A bound that loses derivatives stage by stage is fine only if, as the paper states, the loss does not depend on the stage.
  • The correction cycle closes. Each cycle must improve the exponent by a fixed amount while all new linear and quadratic terms are retained (Proposition 9.6).
  • The force really extends. Limits of all derivatives of f at t = 1 must exist and be compatible, including where r stays positive but q → 0 (Lemmas 10.2, 10.3).
  • Uniqueness in the right class. The conclusion needs a smooth bounded-energy solution to coincide with the constructed one before t = 1 (Lemma 10.5).
  • The formal proof. What exactly was checked in Lean, and against which statement of the theorem.

9Where it comes from

The paper places itself in a line of work (§1.1). Leray (1934) built global weak solutions. Caffarelli, Kohn and Nirenberg limited the size of the singular set. Escauriaza, Seregin and Šverák proved regularity under a scale-invariant bound. Tao (2016) proved blowup for an averaged version of the equations. Buckmaster and Vicol, and then Albritton, Brué and Colombo, proved non-uniqueness of weak solutions. Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa, later with Zheng, produced forced singularities for Euler and hypodissipative Navier–Stokes by amplifying ever-smaller vortex layers. The wave tools go back to Craik and Criminale's exact waves on shear flows and to the Lifschitz–Hameiri and Friedlander–Vishik geometric optics for fluids. What is new here, in the paper's words: "oscillatory pulses generate a mean momentum flux that supplies the missing force on a collapsing background vortex." CITED
