Book X · Ch 7 · 2026-09-19 · What reached us, and in what shape

Up Before Right

Hans Rosling spent a career arguing that the public does not merely lack the facts about development — it holds the wrong ones, in a consistent direction. The arithmetic of his own survey shows he was right in a stronger sense than he put it. Then his causal thesis is put to the one country this volume keeps circling.

A wrong answer is not the absence of a right one. On a three-option question, being reliably wrong takes as much information as being reliably right — it is just pointed the other way. That distinction is what this chapter measures, and it is the whole subject of Book X.

1 · Below chance is not ignorance

Gapminder's Misconception Study 2017 put thirteen fact questions to roughly a thousand people in each of fourteen countries. Twelve were analysed; one, on climate, was set aside because 87 % got it right. Each question offered three options.

quantityvalue
mean score, twelve questions2.2  (18.3 %)
score expected from picking at random4.0  (33.3 %)
respondents who beat random10 %
respondents who got all twelve wrong15 %

Rosling made the comparison with chimpanzees picking lettered bananas, and it was taken as a joke about how little people know. The arithmetic says something harder. A respondent answering at random gets all twelve wrong with probability

(2/3)^12 = 4096 / 531441 = 0.0077 = 0.77 %

and 15 % did. That is 19.5 times the rate chance would produce. Scoring 18.3 % on three-option questions is not what not-knowing looks like; not-knowing looks like 33.3 %. To land reliably below chance you must have a model of the world, apply it, and have it be wrong in a consistent direction.

The distinction Book X exists for An empty channel delivers noise. These answers are not noise. Something was transmitted, it was coherent enough to be applied twelve times in a row, and it was false. Chapter 2 found an object six thousand kilometres from the people who argue about it; chapter 3 found forty figures that almost nobody who cites them has checked. This is the same finding at the scale of a public.

2 · What Rosling said the lever was

In Global population growth, box by box (TED@Cannes, 2010) Rosling stacked the world's population into boxes and made an argument that runs against the intuition most people bring: you do not slow population growth by letting children die. You slow it by stopping children dying. Families that can rely on their children surviving stop insuring against the loss, and the birth rate falls — after the death rate, not before it.

That is a claim about order, and order is testable.

3 · South Korea, in the order the claim predicts

This volume keeps arriving at South Korea. Ha-Joon Chang, interviewed by Finance & Development in September 2026, described living through it as riding a time machine. Take four World Bank series for Korea, 1960 to 2020, and ask one question of each: in what year had half of the whole sixty-year change already happened?

series19602020half reached
under-5 mortality, per 1,000112.93.11970.8
births per woman5.990.841975.0
life expectancy, years53.883.41984.7
GDP per capita, constant 2015 US$1,03833,2161999.5

Child survival, then fertility, then longevity, then income — in that order, spread over 28.7 years. Korea had banked half its century of child-survival gain before it had banked a sixth of its income gain. The order is Rosling's, and nothing in the computation was chosen to produce it: the four series are the World Bank's, the question asked of each is identical, and ch07-verify.py fails if they ever stop falling in that order.

What four dates do not establish This is one country, and an ordering is not a mechanism. Korea also ran an industrial policy, nationalised its banks, and was governed by a military dictatorship — Chang is explicit that the growth and the repression arrived together. A sequence consistent with a causal claim is not that claim proved, and nothing here separates “health first” from “whatever else was happening in 1965 first”. What the table does settle is the reading that wealth comes first and buys health. On these four series, for this country, it did not.

4 · A number in transit

Chang says life expectancy was 53 when he was born in 1963, and adds that statistically he should have been dead for a decade. The series puts Korean life expectancy at 53.8 in 1960 and 55.0 in 1963.

The figure is real and it is his country's; it is three years older than the sentence carrying it. Nothing turns on it — his point survives 55 exactly as well as 53. It is recorded here because this is the volume about what happens to a number in transit, and a chapter that catches a museum, a bestseller and a public survey while waving through the number in its own epigraph would be the thing it is complaining about.

5 · What is open

  • The same four-series test on other countries. One case is an illustration; the claim is about the shape of development, and either the order holds broadly or it does not. The computation is four lines and the data is public.
  • Whether the 2017 misconception figures have moved. The study is nine years old and Gapminder has run others.
