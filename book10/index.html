Two letters do the work of this volume's name. X is ten, and this is the tenth book. X is also the Africa term in BRIX — the variable standing for a continent that has ratified a continental trade treaty and not yet acquired one voice to negotiate under it. The series numbers the book; the second meaning is its subject. And the subject is older than any treaty: every human science has a first chapter, and that chapter is African.

1 · Two halves, one question

This volume opened as political economy — what a producing country has committed away, for how long, and to whom. It is becoming something larger, because the question turned out not to be about commodities.

Ask what share of a country's iron ore is spoken for and you are asking who holds a claim on a thing, under what instrument, and for how long. Ask where the Ishango bone is and you are asking the same three things about a carved fibula from Lake Edward. In both cases there is a public number for where the thing is and no public number for the terms it is held under. In both cases the terms are what decide whether the position is reversible.

The volume's one structural claim

Custody and commitment are the same relation seen from two ends. A holder acquires an option — to keep, to lend, to interpret, to refuse — and the party from whom it came loses the corresponding one. Where the loss is written into a contract, we call it offtake. Where it is written into a museum statute, we call it a collection. The accounting is identical and neither side books it.

2 · The three axes

AxisThe questionWhat is usually missing
History of scienceWhat was known on the continent, where, and when — read from the objects and the records rather than from later summaries of them.The primary object, which is frequently not in the country that produced it, and often not on the continent.
CustodyWho holds it now. Under what instrument. Whether return is possible at all, and if not, what specifically bars it.The instrument. Location is catalogued; terms of holding are not aggregated anywhere.
TransmissionHow it moved — in both directions — and whether the record of the movement survived the movement.The middle of the chain. Endpoints are documented; the passage between them is where attribution is lost.

The three are not separable in practice. A history of science written without custody is a history of whatever happened to stay accessible. A custody question without transmission is a property dispute. Transmission without either is a story.

3 · Why the continent we came out of

This is not a regional volume and should not be read as one. Every lineage of modern humans traces to Africa; so, necessarily, does the first instance of every human cognitive practice — counting, marking, measuring, recording, inferring. A history of science that begins in Africa is not a chapter of the subject. It is the subject's first chapter, and the rest of the discipline has spent a long time treating it as an appendix.

The volume takes that literally rather than rhetorically. The claim to be tested in each chapter is narrow and checkable: what does this object show, where is it, who holds it, and what would it take for the people best placed to interpret it to be in a room with it.

8 · How this volume behaves

In 1971 Italian volunteers taught Zambians in the Zambezi valley to grow magnificent tomatoes. Two hundred hippos ate the crop overnight. The Zambians had always known. “You never asked.”

The story is Ernesto Sirolli's, and his method — “you never initiate anything, you never motivate anybody… you become a servant of the local passion” — is the one this volume is trying to copy. It has no view about what anyone else should be researching and will not acquire one. The chapters below are the author's own work, offered as examples of a standard rather than as a direction.

The rule this volume operates under

We cannot fix the problems in Russia, nor in Mexico, nor in Africa. Nobody outside a country ever has. What is available is narrower and worth more: listen to the people inside the problem, help with what they say they need, and then do the one thing distance is good for — publish the number they are not placed to publish for themselves.

That last clause is the whole of the offer. A person inside a problem is usually a party to it: the aggregate that describes it runs against their own counterparty, their own ministry or their own employer, and assembling it is unpaid work taking months. Someone at a distance with no position to protect can do exactly that. It is the only contribution here that does not require pretending to know better, and it is why every chapter ends in a measurable rather than a recommendation.

The conditions for the questions in this volume to be answered already exist. What is usually missing is not capability but that nobody asked, and that nobody published the answer at an address. Supplying the address is the service. How to write a chapter →

9 · This volume asks for chapters

The rest of this series was written by one author working through one argument. This volume cannot be, and should not be. Custody and transmission are questions whose evidence is distributed across archives, collections, field sites and institutions on several continents, and most of it is held by people who are not the present author and know considerably more.

So Book X is open. A chapter here is not a submission to be judged and published; it is a piece of study carried out in the open, with its sources, its uncertainties and its corrections on the page while the work is happening. The standard is the series standard and nothing more: every claim resolves to an address or is marked open.

What would make this volume wrong

If the custody instruments turn out to be aggregated somewhere already — if some registry does record, for a class of objects, the legal basis of holding and whether return is barred — then chapter 2's premise fails and the work is to publicise that registry rather than to build one. Finding it would be the most useful possible outcome and nobody should be attached to its absence.
