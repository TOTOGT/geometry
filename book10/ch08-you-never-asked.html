You Never Asked

Every channel ran from the donors to the recipients: money, seed, expertise, evaluation. The one fact that would have saved the crop was held by the people standing in the field, and there was no channel in that direction at all.

The tomatoes grew. That is the part of the story everyone forgets, and it is the part that matters: the project did not fail on agronomy, on funding, or on goodwill. It failed on a question nobody asked.

1 · What is established about the project

Ernesto Sirolli, born 1950, worked for an Italian non-governmental organisation in Africa from 1971 to 1977. In southern Zambia, in a valley running down to the Zambezi, his team taught people to grow Italian tomatoes and courgettes. In his account the crop came up magnificently, and was then eaten overnight by hippopotami from the river. Asked why they had not mentioned the hippos, the local people said they had not been asked.

2 · The number this corpus repeated

Chapter 2 of this volume prints the story as part of its own confession — that it was researched from Newark without one conversation with anyone in the country the object came from. It says the crop was lost to two hundred hippos.

That figure was not located in any source reachable in the session that wrote this chapter. The transcript excerpt that was reachable establishes the country, the valley, the organisation, the years and the crop, and stops before the hippos. The count may well be in the talk; the point is that this corpus printed it without having put its hands on it.

The same failure, one level up Sirolli's team did not ask, and lost a crop. This corpus liked the story about not asking so much that it repeated the story's most vivid number without checking it. A parable about receiving is not a licence to receive uncritically, and the number is marked not located here rather than quietly dropped, because a figure that has been repeated needs a record saying so, not a silent deletion.

3 · Why this belongs in a volume about custody

Chapter 1 defined an encumbrance: a commitment that a transaction does not record. Chapter 2 found an object held under a statute whose term nobody states. Chapter 4 found a receiving capacity that is measured every year by a United Nations standard and cited by nobody. Chapter 7 found a public that answers below chance.

Sirolli's hippos are the same structure in its cheapest form. The information needed was not scarce, not technical, not expensive, and not held far away. It was held by the people standing in the field. What was missing was a channel in the direction nobody had built one: from the recipients to the donors. Every other channel — money, seed, expertise, evaluation — ran the other way.

What Enterprise Facilitation is, stated plainly Sirolli's method after 1985 inverts the direction of initiative. The facilitator does not arrive with a project. They wait to be asked, respond to what the person in front of them actually wants to do, and supply the two or three competences that person lacks — on the premise that no one individual is good at making a product, selling it, and managing the money. It is a claim about where the information is, not about where the virtue is.

4 · What this chapter does not claim

  • That the story is false. Every element reachable this session checks out, and the unlocated element is a count, not the event.
  • That Enterprise Facilitation works. That is an empirical question about outcomes over forty years and this chapter measures none of them. The method is described because its premise — that the binding constraint is a missing upward channel — is the thesis of this volume, arrived at independently.
  • That asking is sufficient. Chapter 3 is about a book that asked hard questions and got contested answers. Listening is a precondition, not a method.

5 · What is open

  • Locate the hippo count. Either in the TED transcript at a timestamp or in Ripples from the Zambezi at a page. Then chapter 2 can carry a citation instead of a number, or lose the number.
  • Whether any published evaluation of Enterprise Facilitation exists with outcomes rather than testimonials. If it does, it belongs in this volume; if it does not, that absence is itself the kind of finding chapter 3 deals in.
