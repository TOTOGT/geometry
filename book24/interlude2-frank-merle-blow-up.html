Where linearity ends: blow-up, and Frank Merle’s prize

A linear equation cannot explode in finite time. A nonlinear one can, from perfectly smooth data, and the mathematician most associated with understanding how it happens in the equations of waves and fluids has just received the Breakthrough Prize. This interlude records what the sources say about him, and uses the smallest possible example to show what blow-up is.

The object. The equation u′ = up with u(0) = u0 > 0. For p = 1 the solution grows but is finite at every time. For p = 2 and p = 3 it is finite only up to a time T and then leaves every bound. Choose p and u0 and read off T and the values just before it. This is a toy: it is not Merle’s equation.

2 · Blow-up in one line

Intuition

Blow-up means a solution that is perfectly well behaved up to some time, and then, in finite time, grows past every bound. It needs a feedback that gets stronger as the quantity gets larger: the bigger u is, the faster it grows, and the faster it grows the bigger it gets. In u′ = u2 the rate is the square of the size, and the size doubles faster each time it doubles.

Statement
The solution of u′ = u²For u(0) = u0 > 0, u(t) = 1/(1/u0 − t) for t < T = 1/u0, and u(t) → ∞ as t → T. Moreover u(t)(T − t) = 1 for every t < T.
Proof and numbers

The derivative of 1/(c − t) is 1/(c − t)2, which is the square of 1/(c − t), so the formula solves u′ = u2; at t = 0 it equals u0. As t increases to T = 1/u0 the denominator tends to 0 from above. The product u(t)(T − t) is 1 by the formula. For u0 = 1 the blow-up time is T = 1, and u(0.5) = 2, u(0.9) = 10, u(0.99) = 100, u(0.999) = 1000: at each step the distance to T is divided by 10 and the solution is multiplied by 10. A solution that starts larger blows up sooner: T = 1/2 for u0 = 2 and T = 4 for u0 = 1/4.

Remarks

The identity u(t)(T − t) = 1 is the simplest picture of a profile: multiply the solution by the rate at which it blows up, and what is left does not change. This is only an analogy with the word “profile” as it is used for partial differential equations; it is not a statement about Merle’s results. The script checks, exactly, that the formula satisfies u′ = u2 and u(0) = u0 for u0 in {1/4, 1/2, 1, 2} at ten times each, the identity u(t)(T − t) = 1, and the values 2, 10, 100, 1000 above. 3 · Why a linear equation cannot do this

Intuition

For u′ = u (the case p = 1) the feedback is only proportional: doubling the size doubles the rate, and the solution u0et grows quickly but is finite at every time. Blow-up needs growth faster than proportional. And once the rate is not proportional to the size, the superposition of chapter 2 is gone: the sum of two solutions is no longer a solution.

Statement
Three facts(a) For u′ = u3, u(t)−2 = u0−2 − 2t, so the blow-up time is T = 1/(2u02). (b) For u′ = u there is no blow-up time. (c) For u′ = u2, if u1 and u2 are solutions, then u1 + u2 is not one while both are positive.
Proof

(a) If u′ = u3, then (u−2)′ = −2u−3u′ = −2, so u−2 is a straight line of slope −2 and reaches 0 at t = 1/(2u02). For u0 = 1 that is T = 1/2; for u0 = 1/2, T = 2; for u0 = 3, T = 1/18. (b) u0et is finite for every t. (c) (u1 + u2)′ = u12 + u22, while (u1 + u2)2 = u12 + u22 + 2u1u2; the two differ by 2u1u2. At t = 1/3, for the solutions with u0 = 1/2 and u0 = 1/4, that difference is positive.

The general rule, which this page states without proof, is that for u′ = f(u) with f positive and increasing, the solution blows up in finite time exactly when the integral of du/f(u) out to infinity is finite. For f(u) = up that integral is infinite when p = 1 (it is a logarithm) and finite when p > 1, where it equals the blow-up time: 1/u0 for p = 2 and 1/(2u02) for p = 3.

Remarks

The script checks (a) on u2 exactly for u0 in {1/2, 1, 3} at ten times each, and the three blow-up times; that et is finite at t = 10, 100 and 700; the partial sums behind the integral criterion for p = 1 and p = 2 (a finite check that illustrates it, not a proof of it); and the failure of superposition at t = 1/3, exactly.

4 · The toy is the fold, and where that stops

Intuition

The equation x′ = μ + x2 is the standard picture of a fold (a saddle-node bifurcation, the “fold” of Interlude 1). For μ < 0 it has two equilibria, one steady and one not. As μ rises to 0 they merge, and at μ = 0 the equation is exactly u′ = u2, the toy of Part 2. For μ > 0 there are no equilibria and every solution runs off to infinity in finite time. So, for this one ordinary differential equation, the blow-up and the fold are the same thing seen from two sides.

Statement
The fold normal form x′ = μ + x²For μ < 0 the equilibria are x = ±√(−μ). At μ = 0 the equation is u′ = u2. For μ > 0 the solutions are x = √μ tan(√μ (t − t0)), which run from −∞ to +∞ in time π/√μ. This time grows without bound as μ falls to 0.
Proof

For μ < 0, μ + x2 = 0 exactly at x = ±√(−μ); for μ = −1/4, −4, −9 these are ±1/2, ±2, ±3. For μ > 0, the derivative of √μ tan(√μ t) is μ sec2(√μ t) = μ(1 + tan2(√μ t)) = μ + x2, and tan runs from −∞ to +∞ over an interval of length π, here π/√μ in t. The linearisation at 0 of u′ = u2 is v′ = 0 (the derivative 2u vanishes at 0): it predicts a constant solution, while the real solution starting at 1 reaches 100 at t = 0.99 and keeps going. This is the situation of Interlude 1, where linearisation said nothing.

Remarks

This identification is exact for the ordinary differential equation and for nothing else here. Both words are polysemous, which is the hazard: “fold” here means only the saddle-node normal form x′ = μ + x2 (it also names the Whitney fold of a map of the plane, and a crease in many other settings), and “blow-up” means a solution leaving every bound in finite time (it also means other things in algebraic geometry and in numerical analysis). Two meanings that share a word are not thereby one meaning. Whether the singularities of partial differential equations that Merle and his collaborators study are folds in any sense is a different and much harder question, which this page does not address and the sources read do not claim; the words “blow-up” and “fold” sharing a picture in one dimension is not evidence about them. If a reader wants to claim a connection with another system, such as the DM3 system of this series, the claim needs its own derivation, not a resemblance. The script checks the equilibria for the three values of μ, the merger at μ = 0, the failed linearisation, the tangent solution (numerically, at twelve points, an identity that is proved above), and the passage times π/√μ for μ = 1/4, 1 and 4.

5 · Focusing against dispersion: the virial identity

Intuition

A wave packet of the linear Schrödinger equation spreads, because its short waves run faster than its long ones: this is dispersion. The nonlinear term in the critical equation pulls the packet together. Which one wins is decided by a single number, the energy, through an identity for the size of the packet, y(t) = ∫ x²|u|² dx. The identity says that the acceleration of y is a constant, 16 times the energy. A positive acceleration means spreading; a negative one means the size would have to turn negative, which is impossible, so the solution must collapse. This is the implosion, in the same sense as the one-line toy of Part 2, and dispersion is its opposite.

Statement
The critical equation in one dimensioni ut = −uxx − |u|4u. Conserved: the mass M = ∫|u|² and the energy E = ½∫|ux|² − (1/6)∫|u|6.
Virial identityWith y(t) = ∫ x²|u|² dx, y″(t) = 16 E. So y(t) = y(0) + y′(0) t + 8E t².
ConsequencesIf E > 0, y grows like 8E t²: the packet spreads (for the linear equation E > 0 always, and the identity reads y″ = 8∫|ux|²). If E < 0 and y(0) is finite, the parabola opens downward and would be negative at a finite time, which is impossible for a size, so the solution cannot exist for all time: it blows up in finite time (Glassey’s argument).
Where the mass threshold entersLet Q = 31/4 sech(2x)1/2, the ground state (Q″ − Q + Q5 = 0), with mass MQ = √3π/2 ≈ 2.7207. The sharp Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality (1/6)∫|u|6 ≤ ½(M/MQ)²∫|ux|² gives E ≥ ½(1 − (M/MQ)²)∫|ux|². So if M ≤ MQ then E ≥ 0 and the virial argument cannot produce a collapse: negative energy forces M > MQ. The inequality is Weinstein’s; it is stated here and not derived.
Derivation

The first derivative. From ut = i(uxx + |u|4u), ûut + conj = 2 Re(i û uxx) + 2 Re(i|u|6) = −2 Im(û uxx) = −2 ∂x Im(û ux), because |u|6 is real. Integrating by parts, y′ = 4∫ x Im(û ux) dx. (Here û means the complex conjugate of u.)

The second derivative, organised by scaling. Take the dilation uλ(x) = λ1/2u(λx), which keeps the mass. Then ∫|∂xuλ|² = λ²∫|ux|² and ∫|uλ|6 = λ²∫|u|6: both terms of the energy scale with the same power, and that is what “critical” means. So E(uλ) = λ²E(u). The second derivative of y is 8 times the derivative of the energy along dilations at λ = 1, namely 8 × 2E = 16E; term by term, the kinetic part gives 8∫|ux|² = 16 × ½∫|ux|² and the nonlinear part gives −(8/3)∫|u|6 = 16 × (−1/6)∫|u|6. For another power of the nonlinearity the two terms scale differently, and y″ is no longer a multiple of E. The direct expansion by integration by parts takes a page and is not written out here; the identity is checked numerically below.

Solving for y. E is conserved, so y″ is the constant 16E, and integrating twice gives the parabola. If E < 0 its leading coefficient 8E is negative, so y < 0 for large t; since y ≥ 0 always, the solution cannot be defined for all t > 0. □

Remarks

What was checked. The script integrates the equation numerically (split-step Fourier, floating point, not exact) for the initial data u0 = A e−x²/2e0.7ix with A = 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, and compares a finite-difference second derivative of y at t = 0 with 16E: the two agree to five or six digits in each case (energies +0.217, +0.031 and −36.2). It checks that Q solves its equation (residual about 3 × 10−11), that its mass is √3π/2, that E(Q) = 0, and that the inequality E ≥ ½(1 − (M/MQ)²)∫|ux|² holds for the three test functions, which illustrates the sharp inequality and does not prove it. The three test functions have masses 0.44, 3.99 and 11.1, so two of them lie above MQ; this is a check of an identity, not a study of blow-up.

What the virial argument cannot do. It needs y(0) finite, and it gives blow-up for E < 0 with no information on the rate. Removing the finiteness condition, and bounding the rate from above, near the threshold mass, is what the 2005 paper of Merle and Raphael does (Annals 161); this page has not read its proofs. The dispersive side below the threshold mass (global solutions) rests on the inequality above and is not proved here.

Analogy, not derivation. The competition between a term that spreads (here the Laplacian, in CATGT the coupling J) and a term that localises (here the nonlinearity, in CATGT the on-site binding λ) has the same shape as the self-trapping radius r*(λ) = a√(J/λ) of the CATGT paper. That is a shared functional form, not a shared invariant. r* is a crossover length on a lattice; the threshold here is a mass in a scale-invariant continuum equation. No derivation connects the two, and none is claimed. The compressible-fluid implosion of Merle, Raphaël, Rodnianski and Szeftel (Annals 196, 2022) is a different equation, and this page has not read those papers either.

6 · Three things called implosion, and how structure prevents one of them

Intuition

The word has been used here for three different things. A wave of the critical Schrödinger equation focuses on a point (part 5). A gas converges on a point in the compressible-fluid work of Merle, Raphaël, Rodnianski and Szeftel, which this page has not read. And a hollow hull collapses under outside pressure: the loss of the Titan submersible in June 2023 was a hull failure of that kind, and this page makes no claim about its cause, which belongs to the official investigations. The first two are about equations whose solutions run to a point in finite time. The third is about a structure: a shell that holds until the pressure passes a threshold and then buckles. Nothing here links the three beyond the word, and the one thing they share, a threshold, has a different origin in each.

The question for a structure is how to prevent the collapse. There are two ways. One is to resist: make the wall strong enough to carry the whole load, which is what a crewed hull with air inside at one atmosphere must do. The other is to equalize: let the pressure inside match the pressure outside, so that the wall carries almost nothing. Animals living in the deep sea mostly do the second, by being made of water and having no large gas spaces; electronics on remote vehicles are often immersed in oil for the same reason. This sentence about animals and oil-filled housings is general background and is not sourced or checked on this page.

Statement
Resist: the load in the wallA thin spherical shell of radius R and wall thickness t, with outside pressure p, carries a compressive stress σ = pR/(2t) in its wall. Illustration with round numbers: R = 1 m, t = 0.1 m, p = 50 MPa give σ = 250 MPa.
Resist: when the wall buckles (classical sphere result)The classical buckling pressure of a perfect thin sphere with Young’s modulus E and Poisson ratio ν is pcr = 2E/√(3(1 − ν²)) · (t/R)², which is the stress σcr = E t/(R√(3(1 − ν²))), about 0.605 E t/R for ν = 0.3. Real shells fail below this value, because small imperfections lower the threshold; the factor by which they do is called the knockdown factor and is not given here.
EqualizeIf the pressure inside equals p, the net pressure load on the wall is zero, and the wall has to carry only its own weight and any difference in pressure that remains.
Derivation

The wall stress. Cut the sphere along an equator. The pressure presses on the disc of area πR² with total force pπR², and this is balanced by the stress σ acting on the ring of wall of cross-section 2πRt: pπR² = σ 2πRt, so σ = pR/(2t). For the numbers above, 50×106 × 1 / (2 × 0.1) = 250×106 Pa.

The threshold, by an energy balance. Suppose the wall develops a wrinkle w = w0 cos(kx). Three energies per unit area, each proportional to w0², compete. Bending the wall costs D k4 with D = E t3/(12(1 − ν²)); the radial displacement stretches the curved wall, which costs E t/R²; and the compressive stress does work σ t k² as the wall shortens. The wrinkle can grow when D k4 + E t/R² ≤ σ t k², that is when σ ≥ D k²/t + E/(R²k²). The right side is a sum of two positive terms whose product is DE/(tR²) for every k, so by the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality it is at least 2√(DE/t)/R, with equality when k4 = E t/(D R²). Putting in D, 2√(DE/t)/R = E t/(R√(3(1 − ν²))), and then p = 2tσ/R gives pcr = 2E/√(3(1 − ν²)) · (t/R)². □

What this derivation is. The wrinkle is a one-dimensional model standing in for the sphere’s mode. Its minimum agrees exactly with the classical constant, which I checked symbolically, but the agreement is a check against the known value; the sphere’s linearised shell equations are not solved here, so this is not a derivation from shell theory. The lesson that does not depend on the constant: pcr grows like (t/R)², so thickness helps quadratically, and a hull that is thin for its size is buckling-limited long before the material is.

Remarks

The script checks the force balance in exact arithmetic for the numbers above; checks, by a numerical scan over k, that the minimum of D k²/t + E/(R²k²) is 2√(DE/t)/R and sits where k4 = E t/(D R²), for three sets of values; and checks that the resulting stress equals E t/(R√(3(1 − ν²))), which is 0.6052 E t/R for ν = 0.3. In Lean, sphere_force_balance, wrinkle_stress_lower_bound, wrinkle_stress_attained, zoelly_algebra and zoelly_pressure state the force balance, the minimisation and the substitution; all compiled.

Left out: the shell equations and the proof of the classical constant; imperfection sensitivity; composite materials and fatigue; the design of any real vessel; and the biology. A pressure hull is a design problem with testing and certification, and nothing on this page is guidance for building or diving in one.

7 · Reading a prize citation

Remarks

A prize citation is a sentence written for a committee; it names an area and a few results, and it says nothing about proofs. What can be checked from it is who received it, when, and for which stated work, as in Part 1. What cannot be learned from it is how the results are proved or how they compare with what came before; for that the papers are the only source, and this page did not read them. The cited papers named on the pages read include, for the 2005 Bôcher Prize, work on blow-up for the critical generalized KdV equation (with Yvan Martel) and for the L2-critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation (with Pierre Raphaël), and for the 2023 prize the papers on the implosion of a compressible fluid and on blow-up for energy-supercritical defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger equations.

8 · Exercises

  1. Solve u′ = u2 with u(0) = 3 and find the time at which it blows up. Check that u(t)(T − t) = 1.
  2. Solve u′ = u3 with u(0) = 2 and find T. Check by differentiating u−2.
  3. Explain why u′ = −u2 with u(0) = 1 does not blow up in positive time. What does it tend to?
  4. Show that u′ = u1.5 blows up in finite time. (Hint: u−1/2 is linear.)
  5. For x′ = μ + x2 with μ = 4, compute the time for the solution to run from −∞ to +∞ and compare with π/√μ.
  6. For which μ does x′ = μ + x2 have an equilibrium at x = 1? What is the derivative of the right side there, and is that equilibrium steady or unsteady?
  7. For the linear equation i ut = −uxx, show that y″ = 8∫|ux|², and conclude that y grows at least like t² unless u is zero.
  8. With y(0) = 1, y′(0) = 0 and E = −1/8, find the time at which y(t) = 1 − t² reaches 0 and say what it means for the solution.
  9. A thin sphere has R = 2 m, t = 0.05 m and outside pressure 20 MPa. Find the wall stress pR/(2t). Then find what happens to the buckling pressure if t is doubled.
  10. Show that for a fixed wrinkle wavenumber the stress D k²/t + E/(R²k²) can be made as large as you like, and that it has a single minimum in k.
  11. Write one sentence on what a prize citation tells you about a result and one on what it does not.
