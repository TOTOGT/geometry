Linear algebra for graduate study

The linear algebra a reader needs to work alone in a graduate course, sit a qualifying exam, or understand what a numerical routine is doing, written with interactive plates and with a script behind every example.

Preface

This book is for a reader who has met matrices and small linear systems informally and now needs the subject proved. It aims to take that reader from vector spaces over a field to the spectral theorem, the singular value decomposition and the first ideas of numerical linear algebra, with the theory and the computation kept side by side.

It can be read straight through, used as the text for a course, or opened as a reference. It is also meant to be a way of preparing for the linear algebra part of a preliminary or qualifying exam, which is why the exercises matter as much as the chapters. Each section is written in layers you can switch on and off (intuition, statement, proof, remarks), so a reader can stop at the depth they need. Each chapter has a plate you can drag and push on, and a script that checks the chapter’s examples and formulas in exact arithmetic. Every check has a control that is built to fail, so a check that cannot fail does not count.

Every chapter opens by drawing the object it studies and saying in one line what that object is, and the drawing moves: you change it and watch what stays fixed. The proofs come after the reader has seen the thing, not before.

The reader is assumed to bring seriousness and some practice with proofs. No earlier formal linear algebra is assumed.

What this volume is, and is notIt is a new book written in this series’ own words. Its scope follows the standard graduate syllabus, and for that scope it uses Golan, The Linear Algebra a Beginning Graduate Student Ought to Know (2nd ed., Springer, 2007), as a reference; of that book only the contents list and the preface have been read so far. Nothing here is copied from it. From Chapter 3, the page-addressed text is Lax, Linear Algebra and Its Applications (2nd ed., Wiley, 2007), Chapter 1 so far; Lay (5th ed.) is held as a computational companion and not yet cited. A Lean file exists for Chapter 3 but has not been compiled, so nothing here is claimed as machine-checked.

The order and the chapter titles can still change. Chapters 1 to 10 and both interludes exist. The book goes back and forth: where a chapter has a nonlinear counterpart (Strogatz is the working reference for that half), it is placed beside the linear result, with a label saying whether the link is a definition, an approximation or a derivation.

How this connects to the rest of the series

Book XXI depends on linear algebra at a narrow point: its theorem that two spiral sinks with different μ are not similar is a statement about matrices up to a change of basis, which Chapter 1 here introduces in the plane. That is a reading order, not a claim of shared structure. Where a later chapter here meets an earlier one of the series, the link will say whether it is the same definition, a formal analogy, or a derivation, and a connection without one of those labels will not be drawn.

Book XXIV is not a rung of the floor ladder (docs/floor-ladder.tsv). From Chapter 3 on, chapters are meant to carry a Lean file; VectorSpace.lean has been compiled by the author.
