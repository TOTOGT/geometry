Does the maximum likelihood estimate converge?

Choose the parameter value that makes the data most probable. With enough data, does that choice home in on the truth? Under four conditions it does, and the proof is three lines. The conditions are exactly where an estimate of a steepness exponent can go wrong, which is why this chapter is tagged to the SBIR project: Aim 1 estimates an exponent by maximum likelihood, and Aim 2 asks what data would make that estimate mean anything.

The object. A simulated recovery curve μ(t) = tn/(tn + τn) with true exponent n = 2π/√3 = 3.6276 and τ = 1, observed with Gaussian noise of size 0.05 on k subjects at the observation times of a chosen design. The curve is the profile deviance of the exponent: how much worse each trial exponent explains the data than the best one. Where it stays below 3.84, the exponent is not ruled out at the 95% level. Change the design and watch the valley widen or close. The inflection time τ is searched only between 0.5 and 2, a restriction that keeps the plateau-only regions on the plot narrower than they would be with τ unrestricted.

1 · The statement

Intuition

The average log-likelihood of the data is a noisy picture of a fixed curve: the expected log-likelihood. That fixed curve peaks at the truth, and only there, because any other parameter describes a different distribution and a different distribution describes the data worse on average. As the sample grows the noisy picture settles onto the fixed curve, so its peak settles onto the truth. Everything that can go wrong is a failure of one of those three clauses: the peak is not unique, the picture does not settle uniformly, or the peak wanders off to the edge.

Statement
Wald’s theorem (standard)Let X1, X2, … be independent with the same density f(x; θ0), and let θ̂n maximize Mn(θ) = (1/n) Σ log f(Xi; θ) over a parameter set Θ. Suppose (a) Θ is compact; (b) f(x; θ) is continuous in θ; (c) supθ |log f(x; θ)| has finite expectation; (d) the model is identifiable: f(·; θ) ≠ f(·; θ0) as distributions for θ ≠ θ0. Then θ̂n → θ0 almost surely.
What the theorem does not giveIt does not give a rate, an interval or a sample size. A confidence interval needs more (asymptotic normality, which needs differentiability and a non-singular information matrix), and the width of that interval is governed by the information in the design, section 3.
Proof

The limit peaks at the truth. Put M(θ) = E log f(X; θ) with X drawn from f(·; θ0). Then M(θ) − M(θ0) = −KL(f0 ‖ fθ) ≤ 0 by Jensen’s inequality, since log is concave, and equality holds only if fθ = f0, which (d) rules out for θ ≠ θ0. The picture settles uniformly. By (a), (b) and (c), the uniform law of large numbers gives supθ |Mn(θ) − M(θ)| → 0 almost surely. The peak follows. Fix an open neighborhood U of θ0. On the compact set Θ ∖ U the continuous function M attains a maximum strictly below M(θ0); call the gap δ > 0. Once sup |Mn − M| < δ/2, every point outside U has Mn below Mn(θ0), so the maximizer lies in U. Since U was arbitrary, θ̂n → θ0. □

Remarks

Cramér’s version drops compactness and gives a consistent root of the likelihood equation rather than the global maximizer. Four standard failures: unbounded likelihood (a Gaussian mixture with a free variance), parameters that grow with the sample (Neyman–Scott), non-identifiability, and weak identification, where the parameter is identified but the likelihood is so flat that convergence is useless at any sample size one can collect.

2 · Watching it converge

Intuition

Add subjects one at a time at the design of your choice. The trace is the exponent that maximizes the likelihood of everything seen so far. It jumps about early and settles on the true value as subjects accumulate. Switch to a design that observes only the plateau or only the early rise and the trace still converges in principle, but far more slowly, because the data say little about the steepness.

3 · The design carries the information

Intuition

The steepness shows in how fast the curve climbs through its inflection. Observations before the rise and after the plateau both sit where the curve is nearly flat, so they cannot tell a steep curve from a gentle one. The information about the exponent is carried by the points near the inflection time, and a design that has none of them pays for it in subjects.

Statement
Worked numbers (idealised)With μ = tn/(tn+τn), n = 2π/√3, τ = 1, known noise σ = 0.05 and one shared inflection time, the information about n with τ profiled out is I = Inn − Inτ2/Iττ per subject. A 95% half-width of 0.5 needs about (1.96/0.5)2/I subjects.

How to read thisThese numbers are an illustration of how strongly the design matters, not a sample-size calculation for any cohort. They assume known noise, no between-subject variation and the exact Hill shape. Real recovery data have all three problems, so the true requirement is larger. The comparison across designs, a factor of about fifty between a design that spans the inflection and one that does not, is the point.
Proof

With yij = μ(tj; n, τ) + σεij, the Fisher information is Σj ∂μ/∂θ ∂μ/∂θT/σ² per subject. The derivatives are ∂μ/∂n = μ(1−μ) ln(t/τ) and ∂μ/∂τ = −μ(1−μ) n/τ. The factor μ(1−μ) is largest at the inflection and vanishes on both plateaus, which is the algebra behind the intuition above. The efficient information for n is the Schur complement. ch12-verify.py checks the derivatives against finite differences and recomputes the table.

Remarks

This is the formal version of the question Aim 2 asks. The earlier fits to six published aggregate curves returned exponents from 2.81 to 14.40: curves with few points and no pooling across subjects sit in exactly the weak-identification regime of the last two rows. When the sample grows by adding subjects, each with the same few visits, the observations are independent across subjects and Wald’s theorem applies directly; when it grows by adding visits to a single curve, the right reference is Jennrich’s theorem for nonlinear least squares, which needs the observation times to fill in the curve in a controlled way. Consistency does not give a rate or an interval by itself. The information in the table does that.
