Normal matrices: the norm is the spectral radius

The eigenvalues of a matrix usually say little about how large its entries can be: a matrix whose only eigenvalue is 1 can have an entry of 1000. For normal matrices that gap closes. The longest stretch of the matrix, its norm, equals the largest eigenvalue modulus, and since no entry can exceed the norm, no entry can exceed the largest eigenvalue modulus either. This chapter states that, shows why, breaks it on purpose, and explains why a real matrix needs its complex spectrum.

The object. A real 2×2 matrix A. The gold curve is the image of the unit circle under A, an ellipse; its longest semi-axis is the norm. The teal circle has radius equal to the spectral radius ρ, the largest modulus of an eigenvalue (complex ones included). For a normal matrix the teal circle touches the tip of the ellipse. For any other matrix it falls short.

1 · The statement

Intuition

Eigenvalues are the stretches along directions that A only scales. For most matrices those directions are tilted against each other, and a tilted pair can pile up stretching that no single eigenvalue shows: shear a square and the diagonal grows although the eigenvalues stay at 1. A normal matrix has perpendicular eigen-directions. Nothing piles up, and the largest eigenvalue modulus is the full story.

Statement
Normal matricesA square complex matrix A is normal if A*A = AA*. Real symmetric, skew-symmetric and orthogonal matrices are normal, and so is every matrix of the form [[a, −b], [b, a]] and every circulant.
Theorem (standard)If A is normal, then |A| = ρ(A), where |A| is the operator norm (the largest value of |Ax| over unit x) and ρ(A) is the largest modulus of an eigenvalue of A over the complex numbers.
Corollary: every entry is boundedIf A is normal and every eigenvalue μ has |μ| ≤ r, then |Aij| ≤ r for every i and j, the diagonal entries included.
Proof

Entries. Aij = ei*(Aej), a scalar product of two vectors, one of length 1 and one of length at most |A|. So |Aij| ≤ |A|. This holds for every matrix and uses no eigenvalue.

Norm equals spectral radius. For any matrix, |A*A| = |A|². If A is normal, (A²)*A² = (A*A)², and A*A is Hermitian, so |(A*A)²| = |A*A|² = |A|4. Hence |A²|² = |A|4, that is |A²| = |A|². The same step applies to A², which is again normal, so |A2k| = |A|2k. Gelfand’s formula (cited) says ρ(A) = lim |Am|1/m; along m = 2k the sequence is constantly |A|, so ρ(A) = |A|. Combining: |Aij| ≤ |A| = ρ(A) ≤ r.

Remarks

The bound |Aij| ≤ |A| is true for all matrices; the work normality does is to replace |A| by something computed from eigenvalues alone.

2 · Breaking it on purpose

Intuition

Slide t in the second picture. The matrix [[1, t],[0, 1]] has the single eigenvalue 1 for every t, so the spectral radius never moves. The norm grows like t and the entry t grows with it. At t = 0 the matrix is the identity and everything agrees. The moment t is not zero the matrix is no longer normal and the gap opens; once t passes 1 the entry is larger than every eigenvalue.

Statement
Controls[[1, t],[0, 1]] with t ≠ 0 is not normal, has ρ = 1, norm (t + √(t²+4))/2 and an entry t that exceeds ρ for t > 1. The nilpotent [[0, 1],[0, 0]] has A² = 0, so its only eigenvalue is 0, ρ = 0, yet its norm is 1 and one entry is 1.
Exhaustive countAmong the 2401 real 2×2 matrices with entries −3 to 3, exactly 385 are normal and 2016 are not. For all 385 normal ones the spectral radius equals the norm and every entry is at most ρ. Of the 2016 that are not normal, all 2016 have ρ strictly below the norm, and 872 have an entry larger than ρ. (ch11-verify.py, exact integer tests, 60-digit decimal for the square roots.)
Proof

For [[1,t],[0,1]] the characteristic polynomial is (λ−1)², so ρ = 1. Here F = 2 + t² and D = 1, so the norm squared is (F + √(F²−4))/2 = (t²+2+t√(t²+4))/2 = ((t+√(t²+4))/2)². The matrix is not normal because AAT has (1,1) entry 1 + t² while ATA has 1. For the nilpotent, A² = 0 forces every eigenvalue to be 0.

3 · Real matrices need the complex spectrum

Intuition

Rotate the plane by θ. Nothing except the zero angle and a half turn keeps any direction fixed, so the matrix has no real eigenvalue at all, yet it is perfectly normal and its entries cos θ and sin θ are bounded by 1. The eigenvalues e±iθ live off the real axis, on the unit circle. A statement about “the maximum over real eigenvalues” would be a maximum over the empty set. The bound has to be read in the complex plane.

Statement
The real caseFor a real matrix A, form its complexification (the same entries read as complex numbers). If that is normal and every eigenvalue μ in its complex spectrum has |μ| ≤ r, then |Aij| ≤ r. The 110 matrices [[a, −b],[b, a]] with b ≠ 0 and |a|, |b| ≤ 5 are all normal, all have negative discriminant (no real eigenvalue), and all satisfy the bound with ρ = √(a²+b²).
Proof

The characteristic polynomial of [[a,−b],[b,a]] is λ² − 2aλ + a² + b², with discriminant −4b² < 0, so the eigenvalues are a ± ib, of modulus √(a²+b²), which is at least max(|a|, |b|).

4 · What is not here

Remarks

A stronger statement is true for normal matrices: every diagonal entry lies in the convex hull of the eigenvalues, because Aii = Σk |Uik|²λk when A = U diag(λ) U*. That is the direct computation behind the Schur–Horn picture, and it is not proved in this chapter or in the Lean file; the corollary above is weaker and is what the file states. Off-diagonal entries are not in the convex hull in general, only bounded by ρ. The Lean file SpectralDiagonalBound.lean is uncompiled: it was written against the pinned Mathlib by reading its source and has never been run, and the first compile may fail on the instance for the matrix norm. Until the manual workflow has run and the axiom report exists, nothing here may be quoted as machine-checked.

5 · Exercises

  1. Show that a real symmetric matrix with eigenvalues 3 and −5 has every entry of absolute value at most 5, and find one where the bound is attained.
  2. Find a 2×2 integer matrix, not normal, whose eigenvalues are both 0 and whose entries are all nonzero. (Hint: rank one, trace zero.)
  3. Show that if A is normal and invertible then |A−1| = 1/(smallest eigenvalue modulus), and explain why this makes the condition number of a normal matrix the ratio of its largest to its smallest eigenvalue modulus (compare Chapter 9, section 3).
  4. For which θ does the rotation by θ have a real eigenvalue? What are the eigenvalues then, and do they satisfy the bound?
