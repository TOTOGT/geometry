Matrices, systems and determinants

A matrix is a linear map written down in a basis; multiplying matrices is composing maps. Elimination turns a system of equations into one you can read off. The determinant is the one number that says whether the map can be undone, and it multiplies when maps are composed.

The object. A 2×2 matrix A over Fp. Choose its four entries and read off its determinant ad − bc, its rank, whether it has an inverse, and what happens to the determinant when A is multiplied by itself. The button counts all p4 matrices and compares those with det ≠ 0 to the formula (p² − 1)(p² − p).

1 · A matrix is a linear map in a basis

Intuition

To know a linear map on Fn you only need to know what it does to the n unit vectors: everything else is a combination of those, and the map respects combinations. List the m coordinates of each image as a column and you have a table of numbers with n columns and m rows. Doing one map and then another is then a fixed recipe on the two tables, the product, and nothing more.

Statement
Correspondence (Lax, Chapter 4, pp.32–34)Linear maps T: Kn → Km correspond one to one with m×n matrices (tij): T is given by (Tx)i = Σj tij xj, and column j of the matrix is Tej.
Rule of matrix multiplication (Lax p.35)If T is m×n and S is l×m, the composed map ST has the l×n matrix with entries (ST)ik = Σj sij tjk. Equivalently, (AB)v = A(Bv) for every vector v.
Proof

Let A be the matrix of T (columns Tej) and x = Σj xjej. By linearity Tx = Σj xjTej, whose ith coordinate is Σj tijxj. Conversely that formula defines a linear map with columns Tej. For the product: the kth column of ST is S(Tek) = S(Σj tjkej) = Σj tjk Sej, whose ith coordinate is Σj sijtjk. □

Remarks

Lax pp.32–35. Lean: product_is_composition, proved by Mathlib’s Matrix.mulVec_mulVec; it checks the statement, not this proof, and the file compiles. The script checks: (AB)v = A(Bv) for every pair of 2×2 matrices over F2 (256 pairs), over F3 (6,561 pairs) and every pair of 3×3 matrices over F2 (262,144 pairs), each on every vector. Control: over F3, with A = [[1,1],[0,1]] and B = [[1,0],[1,1]], AB = [[2,1],[1,1]] and BA = [[1,1],[1,2]]; matrix multiplication is not commutative, which is why the order in the rule matters.

2 · Elimination, and what rank is

Intuition

To solve a system, you add multiples of one equation to another until each unknown can be read off, swapping equations when a leading coefficient is zero. None of these moves changes the set of solutions, because each can be undone. What is left is a staircase, and the number of steps is the rank.

Statement
Gaussian elimination (Lax pp.39–43)The three row operations (swap two equations, multiply one by a nonzero scalar, add a multiple of one to another) do not change the solution set of Ax = b. By these operations any matrix reaches echelon form; the number of pivots is the rank of A.
Row rank = column rank (Lax, Chapter 3, Theorem 6, p.27, and his remark on p.28)dim RT = dim RT′ for a linear map T and its transpose T′; Lax notes (p.28) that this is the abstract form of the equality of row rank and column rank. In matrix terms: the dimension of the span of the rows equals the dimension of the span of the columns, so A and its transpose have the same rank.
Worked example

Solve Ax = b with A = [[0,2,1],[1,1,1],[2,1,3]] and b = (4, 6, 11). The first pivot is zero, so swap rows 1 and 2: rows (1,1,1 | 6), (0,2,1 | 4), (2,1,3 | 11). Subtract 2×row 1 from row 3: (0,−1,1 | −1). Halve row 2: (0,1,½ | 2). Add row 2 to row 3: (0,0,3/2 | 1). Back-substitute: x3 = 2/3, x2 = 2 − (½)(2/3) = 5/3, x1 = 6 − 5/3 − 2/3 = 11/3. Check in the original equations: 0 + 10/3 + 2/3 = 4, 11/3 + 5/3 + 2/3 = 6, 22/3 + 5/3 + 6/3 = 11. For the right-hand side (5, 6, 13) the same steps give x = (7/3, 4/3, 7/3).

A singular case: [[1,2],[2,4]] has rank 1 (the second row is twice the first). The system with right-hand side (1, 2) is consistent and has a whole line of solutions; with (1, 3) the second equation contradicts the first and there is none. The proof that row rank equals column rank is Lax’s (Theorem 6, p.27); the script checks it, below, on every matrix of the stated sizes.

Remarks

Lean: rank_transpose_eq, Mathlib’s Matrix.rank_transpose. The file does not state elimination itself. The script solves the worked system in exact rational arithmetic (x = (11/3, 5/3, 2/3), one row swap) and by substitution, and checks row rank = column rank, together with |range| = prank, for every 2×3 and 3×2 matrix over F2 and F3 and every 3×3 matrix over F2 (64 + 64 + 729 + 729 + 512 matrices). This is the link to chapter 5: the range of a matrix has prank elements.

3 · The determinant

Intuition

Over the reals the determinant of an n×n matrix is the factor by which the map scales volume, with a sign for whether it flips orientation. Two things follow at once: composing two maps multiplies the factors, and a map that squashes volume to zero cannot be undone. Over a finite field there is no volume, but the algebra survives: a formula for det that is linear in each column, changes sign when two columns are swapped, and is 1 on the identity.

Statement
Definition (the Leibniz formula)det A = Σπ sign(π) a1π(1) a2π(2) … anπ(n), summed over all n! permutations π. Properties (Lax pp.44–48): it is linear in each column, changes sign when two columns are swapped, and det I = 1.
Theorem 2 (Lax p.49)For all n×n matrices A and B, det(BA) = det A · det B.
Corollary 3 and its converse (Lax p.51; over a field)A is invertible if and only if det A ≠ 0.
Count (not in Lax)The number of invertible n×n matrices over Fp is (pn − 1)(pn − p) … (pn − pn−1): 6 for n = 2, p = 2; 48 for n = 2, p = 3; 168 for n = 3, p = 2; 11,232 for n = 3, p = 3.
Proof

Theorem 2, in the 2×2 case, by expansion. Let A = [[a,b],[c,d]] and B = [[e,f],[g,h]]. Then BA = [[ea + fc, eb + fd],[ga + hc, gb + hd]], and (ea + fc)(gb + hd) − (eb + fd)(ga + hc) = eahd + fcgb − ebhc − fdga (the terms eagb and ebga, and fchd and fdhc, cancel in pairs) = (ad − bc)(eh − fg). In general both sides, as functions of the columns of A, are linear in each column, change sign when two columns are swapped, and agree (to det B) at A = I; such a function is unique up to its value at I, which is Lax’s argument on p.49.

Corollary 3. If AB = I then det A det B = det I = 1, so det A ≠ 0. Conversely, if det A ≠ 0 then A(adj A) = (det A)I (Section 4), so A has the inverse (det A)−1 adj A. □

The count. A matrix is invertible exactly when its columns form a basis, and the columns can be chosen one at a time: the first is any nonzero vector (pn − 1 choices), the second any vector outside the line of the first (pn − p), the third outside the plane of the first two (pn − p2), and so on.

Remarks

Lax pp.44–51. Lean: det_product, invertible_iff_det_ne_zero (Mathlib’s Matrix.det_mul and Matrix.isUnit_iff_isUnit_det), both compiled. The script checks the Leibniz formula against cofactor expansion on 200 random integer 4×4 matrices (fixed seed); det(BA) = det A det B for every pair of 2×2 matrices over F2 (256 pairs), F3 (6,561), F5 (390,625) and every pair of 3×3 over F2 (262,144); that a matrix has an inverse, found by search, exactly when det ≠ 0 (2×2 over F2 and F3, 3×3 over F2); the four counts above; and that swapping the rows of [[1,2],[3,4]] changes det from −2 to 2. Controls: det(I + I) = 4 but det I + det I = 2, so the determinant is not additive; and 3×3 over F3 has 11,232 matrices with det ≠ 0, not 39/2.

4 · Cramer’s rule and the inverse by cofactors

Intuition

The determinant also gives a closed formula for the solution of a square system. It is a good way to see how the answer depends on the data, and a poor way to compute one: the Leibniz formula has n! terms, and elimination does the same job in far fewer steps.

Statement
Cramer’s rule and Theorem 7 (Lax pp.53–54)If det A ≠ 0, the solution of Ax = b has xk = det Ak / det A, where Ak is A with column k replaced by b. The inverse is A−1 = adj(A) / det A, where adj(A)ij = (−1)i+j times the determinant of A with row j and column i removed.
Worked example

For the matrix of Section 2, det A = −3 and adj(A) = [[2,−5,1],[−1,−2,1],[−1,4,−2]]; multiplying out, A adj(A) = −3I. For b = (4, 6, 11), replacing columns of A by b in turn and dividing by −3 gives x = (11/3, 5/3, 2/3), the same answer elimination gave. The proof of the rule is Lax’s (p.54): expanding det Ak along column k and using that a determinant with two equal columns is zero.

Remarks

Lean: cramer_solves states A(cramer A b) = (det A)·b, from Mathlib’s Matrix.mulVec_cramer; it does not need det A ≠ 0, and it compiles. The script checks the example in exact arithmetic: Cramer’s quotients equal the elimination solution, A adj(A) = det A · I with the adjugate above, and A−1b = x. Control: [[1,2],[2,4]] has determinant 0, so the rule has nothing to divide by.

5 · Trace, and what similar matrices share

Intuition

Changing the basis changes the matrix of a map to P A P−1. Quantities that mean something about the map itself, not the basis, must survive that change. The determinant does, by Theorem 2. The sum of the diagonal entries does too, because it does not care about the order of a product.

Statement
Theorems 8 and 9 (Lax pp.55–56)tr A = Σ aii is linear, and tr(AB) = tr(BA). Similar matrices, A and P A P−1 with P invertible, have the same determinant and the same trace.
Proof

tr(AB) = Σi Σk aikbki, and swapping the names of the summation indices gives Σk Σi bkiaik = tr(BA). Then tr(P A P−1) = tr(A P−1P) = tr A, and det(P A P−1) = det P det A det P−1 = det A, because det P det P−1 = det I = 1. □

Remarks

Lean: trace_comm (Matrix.trace_mul_comm) and similar_det (Matrix.det_conj), both compiled. The script checks: tr(AB) = tr(BA) for all 262,144 pairs of 3×3 matrices over F2, and, over F3, that P A P−1 has the same determinant and trace as A for all 48 invertible P and all 81 matrices A (3,888 cases). Controls: tr(AB) is not tr A · tr B (for A = B = I: 2 against 4); and the converse of Theorem 9 is false: over F3, I and [[1,1],[0,1]] have the same determinant and trace, but every conjugate of I is I itself, so the two are not similar.

6 · Which fields

Remarks

Lax works over the reals or complex numbers, and his motivation for the determinant is volume. The algebra in Sections 1 to 5 uses only that the entries lie in a field (the Leibniz formula, Theorem 2 and the trace results hold over any commutative ring; the equivalence in Corollary 3 needs a field; over a general commutative ring the condition is that det A is a unit). The finite-field checks therefore say something about those statements and nothing about the volume interpretation, which needs the reals.

7 · Exercises

  1. Multiply the matrices of Section 1, [[1,1],[0,1]] and [[1,0],[1,1]], in both orders over the integers, and find a pair of 2×2 matrices that does commute and is not diagonal.
  2. Solve the system of Section 2 with right-hand side (1, 0, 0) by elimination, then by Cramer’s rule, then by multiplying by the adjugate divided by −3. The three answers must agree; the third column of A−1 comes out as the solution for (0, 0, 1).
  3. Over F2, list the six invertible 2×2 matrices and verify that their determinants are all 1. Why must the determinant of an invertible matrix over F2 be 1?
  4. Show that a 2×2 matrix over Fp is invertible exactly when its columns are linearly independent (neither is zero and neither is a multiple of the other), and count them: (p² − 1)(p² − p). Compare with the plate for p = 5 (480).
  5. Prove det(P A P−1) = det A in one line from Theorem 2.
  6. Find two 2×2 matrices over the rationals with the same determinant and trace that are not similar, other than the pair in Section 5.
  7. Rank–nullity (chapter 5) says dim N + dim R = n. For the singular matrix [[1,2],[2,4]], find N and R and check it.
  8. Compute the determinant of a 3×3 matrix by the Leibniz formula (six terms) and by elimination, and count the multiplications each takes.
