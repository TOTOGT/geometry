Order finding and the arithmetic of Shor

The reduction, the distribution, the continued-fraction step and the classical lemma, each simulated or enumerated and compared with the paper.

Shor, 1994/1997; Nielsen & Chuang §5.3

Part I · The reduction

Factoring is order finding, and most choices of a work

To factor an odd composite N, pick a coprime to N and let r be the order of a mod N. If r is even and ar/2 ≢ −1 (mod N), then (ar/2 − 1)(ar/2 + 1) ≡ 0 (mod N) with neither factor ≡ 0, so gcd(ar/2 ± 1, N) are nontrivial divisors. Shor's lemma says a random a fails with probability at most 1/2k−1, where k is the number of distinct odd prime factors: the multiplicative group mod a prime power is cyclic, so each prime power has probability at most 1/2 of giving any particular power of 2 dividing its order, and the algorithm fails only when all k powers agree. The quantum computer's only job is to find r.

Part II · The quantum step

What the measurement distribution is

Take q with N² ≤ q < 2N². Prepare Σx|x〉|ax mod N〉, measure the second register and get ak for some k < r: the first register is now uniform over x ≡ k (mod r). A QFT of size q and a measurement give c with probability

P(c, k)  =  | (1/q) sum_{x = k mod r}  exp(2 pi i x c / q) |^2      =  | (1/q) sum_{b=0}^{floor((q-k-1)/r)} exp(2 pi i b {rc}_q / q) |^2

where {rc}q is the residue of rc mod q in (−q/2, q/2]. The second form is a geometric sum, peaked when {rc}q is small, i.e. when c/q is close to a multiple of 1/r. Shor shows P(c, k) ≥ 4/(π²r²), at least 1/(3r²) for large N, whenever |{rc}q| ≤ r/2. In that case |c/q − d/r| ≤ 1/(2q) for some d, and because q > N² at most one fraction with denominator below N is that close, so continued fractions of c/q find d/r in lowest terms; if gcd(d, r) = 1 that reveals r. There are rφ(r) such good (c, k) pairs, giving success probability at least φ(r)/(3r).

Part III · The part you can check

Shor's own example, and three more

The checker simulates the periodic state and its DFT exactly for four cases (the first is Shor's Figure 5.1 example: N = 33, a = 5, r = 10, q = 256), and checks that the squared amplitudes sum to 1 and equal the closed form to 10−9.

The N = 33 row uses q = 256, below N² = 1089, because that is Shor's plotting example (his text says q is taken smaller “so as to make the values of c in the plot distinguishable”); the other three satisfy N² ≤ q < 2N². With q = 16 for N = 33 the same computation recovers r with probability 0.0000: the condition on q is not decoration.

The classical half, exhaustively. For all 110 odd composite non-prime-power N ≤ 400, the failing fraction of a never exceeds 1/2k−1, and it reaches it (worst ratio 1.000), so Shor's bound is tight, not merely true. Over 956 pairs (N, a) for twenty odd composites, a factor comes out exactly when r is even and ar/2 ≢ −1, and the factors found divide N (708 gave factors). Controls: q = 16; the 106 pairs with odd order, where ar/2 is not an integer; N = 15, a = 14, where ar/2 = −1; and using period r+1 in the closed form no longer matches the simulation.

What this does not show. It simulates the QFT step on a classical machine for N up to 35; it says nothing about the cost of modular exponentiation on a quantum computer (Shor's O(l² log l log log l) estimate is quoted, not checked), about noise, or about running Shor at cryptographic sizes. Nor does it treat the discrete-logarithm half of Shor's paper.
