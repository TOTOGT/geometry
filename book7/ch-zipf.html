George Kingsley Zipf

Rank times frequency is roughly constant: the commonest word is about twice as frequent as the second, three times the third. This page does not test that on a corpus. It shows what a pure random-typing model produces, which is also a power law with an exponent slightly above 1, and says what that does and does not imply.

The man and the law, in cited facts

CITED RESULTCITED George Kingsley Zipf (1902 to 1950) was an American linguist at Harvard. His books The Psycho-Biology of Language (1935) and Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort (1949) set out the regularity now named for him: if the words of a text are ranked by frequency, the frequency of the word of rank $r$ falls roughly as $1/r$. He explained it by a principle of least effort, balancing the speaker's economy against the listener's. That the law holds roughly across many languages and texts is a widely repeated empirical claim. No corpus was used for this page, so it is cited and not checked.

A model with no meaning in it

Let a typist press one of $k$ letters, each with probability $(1-s)/k$, or a space with probability $s$, independently each time. Cut the output at spaces. A given word of length $L$ then has relative frequency proportional to $q^L$ with $q = (1-s)/k$, and there are $k^L$ words of length $L$. Rank every possible word by frequency. The words of length $L$ occupy ranks $N(L-1)+1$ to $N(L)$, where $N(L) = k + k^2 + \cdots + k^L$.

THEOREMPROVED Between consecutive length blocks the slope of log-frequency against log-rank tends to $$\alpha = 1 + \frac{\ln\bigl(1/(1-s)\bigr)}{\ln k},$$ so the model gives a power law with exponent above 1 for every $s > 0$, and the exponent tends to 1 as the alphabet grows. Proof: the block of length $L$ has frequency $\propto q^L$ and its ranks run from about $k^L/(k-1)$ to $k^{L+1}/(k-1)$; the ratio of geometric midpoints of consecutive blocks tends to $k$, and $\ln(q^{L}/q^{L+1}) / \ln k = \ln(1/q)/\ln k = (\ln k + \ln(1/(1-s)))/\ln k$.

CITED RESULTCITED This random-typing argument is usually credited to George A. Miller (1957, “Some effects of intermittent silence”) and is closely related to work of Benoit Mandelbrot. It is cited here and was not checked against those papers.

CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED For $k = 26$ and $s = 0.2$ the limit is $\alpha = 1.0685$, and the slopes in the table, computed from exact block boundaries, reach it by length 3 and approach it from below. A seeded simulation of 3,000,000 typed characters gives 479,466 word tokens and 260,594 distinct words; the least-squares slope of log-frequency against log-rank over ranks 1 to 1000 is 1.0315, within the tolerance of 0.15 around $\alpha$ that was fixed before the run. As a control, equal frequencies give slope 0. The agreement is loose by design: inside a block the sampled counts scatter, and 1.0315 is not claimed to equal 1.0685.

What this does and does not say

A text with no meaning, no speaker and no listener already has a rank-frequency law of Zipf's form. So the appearance of a power law in a real language does not by itself show a principle of least effort, which is the point usually drawn from the random-typing model. Whether real vocabularies deviate from the monkey model in ways that carry information is a separate question about data.

OPEN QUESTIONOPEN What produces the exponent seen in natural-language corpora, and whether a single mechanism does, is not settled on this page. Nothing here says that language is random, or that the typist model is a theory of speech. It is a null model, and a power law is the null result.
