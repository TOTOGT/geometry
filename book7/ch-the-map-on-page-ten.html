The Map on Page Ten

In 1994 Strogatz drew the entire subject on a single page — two axes, ten cells, sixty-one named systems, and one region he labelled The frontier. This corpus had never been placed on that page. Placing it took an afternoon and cost one instrument.

The page itself

Open Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos at printed page 10. There are no equations on it. There is one figure, captioned Figure 1.3.1, and the figure is the book — not its table of contents, which is elsewhere, but its argument about where difficulty lives. Two axes. Along the top, the number of variables needed to fix the state of a system: n = 1, n = 2, n ≥ 3, n >> 1, Continuum. Down the side, two rows only: Linear and Nonlinear. Ten cells. Into them Strogatz drops sixty-one named systems, from RC circuit in the top-left corner to Life in the bottom-right, and across the bottom-right he writes The frontier.

He explains the joke on the next page. “It’s like in those old maps of the world, where the mapmakers wrote, ‘Here be dragons’ on the unexplored parts of the globe.” That is the whole method of the figure: it is a map drawn so that the blank parts are visible as blank. A table of contents cannot do that. A table of contents has no empty cells.

What the two axes actually assert

The horizontal axis is not a difficulty scale. It is the dimension of the phase space, and Strogatz is explicit that this is the same number twice over: “Equivalently, this number is the dimension of the phase space.” The vertical axis is not a difficulty scale either — it is whether superposition holds.

The claim of the figure is that difficulty is the product of the two, and that the product is badly behaved: the Continuum column, with infinitely many variables, holds Maxwell's equations and the heat equation and is tractable, while the bottom-left cell, with one variable, is where the book chooses to begin. Dimension alone predicts nothing. Linearity alone predicts nothing. Only the cell does.

Reading a figure through a text extractor

Placing anything on this figure means knowing which cell each of the sixty-one entries is in, and the only machine-readable copy to hand is a PDF. That turns out to be the first of two measurement problems on this page, and it is worth stating before any number is quoted, because it decides which numbers this chapter is allowed to publish.

The figure is set rotated on the page. Its reading-order horizontal direction is the PDF's y coordinate and its vertical direction is the PDF's x, so every glyph carries its position and the grid can be recovered from the glyph positions rather than guessed. Two hundred and thirty-six positioned text runs make up the page. What they settle:

So a figure read through a text extractor loses precisely the information the figure exists to carry. Position is the content here; the strings are captions. Fifty-three of the sixty-one entries therefore have no column this chapter is entitled to publish, and none is published. What is published is the row — which the glyph bands settle outright — and the eight columns Strogatz states in words.

The class labels, placed by coordinate

One structural feature does survive. Six italicised phrases mark where a broad class of behaviour first arises, and each is a short run with its own position. Calibrating on the four whose column Strogatz confirms in prose — growth and decay at n = 1, oscillations at n = 2, chaos at n ≥ 3, and the linear partial differential equations in the Continuum column — a label sits about 23 units of y before the column it heads, with a spread of 14 to 28 across the four. Applying that offset to the labels prose does not place, and taking the nearest column marker:

label side y column it heads Growth, decay, or equilibrium Linear 227.8 n = 1 (prose) Oscillations Linear 303.7 n = 2 (prose) Collective phenomena Linear 478.4 n >> 1 (offset) Waves and patterns Linear 583.3 Continuum (prose) Chaos Nonlinear 392.7 n >= 3 (prose) Spatio-temporal complexity Nonlinear 585.8 Continuum (offset) The frontier Nonlinear 444.7 straddles n >= 3 / n >> 1

Three cells carry no class label at all: linear n ≥ 3, and nonlinear n = 1 and n = 2. Nothing first arises there, which is consistent with what the labels are for and is a small independent check that the offset is doing real work.

The corpus, placed

This corpus has thirteen volumes and several hundred chapters and has never once been laid over Figure 1.3.1 — which is strange, because the corpus is a nonlinear-dynamics corpus, the figure is the standard chart of that subject, and ch-strogatz already establishes that the attractor the whole framework is built on is Strogatz's own Example 7.1.1 with a third coordinate added.

Forty-four of sixty-one entries occupied, seventeen at zero. That is better coverage than the corpus deserved, and the distribution is the interesting part. Eight of the seventeen zeros are in the linear half — the half with only eighteen entries in it — and they are not exotic topics: RC circuit, RLC circuit, mass and spring, 2-body problem, coupled harmonic oscillators, equilibrium statistical mechanics, radioactive decay, viscous fluids. A corpus that can compute a Conley index and cannot point at an RC circuit has skipped a floor. That floor is WP-82's subject, and Figure 1.3.1 finds it in one pass because Strogatz put those systems on the page precisely so the rest of the page would have a scale.

The two zeros in that table are the two systems Strogatz uses to explain the horizontal axis: “an RC circuit has n = 1 and cannot oscillate, whereas an RLC circuit has n = 2 and can oscillate.” Of the eight entries in the figure whose cell its author bothered to state in words, the only two this corpus cannot say anything about are the pair that make the axis mean something. The other six read 260, 119, 71, 69, 6, 3, and the two largest are both at n = 1 in the nonlinear row — the corner page 11 prescribes as the place to start: “in this book we start in the lower left corner and systematically head to the right.” Nobody instructed this corpus to obey that sentence. Its density profile obeys it anyway.

The instrument that broke

Then Plasmas returned 93, and the reading stopped.

Ninety-three would make plasma physics the corpus's fourth-largest subject, ahead of chaos. It is not. Opening the files: plasma cells in the immunology chapters, blood plasma, plasma membrane, a navigation link reading Plasma, and — legitimately — stellar plasma and plasma drag in the Enceladus work. The pattern was correctly spelled, correctly anchored, entity-aware, and measuring the wrong thing.

A fourth failure mode of this corpus's counting instrument

WP-82 §4 records three ways git grep -il lies about this corpus: wrong spelling (k-theory misses $K$-theory), substring inflation (gns returns 68 on designs and assignments), and HTML entities (Li&eacute;nard matches neither spelling of the name). All three produce a number a reader can see is wrong.

This is the fourth, and it is the only one with no symptom: sense collision. The string is right, the anchor is right, the entity handling is right, the count is arithmetically correct — and the referent is a different subject. Figure 1.3.1 is what exposed it, because the figure's cells are senses and not strings. Every previous count this corpus published was a string count wearing a sense's name.

Seven entries were then audited by companion pattern: a file counts toward the figure's entry only if it also carries a term that fixes the dynamical sense, and against it if it carries one that fixes another. The audit is weaker than reading every page, and the script says so, but it is reproducible and it is printed:

Life is the figure's own punchline and the corpus's largest false positive at once. Strogatz put Life in the bottom-right corner, inside The frontier, as the hardest named thing on the page. The corpus returns 151 files for it, and 140 of those are the ordinary English word in ordinary sentences. Eleven files are about the origin of life, which is the entry Strogatz meant, and eleven is the honest number.

And then the audit failed the same way, on its first run

The first companion pattern written for Plasmas accepted a file as physics if it contained any of magnetohydrodynam, tokamak, stellar plasma, plasma drag, reconnect, fusion, ionis, ioniz. It returned 83 of 93 — which would have promoted the false positive into a verified count.

Printing the per-term breakdown instead of the total showed why: reconnect matched 304 times and fusion 43, almost none of them magnetic reconnection or nuclear fusion, while tokamak matched twice and stellar plasma once. The audit instrument had failed by substring inflation — WP-82's second failure mode — while auditing the fourth. It was caught because the script printed a breakdown and not a number. That is the transferable lesson of this page, and it is worth more than the tables above it.

Two patterns there are worth naming. First, several entries are occupied by person and empty by phrase: the corpus has written Smale and Levinson and van der Pol and cannot say forced nonlinear oscillator or anharmonic. It knows the people and not the vocabulary, which is the exact inverse of the usual failure and is consistent with a corpus built by reading biographies. Second, the linear row's zeros are the first systems in an undergraduate course. Both facts took one afternoon to find and neither was visible from inside the corpus.

What is missing from this page

No column is published for fifty-three of the sixty-one entries, and the reason is a limitation of the extractor, not of the figure: someone reading the printed page can see every cell at once. The class-label offset is calibrated on four labels whose spread is 14 to 28 units and applied to three, which is a small sample for a rule; it survives only because the columns are roughly 80 units apart, so an error of ten does not change which column is nearest. The counts measure files that mention a thing, not files about a thing: fixed points at 260 does not mean 260 chapters of fixed-point theory. The sense audit covers seven entries; the other fifty-four have not been audited, and on the evidence of these seven there is no reason to assume they are clean. And the figure comes from a textbook's overview chapter and is offered by its author as debatable — “some aspects of the picture are debatable” — so a zero in an entry is a fact about one book's taxonomy and this corpus, not a fact about the world.

Scholium — scope of this page

Nothing here is a result about dynamical systems. It is a measurement of this corpus against one figure in one textbook, and the only general claims are about instruments: that a figure read through a text extractor loses the position that is its content, and that a count can be correctly spelled, correctly anchored, entity-aware and still measure a different subject, with no property of the count revealing it. The seventeen zeros are zeros in the spellings tried, per R15 — the corpus has repositories this measurement did not read, and an entry at zero here may be written elsewhere. The sense audit is a co-occurrence rule, not a reading of the pages; its numbers are weaker than the string counts they correct, and both are printed rather than reconciled. No priority is claimed for anything: Figure 1.3.1 is Strogatz's, published 1994.
