Rudolf Lipschitz

His name is on a single inequality that says how fast a function may stretch. Put a ceiling below 1 on that rate and iteration is forced to settle on one answer. This page builds that from a chapter of numerical-analysis notes and checks the notes’ worked example.

14 May 1832, Königsberg – 7 October 1903, Bonn · sourced from one footnote
How this chapter is sourced One document: Chapter 1, Solution of equations by iteration, a PDF of course notes filed under the name ‘suli’ on the NYU MATH-UA 252 (spring 2022) course page. It is the only source for what is said below, about the man and the mathematics alike. The PDF does not print its book title or authors in the text I extracted, so I do not give them. This is not Robert Lipshitz (no c), the knot theorist of the previous chapters; the two names look alike and nothing here connects them.

1 · The man, as far as the source says

The notes give him a footnote: Rudolf Otto Sigismund Lipschitz, born 14 May 1832 in Königsberg, Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia), died 7 October 1903 in Bonn. It says he made important contributions to number theory, the theory of Bessel functions and Fourier series, ordinary and partial differential equations, and analytical mechanics and potential theory. That is everything this chapter will say about his life. A chapter with more sources would say more.

2 · The condition

Definition (source, Def 1.2 and Remark 1.2)
Lipschitz condition, and contraction

A function $g$ on an interval $[a,b]$ satisfies a Lipschitz condition with constant $L > 0$ if

|g(x) - g(y)| <= L · |x - y| for all x, y in [a, b]

When $0 < L < 1$ the function is a contraction: the distance between two images is at most $L$ times the distance between the points, so every pair of points is pulled closer together.

3 · Why a ceiling below 1 forces one answer

Theorem (source, Thm 1.3)
Contraction mapping theorem on an interval

Let $g$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ with $g(x) \in [a,b]$ for all $x$, and a contraction there. Then $g$ has a unique fixed point $\xi$ in $[a,b]$, and for every start $x_0 \in [a,b]$ the iteration $x_{k+1} = g(x_k)$ converges to $\xi$.

Uniqueness. If $\xi$ and $\eta$ were both fixed, then $|\xi - \eta| = |g(\xi) - g(\eta)| \le L|\xi - \eta|$, so $(1-L)|\xi - \eta| \le 0$; since $1 - L > 0$, $\eta = \xi$.

Convergence. $|x_k - \xi| = |g(x_{k-1}) - g(\xi)| \le L|x_{k-1} - \xi|$, and by induction $|x_k - \xi| \le L^k |x_0 - \xi|$, which tends to 0 because $L < 1$.

The source gets existence of a fixed point on the interval from Brouwer’s fixed point theorem (its Theorem 1.2). The uniqueness and the rate need only the Lipschitz condition. The source does not say who first proved the theorem, and this chapter does not either.

4 · The worked example, checked

The source solves $e^x - 2x - 1 = 0$ on $[1,2]$ by iterating $g(x) = \ln(2x+1)$. It checks the hypotheses by hand: $g(1) = 1.0986\ldots$ and $g(2) = 1.6094\ldots$ lie in $[1,2]$; $g'(x) = 2/(2x+1)$ is decreasing, since $g'' = -4/(2x+1)^2 < 0$, so on $[1,2]$ it ranges over $[2/5, 2/3]$. By the mean value theorem $|g(x) - g(y)| \le \tfrac23 |x - y|$, so $L = 2/3$ and the iteration converges from any start in $[1,2]$. Starting from $x_0 = 1$ the source prints these iterates:

ch-rudolf-lipschitz-verify.py reproduces all twelve to the six printed decimals, using 60-digit arithmetic, and checks the slope range exactly. It solves for $\xi$ to 50 digits, getting $\xi = 1.256431208626\ldots$, and confirms the error bound $|x_k - \xi| \le (2/3)^k |x_0 - \xi|$ for $k = 0, \ldots, 40$. It also notes that the observed ratio of successive errors is about $0.569$, which equals $g'(\xi)$: the bound $2/3$ is safe but the iteration is faster than the bound says.

The controls A bound that holds for the right $L$ proves nothing unless it can fail. Two controls: the same inequality with $L = 0.3$ fails, and a corrupted table entry is detected. Two more show the hypotheses matter: $g(x) = x$ on $[1,2]$ has ratio exactly 1 and every point is fixed, so $L = 1$ gives no uniqueness; and $\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,1]$ has difference quotients at 0 that grow without bound ($3.16$ at $h = 10^{-1}$ to $316228$ at $h = 10^{-11}$), so it satisfies no Lipschitz condition there.

5 · What the condition does not need

The source points out that a Lipschitz function need not be differentiable. $g(x) = |x|$ satisfies the condition with $L = 1$ on any closed interval and has slope $-1$ on one side of 0 and $+1$ on the other; the verifier prints exactly those two difference quotients. It adds, in a footnote, that a function satisfying the Lipschitz condition on an interval is differentiable except possibly on a set of Lebesgue measure zero, and names this Rademacher’s theorem. I did not check that statement.

6 · Beside the Lean file

The series has a Lean file, book5/ExistenceBanach.lean, that proves an existence-and-uniqueness statement by exactly this route: the map is shown to be a contraction with constant $r < 1$, and Mathlib’s ContractingWith and LipschitzWith supply the unique fixed point and the convergence of the iterates. It works in a complete metric space instead of an interval, where completeness takes the place of Brouwer’s theorem for existence; that generalisation is standard and is not in the source above. That file has not been compiled by anyone yet. Nothing on this page is evidence that it compiles. The existing page Book 5, Chapter V is a neighbour; I have not re-read it for this chapter.
