Richard Montague

Treat a sentence of English like a formula: build its meaning from the meanings of its parts by function application, and let “every” and “a” denote operations on sets. This page runs that idea on a model with three men and two women, shows that “every man loves a woman” has two readings that differ in 12 of the 64 possible worlds, and checks one structural property of quantifiers.

The man, in cited facts

CITED RESULTCITED Richard Montague (1930 to 1971) was an American logician and philosopher at UCLA. In the papers “English as a Formal Language” and “Universal Grammar” (both 1970) and “The Proper Treatment of Quantification in Ordinary English” (1973) he argued that natural language can be given a precise syntax and a model-theoretic semantics of the same kind as a formal language, and gave a fragment of English as a worked case. Titles and dates are from memory of the literature and were not checked for this page; the approach is usually called Montague grammar.

Quantifiers as operations on sets

Let a model have a set of entities and a set for each noun and verb. A determiner such as “every” denotes a function that takes the set $P$ of the noun and the set $Q$ of the predicate and returns true or false: $\mathrm{every}(P)(Q)$ is true when $P \subseteq Q$, and $\mathrm{a}(P)(Q)$ is true when $P \cap Q \ne \varnothing$. A sentence is built by applying such functions to the denotations of its parts.

One sentence, two meanings

Take three men, two women, and a relation loves from men to women, which can be any of $2^6 = 64$ relations. “Every man loves a woman” is built two ways. In the surface reading, each man loves some woman: $\mathrm{every}(\mathrm{man})(\lambda x.\ \mathrm{a}(\mathrm{woman})(\lambda y.\ \mathrm{loves}(x,y)))$. In the inverse reading, there is one woman whom every man loves: $\mathrm{a}(\mathrm{woman})(\lambda y.\ \mathrm{every}(\mathrm{man})(\lambda x.\ \mathrm{loves}(x,y)))$.

THEOREMPROVED The counts follow from inclusion–exclusion. The surface reading needs each of 3 men to love at least one of 2 women, which is $(2^2 - 1)^3 = 27$ relations. The inverse reading needs some woman loved by all three men: $2 \cdot 2^3 - 1 = 15$ relations, since each woman loved by all fixes three pairs and leaves $2^3$ free, and the case where both women are loved by all is counted twice and so subtracted once. The inverse reading implies the surface reading, because a woman loved by every man is a woman each man loves, and not conversely.

CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED All 64 relations were enumerated. The compositionally built readings agree with the first-order sentences “for all $x$ there is $y$” and “there is $y$ for all $x$” in every case. The counts are 27 and 15, the inverse reading entails the surface reading in all 64, and 12 relations separate them. The page's own table is compared with this output.

A property of natural quantifiers

CITED RESULTCITED A determiner $Q$ is called conservative when $Q(A,B)$ and $Q(A, A \cap B)$ always agree, so that only the part of $B$ inside $A$ matters. Barwise and Cooper (1981) proposed that every natural-language determiner is conservative; the claim is cited here and not checked against their paper.

CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED Six determiners, “every”, “a”, “no”, “exactly two”, “at least two” and “most”, are conservative on all $16 \times 16 = 256$ pairs of subsets of a four-element set. The control “only”, read as $B \subseteq A$, is not conservative, which is the standard reason it is treated as an exception. A check on one finite domain is not a proof for all domains.

What is not shown

OPEN QUESTIONOPEN Whether a compositional model-theoretic semantics of this kind covers natural language in general is not settled on this page. It shows one sentence and one set of quantifiers in one small model. Ambiguity, context, tense and the rest of meaning are not touched, and nothing here is a parser or a corpus result. For the other side of formal linguistics, rule systems that generate structure, see ch-chomsky.
