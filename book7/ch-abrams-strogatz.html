Abrams and Strogatz

Two languages compete for speakers, and the one with higher status gains. The 2003 model of Daniel Abrams and Steven Strogatz is a single differential equation with a tipping point: above a threshold share, one language takes over, and below it the other does. This page solves for the threshold, proves which states are stable, and checks it numerically. It also reports, with its source, how many of the world's languages are in danger.

The model, as read from the paper

CITED RESULTCITED D. M. Abrams and S. H. Strogatz, “Modelling the dynamics of language death”, Nature 424 (2003) 900. Let $x$ be the fraction of a population speaking language X and $1-x$ the fraction speaking Y, and let $s \in [0,1]$ be the relative status of X. The paper's minimal model is $\dot x = (1-x)P_{yx} - xP_{xy}$, with the rate at which Y speakers switch to X equal to $c\,x^a s$ and the reverse rate equal to $c\,(1-x)^a(1-s)$. The paper fits the exponent to historical data and reports $a \approx 1.31 \pm 0.25$, roughly constant across the cases it studied, and concludes that two such languages cannot coexist stably. It also describes an extension with feedback on status in which a bilingual state can be stabilized. This summary was read from the authors' paper in this session; the fit was not repeated.

The tipping point, proved

Take $c = 1$, which only sets the time scale. Then $\dot x = f(x) = s\,x^a(1-x) - (1-s)(1-x)^a x = x(1-x)\bigl[s\,x^{a-1} - (1-s)(1-x)^{a-1}\bigr]$.

THEOREMPROVED For $a > 1$ and $0 < s < 1$, the equation has exactly one interior fixed point, $$x^* = \frac{1}{1 + \bigl(s/(1-s)\bigr)^{1/(a-1)}},$$ it is unstable, and $x = 0$ and $x = 1$ are stable. For $a < 1$ the interior point is stable and $0$ and $1$ are unstable. For $a = 1$, $f(x) = (2s-1)\,x(1-x)$ and there is no interior fixed point unless $s = 1/2$. Proof. In the bracket, $s\,x^{a-1}$ is increasing in $x$ and $(1-s)(1-x)^{a-1}$ is decreasing when $a > 1$, so the bracket increases from $-(1-s)$ at $x = 0$ to $s$ at $x = 1$ and has one zero. Setting it to zero gives $(x/(1-x))^{a-1} = (1-s)/s$, which solves to $x^*$. The bracket is negative below $x^*$ and positive above, so $f < 0$ below and $f > 0$ above: trajectories leave $x^*$ and run to $0$ or $1$. When $a < 1$ both monotonicities reverse and the signs flip. The case $a = 1$ is direct algebra.

CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED On a grid of $s$ from 0.05 to 0.95 and $a \in \{1.1, 1.31, 2, 3\}$ the sign pattern of $f$ on $(0,1)$ is negative below $x^*$ and positive above it, and the closed form matches bisection to $10^{-10}$ on the 68 pairs where the root is not too close to an endpoint. For $a \in \{0.5, 0.8\}$ the pattern reverses. For $a = 1.31$ and $s = 0.55$, $x^* = 0.3436$: a Runge–Kutta integration started 0.02 below it ends at 0 and one started 0.02 above it ends at 1. With equal status, $x^* = 1/2$. A higher status for X lowers the share it needs.

The number behind the model

CITED RESULTCITED A 2018 study by Frank Seifart, Nicholas Evans, Harald Hammarström and Stephen Levinson, published in Language, found that just over half of the roughly 7,000 living languages, about 3,660, were classed as threatened, endangered, moribund or nearly extinct, drawing on three existing catalogues (the Catalogue of Endangered Languages, UNESCO's Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger, and Ethnologue). The figure was read from a university press release in this session, not from the paper, and it is from 2018: a more recent count was not retrieved, and the classification depends on how “endangered” is defined.

What the model does and does not say

The model says that if two languages compete with an exponent above 1, a small status edge becomes a takeover once a population crosses the tipping share. It does not say why the exponent is 1.31, and it leaves out bilingual speakers, schools, migration and policy, which are the usual levers in real cases and were not modelled here. Read as a statement about the standardizing pull of any common language, it is only as strong as the claim that status works the way the equation says.

OPEN QUESTIONOPEN Whether new communication technologies shift “status” in the sense of this model, and so move the tipping point for some languages, is not established on this page and no data on it was examined. Whether the paper's fitted exponent holds for any given language pair is also open. This model shares its two-attractor shape with other tipping-point models in the corpus; that is a shared form of equation and not a shared mechanism, and the page does not file it as a row of the cross-domain table.
