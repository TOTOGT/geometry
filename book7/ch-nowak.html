Martin Nowak

A population of speakers is a population of grammars: children learn the grammar of their parents imperfectly, and speakers who understand each other best leave more descendants of their grammar. Nowak, Komarova and Niyogi wrote that as one equation. This page reads the equation from the paper, reduces it to two grammars, and derives where a mixed population gives way to a dominant grammar. The reduction is the page's own and is labeled that way.

The man and the paper, in cited facts

CITED RESULTCITED Martin Nowak is a mathematical biologist at Harvard who works on evolutionary dynamics, including the evolution of language; his institutional details are from memory and were not checked for this page. The paper used here is M. A. Nowak, N. L. Komarova and P. Niyogi, “Computational and evolutionary aspects of language”, Nature 417 (2002). Its Eq. 1, as read in this session, is $$\dot x_j = \sum_i f_i(x)\,Q_{ij}\,x_i - \varphi(x)\,x_j,\qquad f_i = \sum_j x_j F_{ij},\quad \varphi = \sum_i f_i x_i,$$ where $x_j$ is the share of speakers of language $j$, $F_{ij}$ is the payoff for communicating between $i$ and $j$, $Q_{ij}$ is the chance that a child of an $i$-speaker acquires $j$, and $\varphi$, the average fitness, is read as a measure of coherence.

CITED RESULTCITED The paper states its coherence thresholds in terms of the number of example sentences $N$ a learner hears and the number of candidate grammars $n$, with unspecified constants: roughly $N > C_1 n$ for a memoryless learner and $N > C_2 \log n$ for a batch learner, with different forms for random similarity between grammars. It reads these as a minimum specificity of universal grammar. They are cited here as the paper reports them. They are not derived on this page, and the page's own threshold below is a different quantity.

A two-grammar reduction

Take two grammars with payoff $F = \begin{pmatrix}1 & a\\ a & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $0 \le a < 1$, so that speakers of the same grammar communicate best, and learning matrix $Q = \begin{pmatrix}q & 1-q\\ 1-q & q\end{pmatrix}$, where $q$ is the chance that a child acquires its parents' grammar. With $x$ the share of grammar 1, Eq. 1 becomes a cubic in $x$.

THEOREMPROVED The right-hand side factors as $F(x) = (2x-1)\bigl(ax^2 - ax + q - x^2 + x - 1\bigr)$. Hence $x = 1/2$ is always a fixed point, and $F'(1/2) = (4q - 3 - a)/2$, so the mixed state is stable exactly when $q < (3+a)/4$. For $q > (3+a)/4$ the second factor has two roots $x = \tfrac12 \pm \sqrt D / (2(1-a))$ with $D = (1-a)(4q - 3 - a) > 0$; they lie in $(0,1)$ and are stable, and $1/2$ is unstable. For $q < (3+a)/4$ one has $D < 0$, so there is no other fixed point. The factorization is direct algebra, and was also verified exactly, in rational arithmetic, at 41 points for 9 parameter pairs. The stability statements follow from the sign of $F'$.

CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED For $a = 0.5$ the threshold is $q^* = 7/8 = 0.875$. A Runge–Kutta integration with $q = 0.80$, started at $x = 0.6$, returns to $1/2$. With $q = 0.95$, started at $x = 0.51$, it leaves $1/2$ and settles at 0.8873, the predicted off-centre state. Below the threshold $F$ is positive left of $1/2$ and negative right of it, on a grid for three values of $a$. The sign pattern is the same as in a continuous (pitchfork) symmetry-breaking transition: there is no range of $q$ with two stable states at once, so in this reduction there is no hysteresis.

Reading it

In this reduction, faithful transmission is what lets one grammar take over: when children copy accurately enough, the small payoff gain from sharing a grammar with the majority is not washed out by learning errors, and the symmetric mix breaks into a dominant grammar. When learning is noisy, the mix survives. This is a statement about two equations, not about people. The paper's own coherence condition, in terms of $N$ and $n$, is the substantive result and is cited above, not reproduced.

OPEN QUESTIONOPEN Whether a two-grammar payoff of this shape, with a single parameter $a$, describes any real pair of grammars is not examined, and the page supplies no data. The comparison with ch-abrams-strogatz is a comparison of two equations that both have a symmetry-breaking structure: there the unstable interior point separates two basins, here a stable mixed state loses stability continuously. These are different bifurcations, and no shared mechanism is claimed.
