Thomas Piketty

In Capital in the Twenty-First Century the inequality r > g is “a contingent historical proposition”, not a law. This page reads what the book says, page by page, and says where this series’ own work touches it and where it does not.

What this page is, and what it is not It is a reading of one book, from a held copy, by printed page number. It paraphrases; it quotes only a few words. It does not summarise his other books, his data, or the debate about him. The pasted summaries of his work that circulate online were not used. Where the series adds something of its own, it is marked as the series’ and not his.
Part I · What the book states

Two accounting laws, one inequality

The book’s first “fundamental law” is an identity, and the book says so. With capital’s share of national income α, the rate of return r and the capital/income ratio β, α = r × β (p. 52). His example is β = 600% and r = 5%, which gives α = 30%. The second law, β = s / g, ties the long-run ratio to the savings rate s and the growth rate g (p. 166); with s = 12% and g = 2% it gives 600%. The book adds that this second law is asymptotic: it holds only if s and g stay the same indefinitely (p. 168).

Part II · Where this series touches the book

Two papers in Book 6

WP-135 uses the identity of p. 52, the slave example of pp. 162–163 and Table 7.2 on p. 248 (the bottom half’s share of capital: 5% in the United States in 2010 and in Europe in 1910 and 2010, his estimates). Those passages were read by text search and the pages around them, not the whole book. WP-32 §7.4, “Past Piketty”, proposes a concentration ratchet and says r > g “is a description, not a mechanism”.

A sentence to check in WP-32 The book does give reasons why r can exceed g: technology, and attitudes toward saving and property (p. 358), and the claim that a more perfect capital market makes it more likely (p. 27). WP-32’s sentence is therefore safe only if it means “the identity does not say how the excess return is collected, by whom, or why it persists for a given holder”, which is what WP-32 goes on to argue. As a statement about the book it overreaches. WP-32 has not been edited; this page only flags it.
Part III · A reading with two regimes (the series’ own, not his)

The arithmetic of the two laws

Put the two laws together and hold r at 5% and s at 12%. If g is 2%, β is 6 years of income and α is 30%. If g falls to 1.5%, β is 8 and α is 40%. At 1%, β is 12 and α is 60%. This is arithmetic on his two formulas. The book itself warns (p. 168) that the second law is asymptotic, so these are comparisons of long-run levels, not a forecast, and holding r fixed is an assumption, not his claim.

Why the two-regime idea needs care

His figures have the shape of a U: high before 1914, low in the mid-century, high again (Figure I.1, Figure 10.1, Figure 10.5), and he puts the fall down to shocks. One could read that as a system with two states and shocks that move it from one to the other. That is a reading, not his claim: the book speaks of forces of convergence and divergence (p. 571), not of two stable states. And a single country’s series has too few switches to test the idea: in the toy of Book XXVI chapter 19, a bimodality test that finds a two-state system in 98% of series when it switches about eight times per series finds it in 17% when it switches about once every five series. Whether wealth shares in any country sit in two regimes is therefore not established here.
