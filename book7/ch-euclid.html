MATHEMATICS · PUREPROFILE

Euclid

Two propositions from Book IX of the Elements: that the primes do not run out, and that a short recipe makes perfect numbers. The recipe is twenty-three centuries old and still the only known source of one kind of them. Whether the other kind exists, nobody knows.

How to read the labels on this page

Four kinds of claim appear here, and each carries its kind where it is made. THEOREMPROVED means a proof is given or cited; CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED means a finite computation, printed by tools/odd_perfect_check.py, and says nothing beyond the cases it ran; OPEN QUESTIONOPEN means nobody knows; CITED RESULTCITED means a published result this page reports and does not re-derive.

Proposition IX.20 — there is always another prime

THEOREMPROVED Prime numbers are more than any assigned multitude of prime numbers (Euclid, Elements IX.20).

Take any finite list of primes and multiply them. Add one. The result leaves remainder 1 on division by every prime on the list, so any prime factor of it is not on the list. The list was arbitrary, so no finite list holds every prime.

The step people misremember is that the new number need not itself be prime. A small instance:

CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED $2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13 + 1 = 30031 = 59 \times 509$. The product-plus-one is composite, and both of its prime factors are new.

Proposition IX.36 — the recipe

THEOREMPROVED If $M = 2^p - 1$ is prime, then $N = 2^{p-1}M$ is perfect, meaning the sum of its divisors smaller than $N$ is $N$ (Euclid, Elements IX.36, stated there for the sum of a doubling sequence from the unit).

The divisors of $N$ are $1,2,4,\dots,2^{p-1}$ and $M,2M,\dots,2^{p-2}M$, with $N$ itself left out of the sum. The first group adds to $2^p-1 = M$. The second adds to $M(2^{p-1}-1)$. Together:

M + M(2^{p-1} - 1) = M · 2^{p-1} = N

Primality of $M$ is what makes that list complete: if $M$ had a factor, $N$ would have divisors the list leaves out. The three smallest cases are on the page for anyone to add by hand: $p=2$ gives $M=3$ and $N=6 = 1+2+3$; $p=3$ gives $M=7$ and $N=28 = 1+2+4+7+14$; $p=5$ gives $M=31$ and $N=496$.

The converse, and the question it leaves

THEOREMPROVED Every even perfect number has the form $2^{p-1}(2^p-1)$ with $2^p-1$ prime. Euler is credited with this converse; his writing on it was published after his death. The statement is machine-checked in Lean's Mathlib Archive: Theorems100.Nat.even_and_perfect_iff, in Archive/Wiedijk100Theorems/PerfectNumbers.lean, which asserts Even n ∧ Nat.Perfect n ↔ ∃ k, Nat.Prime (mersenne (k+1)) ∧ n = 2^k * mersenne (k+1).

The name and statement above were read from local Mathlib checkouts at revisions 4bbdccd9c5 and eba3d887fc. They were not compiled here, and they were not read at this corpus's pinned revision 81a5d257c8. That is a gap, and it is listed in the scholium below.

OPEN QUESTIONOPEN Is there an odd perfect number? Euclid's recipe, plus Euler's converse, describes all the even ones and none of the odd ones. Whether the set of odd perfect numbers is empty is not known.

That question has its own interactive page: Can a Perfect Number Be Odd? — add up divisors yourself, run the Lucas–Lehmer test on an exponent of your choice, and run a bounded search for an odd perfect number, with the exact interval and the count printed.
