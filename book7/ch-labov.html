LINGUISTICSPROFILE

William Labov

Labov's programme treats variation in speech as orderly and measurable: how often a speaker uses one form over another depends on who they are, where, and how carefully they speak. When a change spreads, its share over time traces an S-curve. This page shows the arithmetic of that curve and of the “constant rate” claim attached to it, using a model with made-up parameters. It uses no data from Labov or anyone else.

The man and the studies, in cited facts

CITED RESULTCITED William Labov (born 1927) is an American linguist, long at the University of Pennsylvania, and a founder of variationist sociolinguistics. His 1963 study of Martha's Vineyard measured how far speakers centralize the first element of the diphthongs in words like price and mouth. As summarized in course notes read in this session, centralization was low among the oldest speakers, peaked among speakers aged about 31 to 45, and was lower again among the youngest, and it tracked identification with the island: speakers who identified more strongly with the Vineyard centralized more. Labov is also known for a study of the pronunciation of r in New York department stores, repeated by later researchers; that study was not read for this page. All of this is reported from the literature and was not checked beyond the course notes.

The S-curve

CITED RESULTCITED The S-shaped course of a linguistic change, slow at first, fast in the middle, slow at the end, is usually traced to Weinreich, Labov and Herzog (1968). Its logistic form, in which the log-odds of the incoming variant rise linearly in time at the same rate in every linguistic context while the starting level differs by context, is usually called Kroch's constant rate hypothesis (1989). Both attributions are from memory and were not checked for this page, and the hypothesis is an empirical claim about changes, not a theorem.

In symbols, the share $p(t)$ of the new form in a context satisfies $\mathrm{logit}\,p(t) = st + c$, equivalently $\dot p = s\,p(1-p)$, where $s$ is the rate and $c$ sets the starting level, which may differ from one context to another.

THEOREMPROVED If $\dot p = s\,p(1-p)$ then $\mathrm{logit}\,p(t) = st + c$, so $p(t) = 1/(1 + e^{-(st+c)})$. Two contexts with the same $s$ and intercepts $c_1, c_2$ have log-odds differing by the constant $c_2 - c_1$ at every time. The time to go from 10% to 90% is $2\ln 9 / s$, whatever the intercept. Proof: $\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{logit}\,p = \dot p / (p(1-p)) = s$; the gap is then $(st + c_2) - (st + c_1)$; and logit $0.9 - $ logit $0.1 = 2\ln 9$.

CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED A Runge–Kutta integration of $\dot p = s\,p(1-p)$ over 100 model years agrees with the closed form to about $10^{-14}$ at every whole year. For two contexts with $s = 0.1$ and $c = -1$ and $-3$, the log-odds gap is 2 to within $10^{-12}$ at every year from 0 to 100. As a control, a second context with a different slope, $s = 0.15$, gives a gap that moves by 5 over the same years. All parameters are chosen for the illustration; they are not estimates from any change.

What this does and does not say

A simple fact about the curve is that its width is set by one number: halve the rate and the 10-to-90 time doubles. If a change really follows this curve in every context at one rate, then the share of the new form at any single time in one context fixes it in the others. Whether real changes do is exactly what the hypothesis asserts and what this page does not test.

OPEN QUESTIONOPEN Whether the shares of real variants follow a common-rate logistic, what mechanism would produce it (the model here has none; it only states the shape), and whether the social factors Labov measured, such as age, class and identity, act on $s$, on $c$, or on both, are not settled on this page. It also does not claim a link between this curve and the competition model in ch-abrams-strogatz; the two share a logistic form of equation and nothing more is claimed.
