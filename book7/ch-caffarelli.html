MATHEMATICS · ANALYSIS

Luis A. Caffarelli

He learnt the obstacle problem from scratch after a lecture series, and went on to prove how smooth free boundaries are, then how regular solutions of the Monge–Ampère equation are. The second of those is the regularity theory that optimal transport leans on.

b. 1948, Buenos Aires · PhD Buenos Aires 1972, under Calixto Calderón · Minnesota, Courant, Chicago, Institute for Advanced Study · Sid Richardson Chair, University of Texas at Austin, since 1997

Part I · The path

From Jacobi series to free boundaries

He was born in Buenos Aires in 1948 and studied mathematics there. His 1972 doctorate, under Calixto Calderón, was on polynomials: Sobre conjugación y sumabilidad de series de Jacobi. The next year he went to the University of Minnesota as a postdoctoral fellow, where Calderón had taken a permanent post.

At Minnesota the direction changed. He attended a lecture series on harmonic analysis by Hans Lewy, asked Lewy for problems, and was given the obstacle problem: how an elastic membrane rests on a given obstacle. He had to learn the subject from nothing and, in the biography's phrase, was hooked. The region where the membrane touches the obstacle has a boundary that is not given in advance — a free boundary — and the question is how regular it is. He published six papers in 1976, and in 1977 his first paper in Acta Mathematica, The regularity of free boundaries in higher dimensions.

Part II · The two papers people name

Navier–Stokes, and Monge–Ampère

On a walk in Chinatown, after the move to the Courant Institute, he, Robert Kohn and Louis Nirenberg decided to write together about the Navier–Stokes equations. The result, Partial regularity of suitable weak solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations (1982), won the American Mathematical Society's 2014 Steele Prize for Seminal Contribution to Research. Nirenberg, asked about him, is quoted as saying he had a hard time keeping up and that Caffarelli “immediately sees things that other people don't see.”

In the Institute years a major focus was the Monge–Ampère equation, and what is now called Caffarelli's regularity theory comes from that work. The biography says it has important applications elsewhere, optimal transportation among them. ch-optimal-transport is the companion chapter: what the transport problem is, why it becomes a Monge–Ampère equation, and checks of the pieces that can be checked on a computer.

He has published 320 papers and, at 74, still publishes several a year. He has co-written with more than 130 people, most often with Avner Friedman; his papers have about 19,000 citations (the biography's source: MathSciNet); and he has advised more than thirty PhD students, among them Alessio Figalli, who won the Fields Medal in 2018.
