Śaṅkara

What śruti is, how its first great commentator argued that it comes before reasoning, and what it does and does not have to do with mathematics. The history and the philosophy are cited and labeled as cited; the one computation on the page is the Śulba approximation of √2, done in exact arithmetic.

The man, and how much of his biography is firm

CITED RESULTCITED Śaṅkara (also Śaṅkarācārya, Adi Śaṅkara) is the best-known teacher of Advaita Vedānta, the school that holds that the self and Brahman are not two. He is usually placed in the eighth century CE; the traditional dates are a little earlier, and scholars differ. His commentaries on the Brahma Sūtras, the Bhagavad Gītā and the principal Upaniṣads are widely accepted as his. Many other works are attributed to him and are disputed.

CITED RESULTCITED The stories of his travels, his public debates and the monastic centres he is said to have founded come from hagiographies written well after his time. They are tradition. This page does not use them as evidence for anything.

What śruti is

Śruti means “what is heard.” It is the revealed corpus, the Vedas and, for Śaṅkara, above all the Upaniṣads. The tradition holds that it has no human author (apauruṣeya). Smṛti, “what is remembered,” is everything derived from it: the law books, the epics and the commentaries. Śaṅkara's own commentaries belong on the smṛti side of that line, a point he does not hide.

CITED RESULTCITED Śruti is not individual intuition and not mystical insight in the modern sense. For Śaṅkara it is a pramāṇa, a valid means of knowledge, in the way perception and inference are, and it is the only one that reaches Brahman, because Brahman is not an object of perception or inference. Later Advaita lists six pramāṇas (perception, inference, comparison, testimony, presumption, non-apprehension); testimony (śabda) is the category that śruti belongs to.

Why śruti before reasoning

CITED RESULTCITED In his commentary on the Brahma Sūtras (2.1.11, tarkāpratiṣṭhānāt), Śaṅkara argues that reasoning with no source outside itself has no fixed footing: for any argument, a more skilful reasoner can build a counter-argument. His conclusion is that reasoning cannot by itself settle questions about the ultimate. Reasoning is not rejected. It is used to clear objections and to show that the text is coherent, in the service of śruti.

This is an argument about the limits of unaided reasoning, and it is worth keeping apart from the claim that reasoning is inferior in kind. It is also unlike the way a proof assistant works. In a kernel-checked proof the authority is the checker, and an insight is a candidate until it compiles. In Śaṅkara the seed is authoritative and the later work is checked against it.

OPEN QUESTIONOPEN Whether the two structures, a received seed checked by commentary and a candidate checked by a kernel, have anything in common beyond a shared shape is not settled and is not claimed here. See the scope note at the end of the page.

Who may hear it

CITED RESULTCITED The teaching came with a condition, adhikāra, fitness to receive it, which meant a prepared student. In his commentary on Brahma Sūtra 1.3.34–38 he also argues that śūdras are not entitled to Vedic study. The page reports that as part of the record. It is a restriction on who could receive the knowledge, imposed by the social order and not by the difficulty of the subject, and it should not be left out when he is described as having opened knowledge up.

Waking, dream, deep sleep

CITED RESULTCITED The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad analyses waking, dream and deep sleep, and a fourth state (turīya) that is not one of them. Gauḍapāda wrote verses (kārikā) on it and Śaṅkara commented on those verses. Deep sleep is treated as a state in which nothing is known and yet the knower is not lost, which is why the tradition uses sleep as evidence about the self.

This is a philosophical use of sleep. It is not a theory of how ideas arrive, and the page does not read it that way.

Mathematics near the scripture

The mathematics that is documented in the Vedic orbit is in the Śulba Sūtras (Baudhāyana, Āpastamba, Kātyāyana), manuals for laying out the altars of the ritual with cords. Strictly these are Vedāṅga, ancillary texts, and not śruti. Their dates are uncertain; Baudhāyana is placed between about 800 and 500 BCE depending on the scholar.

CITED RESULTCITED The Śulba Sūtras state the rule that the square on the diagonal of a rectangle equals the sum of the squares on its sides, and they give a rational approximation of √2 as 1 + 1/3 + 1/(3·4) − 1/(3·4·34). The statements are cited. The arithmetic below is not, and is done exactly.

Whether the authors of the Śulba Sūtras knew why the fraction is so good is not shown by the arithmetic.

CITED RESULTCITED Later Indian mathematics has a clear case of terse results expanded by commentary. Mādhava of Saṅgamagrāma (about 1340–1425) is credited with infinite series for π and for the sine and cosine. The Yuktibhāṣā (about 1530, usually attributed to Jyeṣṭhadeva, in Malayalam) gives the derivations. A line from the Śulba Sūtras through Āryabhaṭa and Brahmagupta to Kerala is plausible and partly documented by later authors citing earlier ones, but each step being caused by the one before is not established, and the thesis that mathematics grew out of ritual geometry is contested. Other pages in the corpus: ch-madhava and, for another Indian mathematician, ch-ramanujan.

Insight that arrives before the explanation

Mathematicians in many centuries report seeing a result before they can justify it, and some report that it came in or near sleep. The reports differ a great deal in how well they are sourced, and the table says so plainly.

OPEN QUESTIONOPEN Whether one neural process (a calm state before sleep, for instance) lies behind the sleep reports across people and centuries is not established. The controlled studies support sleep as a route to insight. They do not show that the insight is correct, and Ramanujan's record shows both. Many of his formulas held, and some claims in his early letters did not. A mind that generates better candidates is still checked afterwards.

Where this touches the rest of the corpus

The Book 3 chapters on allostatic load and on circadian rhythm are where sleep enters the corpus as a model. In the allostatic toy model, recovery is the rate $k$ in $\dot x = s - kx + bx^2/(1+x^2)$, and a fold appears when $b \ge 1.54\,k$. Whether $k$ is sleep is an assumption of the model, with no data behind it. The circadian chapter covers when sleep happens, not what it does. Neither chapter has a term for learning or consolidation.

Scholium — scope of this page

One computation was checked: the approximation of √2 and the four triples. The claims about Śaṅkara, the Śulba Sūtras, the Kerala school and the dream reports were not checked for this page. They are reported from the literature and tradition, and some of them (the biography above all) rest on late sources. The page does not claim that these traditions state one shared truth. Other pages in the corpus list Advaita among “convergent” traditions; this page does not support that claim. A shared shape, such as a compressed statement expanded by later work, is an observation, and it would take a stated definition and a case where it fails to become more than that.
