Bhāskara II

The cyclic method (chakravāla) for the equation $x^2 - Ny^2 = 1$, which he is credited with setting out in full in the twelfth century. This page runs the method on $N = 61$, $67$ and $109$ in exact integer arithmetic, checks the answers a second way, and says which of the historical claims are cited and not checked.

The man, in cited facts

CITED RESULTCITED Bhāskara II (also Bhāskarācārya, 1114 to about 1185 CE) worked in the astronomical tradition associated with Ujjain. His Siddhānta Śiromaṇi (completed about 1150) has four parts: Līlāvatī (arithmetic and geometry), Bījagaṇita (algebra), Grahagaṇita (planetary computation) and Golādhyāya (the sphere). The equation below is treated in the Bījagaṇita. Dates and places are as the literature gives them; this page did not check them.

The equation

For a positive integer $N$ that is not a perfect square, find positive integers $x, y$ with $x^2 - Ny^2 = 1$. The solutions are not obvious: for $N = 61$ the smallest has $x$ above 1.7 billion.

THEOREMPROVED (Lagrange.) For every non-square $N$ the equation has infinitely many solutions, all of them powers of one smallest solution, $x_k + y_k\sqrt N = (x_1 + y_1\sqrt N)^k$. This is classical; it is stated here and not proved here.

CITED RESULTCITED As the standard histories tell it, Brahmagupta (628 CE) stated the equation and a rule for composing solutions (bhāvanā); Bhāskara II is credited with the complete cyclic method and with worked examples, among them $N = 61$ and $N = 67$. In 1657 Fermat posed equations of this type as a challenge to English mathematicians, with $N = 61$ and $N = 109$ among the cases. Euler's attaching of Pell's name to the equation is usually described as a mistake of attribution. Lagrange gave the existence proof in 1768.

The cyclic method, as run here

Keep a triple $(a, b, k)$ with $a^2 - Nb^2 = k$. Start from $b = 1$ and the $a$ nearest $\sqrt N$. At each step choose the integer $m$ with $a + bm \equiv 0 \pmod{|k|}$ that makes $|m^2 - N|$ smallest, and replace the triple by

$$a' = \frac{am + Nb}{|k|},\qquad b' = \frac{a + bm}{|k|},\qquad k' = \frac{m^2 - N}{k}.$$

Stop when $|k| = 1$. If $k = 1$ the pair is the answer; if $k = -1$, squaring $a + b\sqrt N$ gives it, as $(a^2 + Nb^2,\ 2ab)$. The multiplier $m$ is recomputed from the current triple at every step.

CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED Each row satisfies $x^2 - Ny^2 = 1$ exactly. The same $(x, y)$ comes out of an independent route, the continued fraction of $\sqrt N$, for these three values and for all 282 non-square $N$ from 2 to 299. For $N = 61$ no $y$ below 2,000,000 works, which is a partial check that the answer is the smallest; the full statement is the continued-fraction theorem, which is cited. That the method terminates for every $N$ is also a cited theorem, and the 282 cases are evidence for it and not a proof.

The solutions form a ladder

CHECKED INSTANCECOMPUTED For $N = 61$, the first three powers of $x_1 + y_1\sqrt{61}$ match the terms of the linear recurrence $s_{k+1} = 2x_1 s_k - s_{k-1}$, started from $(1, 0)$ and $(x_1, y_1)$, and every term solves the equation exactly. The recurrence is a property of the whole tower of solutions. It is not the cyclic method, which picks its multiplier afresh at each step.

OPEN QUESTIONOPEN Whether this recurrence has anything to do with the recurrence-ladder framework elsewhere in the corpus, beyond sharing the word, is not examined here and is not claimed. The Book 7 index has described the cyclic method as an ancestor of the $n$-bonacci recurrence; that description is not supported by this page.

What is not claimed

Claims that Bhāskara II anticipated calculus are sometimes made. They are not examined here. Nor does the page say how his method reached Europe or whether it did; Fermat's challenge and Lagrange's proof are reported above as history, with no claim of a line of transmission. See also ch-euler for Euler, ch-madhava for the Kerala school, and ch-shankara for how the scripture and the mathematics of the same civilization relate.
