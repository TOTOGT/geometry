Georg Cantor

Born in St Petersburg in 1845, he proved that infinity comes in sizes. This corpus names him in 30 files and states his theorem in Lean in one line — and the man who corresponded with him most, Dedekind, appears in 2.

Part I · The result

Two sets have the same size if their members can be paired off one to one. For finite sets that is counting without numbers. Cantor used it as the definition for infinite sets, and then proved that the definition does not collapse: the natural numbers and the real numbers cannot be paired. His theorem is more general.

Theorem (Cantor). For every set X there is no function from X onto its power set P(X). Hence |X| < |P(X)|.

Proof. Suppose f maps X onto P(X). Let D be the set of those x in X that are not members of f(x). Because f is onto, D = f(y) for some y. If y is in D, then y is not in f(y) = D. If y is not in D, then y is in f(y) = D. Both cases are contradictions, so no such f exists. ■

The same argument, applied to the integers or to digit lists, is the “diagonal”. Applied again and again to N, it gives P(N), P(P(N)) and so on without end. The first step of that ladder, from N to P(N), is the step from the countable to the continuum. Whether any size lies between the two is the Continuum Hypothesis, which Gödel (1940) and Cohen (1963) showed ZFC can neither refute nor prove; Chapter 8 says this and builds a beth ladder for exactly that reason.

The ratio is the finding. The man with the most citations as an opponent is in 8 files; his correspondent is in 2; Hilbert, who defended the result, is in 48. The corpus tells Cantor’s story through his theorem and his enemies, and barely at all through the people he wrote to.

Part III · His languages

He wrote his set theory in German. From about the age of eight he attended a German Lutheran school in St Petersburg whose timetable listed Russian, German and French, with English and Latin optional; he did not take Latin. A biographer quotes Grattan-Guinness that he seemingly never wrote in Russian although he must have known it. French versions of his papers appeared in Acta Mathematica in 1883, translated by others, among them Poincaré; Jourdain made the English edition of 1915. Not found: the language spoken at home; the language of the 1867 dissertation text (only its Latin title was seen); Marotte’s 1899 French translation. The fuller table, with what is only plausible, is in Cantor and Crossing Infinities (Book 3).
