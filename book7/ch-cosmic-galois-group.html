The Cosmic Galois Group

From the electron’s magnetic moment to Brown’s multiple motivic L-functions — what is measured, what is proved, what is only numerical, and the one place where this chapter can say something new and exact about a reflection symmetry that does not locate zeros.

Read this first

Nothing in this chapter bears on the truth of the Riemann hypothesis. Agreement between QED and experiment confirms QED. Special values of zeta and of modular L-functions at integers are not constraints on non-trivial zeros. Every claim below carries one of six labels; the label is the claim.

Part I · What is measured

The electron, to a part in a trillion

Fan, Myers, Sukra and Gabrielse measured the electron magnetic moment with a one-electron quantum cyclotron (Phys. Rev. Lett. 130, 071801, 2023): $g/2 = 1.001\,159\,652\,180\,59\,(13)$, an uncertainty of 0.13 parts per trillion, 2.2 times better than the value that stood for fourteen years. ESTABLISHED (abstract read; error budget not read.) The Standard Model prediction agrees with it to about one part in $10^{12}$, and the same comparison yields $\alpha^{-1}=137.035\,999\,166\,(15)$. ESTABLISHED

Correction to a secondary summary. A video summary relayed to this project quoted a theory value ending …652 811 3 next to the measurement. That differs from the measured value at the tenth decimal, which would be a $10^{-10}$ disagreement, not $10^{-12}$; it is almost certainly a transcription error, and the measured value labelled “2012” there is probably the 2008 result. Neither number is used here. The video itself was never opened by this project, so all of its timestamps remain leads, not sources.

What this does and does not show ESTABLISHED

It shows that the perturbative series in $\alpha/\pi$ describes nature to twelve digits. It says nothing about the zeros of any L-function. The only bridge between the two is structural: the numbers that appear in the series are periods, and periods have an arithmetic geometry. That bridge is the subject of the rest of the chapter, and it stops well short of zeros.

Part II · The arithmetic anatomy of the series

Schwinger, and the first place a zeta value appears

Write $a_e = \sum_{n\ge1} A_n\,(\alpha/\pi)^n$. The first coefficient is $A_1=\tfrac12$ (Schwinger). ESTABLISHED The mass-independent two-loop coefficient is

$$A_2 \;=\; \frac{197}{144}+\frac{\pi^2}{12}+\frac34\zeta(3)-\frac{\pi^2}{2}\ln 2 \;=\; -0.328\,478\,965\,579\,193\ldots$$

The closed form was evaluated to 30 digits by the verification script and gives $-0.3284789655791937845$. NUMERICAL That the closed form is the physical coefficient is the standard literature value; this project confirmed the value of the formula, not the diagram calculation behind it.

Proposition 1 DERIVED

Since $\pi^2/6=\zeta(2)$, one has $\pi^2/12=\tfrac12\zeta(2)$ and $\pi^2/2=3\zeta(2)$, so $$A_2=\frac{197}{144}+\tfrac12\zeta(2)+\tfrac34\zeta(3)-3\zeta(2)\ln2 .$$ Hence the two-loop coefficient is a rational linear combination of $1,\ \zeta(2),\ \zeta(3),\ \zeta(2)\ln 2$: every transcendental constant in it is a product of a zeta value and a logarithm of a rational number.

Direct substitution of $\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$. The script checks $\pi^2/12=\zeta(2)/2$ and $\pi^2/2=3\zeta(2)$ to 28 digits and, as a negative control, confirms that flipping the sign of the $\zeta(3)$ term does not reproduce the value.∎

Four loops: what Laporta actually computed

Laporta (arXiv:1704.06996, read in full) evaluates the four-loop coefficient to 1,100 digits: $A_4=-1.9122457649264455741526\ldots$ (in units of $(\alpha/\pi)^4$), agreeing at $0.9\sigma$ with an earlier numerical value, with 891 vertex diagrams, 334 master integrals in 220 topologies. ESTABLISHED The “4,800 digits” in the abstract refers to finite master-integral pieces used as fit ingredients, not to the coefficient. The constants appearing include zeta values, $\ln2$, Nielsen-type sums, Clausen values, harmonic polylogarithms at roots of unity of order 4 and 6, and elliptic constants; six elliptic constants are not yet fitted analytically, so the closed form is incomplete. What Laporta does not say

The text contains no identification with L-values of modular forms and no Bessel-moment integrals. A claim that the four-loop coefficient is “Bessel integrals matching modular L-values” is not supported by that paper. The nearest source for modular-form L-values in Feynman diagrams is Broadhurst–Schnetz (arXiv:1409.5570, abstract only), which concerns specific massive diagrams at 3, 4 and 6 loops and the obstruction at 8 loops in $\varphi^4$ theory, not the electron $g{-}2$ coefficient.

Part III · Graphs, polynomials, periods

A complete small example: the wheel with three spokes

For a connected graph $G$ with edge variables $x_e$, the Kirchhoff (first Symanzik) polynomial is $\Psi_G=\sum_{T}\prod_{e\notin T}x_e$ over spanning trees $T$. For a primitive log-divergent graph with $n=2h$ edges ($h$ loops), the period is the projective integral $\int \Omega/\Psi_G^{2}$ over the positive orthant. ESTABLISHED (as a definition in the literature; the source for this definition, Bloch–Esnault–Kreimer, was not read.)

Take $K_4$: four vertices, six edges, $h=3$. By Cayley $K_4$ has $4^{2}=16$ spanning trees: 4 stars and 12 paths. So $\Psi_{K_4}$ is a sum of 16 monomials of degree 3, each the product of the three edges outside a tree. DERIVED (the script enumerates and counts 16 trees by three independent methods.) Which graph, and which period. Schnetz (arXiv:0801.2856) defines periods for completed primitive graphs: 4-regular graphs, with one vertex labelled $\infty$. Proposition 2.6 there says $\Gamma-v$ is primitive iff $\Gamma$ is completed primitive; eq. (2.3) defines $P_\Gamma=\pi^{-2h_1}\int\Omega/\prod Q_i$ (position-space form, with the prefactor $\pi^{-2h_1}$); and Definition and Theorem 2.7 (§2.4), eq. (2.22), gives $P_\Gamma=\int_\Delta\Omega(\alpha)/\Psi_{\Gamma-\infty}(\alpha)^2$ over $\alpha_i>0$, without a $\pi$ prefactor, with $\Psi=\sum_T\prod_{e\notin T}\alpha_e$ (eq. (2.7)). That is the integral this chapter writes, for the decompleted graph $\Gamma-\infty$. Example 2.8 computes the completed period of the graph $P_3$ and ends at $6\zeta(3)$. ESTABLISHED (confirmed in the raw text extraction of the paper; printed page numbers are not visible there, section numbers are used instead.)

The identification with $K_4$ is derived here, not quoted. For a completed 4-regular graph on $N$ vertices there are $2N$ edges; deleting one vertex and its four edges leaves $N-1$ vertices and $2N-4$ edges, so $h=(2N-4)-(N-1)+1=N-2$. For $h=3$ this forces $N=5$, and a simple 4-regular graph on five vertices is $K_5$. Deleting a vertex of $K_5$ leaves $K_4$: $V=4$, $E=6$, $h=6-4+1=3$, which is the three-loop wheel with three spokes (a hub joined to a triangle). DERIVED Schnetz’s text as extracted does not itself name the graph $P_3$ as $K_5$ or its decompletion as $K_4$ or the wheel; that naming is the derivation just given, and it assumes the completed graph is simple. Equality of the two periods is then not inferred from the graph isomorphism alone: it is eq. (2.22) with the same $\Psi$, the same positive-orthant domain and no extra prefactor, so the quantity $\int\Omega/\Psi_{K_4}^2$ with $\Omega$ the standard projective form is $P_3=6\zeta(3)$. The normalisation of the chapter’s integral is fixed by derivation in Proposition 8 below. What could not be confirmed is Schnetz’s own $\Omega$: in the text extraction, eq. (2.4) shows $\Omega=\sum(-1)^i\,dx_0\cdots\widehat{dx_i}\cdots dx_m$ with no factor $x_i$ (almost certainly lost in extraction, since without it the form would not be projectively invariant, but that is my inference, not something read), eq. (2.22) prints its domain as $\Lambda$ while defining only $\Delta=\{\alpha_i>0\}$, and the proof of Theorem 2.7 works in the chart $\alpha_1=1$. So agreement of normalisation with Schnetz is argued (same $\Psi$, positive orthant, no $\pi$ prefactor, standard projective form) and not confirmed symbol by symbol. Broadhurst–Kreimer (hep-ph/9504352) state $6\zeta(3)$ in counterterm/β-function normalisation; equating that with the period is an inference, and this chapter does not rely on it. Schnetz’s census Table 4 row for $P_3$ was seen only through a summary and is not cited. The quasi-Monte-Carlo estimate on the simplex gave $7.04\pm0.04$ (spread of eight scrambled replicates, not a rigorous error bound) against $6\zeta(3)=7.212$, so it is INCONCLUSIVE and does not confirm the identity. Numerical period versus motivic period. $6\zeta(3)$ is a numerical period, a complex number. The cosmic Galois group acts on motivic periods, elements of an algebra equipped with a coaction, not on numbers; the passage from one to the other is the period map, and statements about the group are statements about the motivic side.

Proposition 8 · the projective measure on the simplex DERIVED

Let $\Omega=\sum_{i=1}^{6}(-1)^{i-1}x_i\,dx_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{dx_i}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_6$. On the simplex $\Delta_5=\{x_i\ge0,\ \sum_{i=1}^6x_i=1\}$, parametrised by $(x_1,\dots,x_5)$ with $x_6=1-\sum_{i\le5}x_i$, the pullback of $\Omega$ is $-\,dx_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_5$. The coordinate volume of $\Delta_5$ is $1/5!=1/120$. Hence, up to orientation, $\int_{\Delta_5}\Omega/\Psi_{K_4}^2=\tfrac1{120}\,\mathbb E\big[\Psi_{K_4}^{-2}\big]$ for $x$ uniform on $\Delta_5$, which is what the script estimates.

$\Psi_{K_4}$ is homogeneous of degree 3, so $\Psi^2$ has degree 6, equal to the degree of $\Omega$ (six variables): $\Omega/\Psi^2$ is projectively invariant, so any section of the positive orthant gives the same integral. On $\Delta_5$, $dx_6=-\sum_{j\le5}dx_j$. The $i=6$ term is $(-1)^5x_6\,dx_1\cdots dx_5=-x_6\,dx_1\cdots dx_5$. For $i\le5$ the form $dx_1\cdots\widehat{dx_i}\cdots dx_5\wedge dx_6$ keeps only the $-dx_i$ part of $dx_6$ (the other parts repeat a factor); moving $dx_i$ to slot $i$ costs $(-1)^{5-i}$, so the term is $(-1)^{i-1}x_i\cdot(-1)(-1)^{5-i}dx_1\cdots dx_5=-x_i\,dx_1\cdots dx_5$. Summing, $\Omega\mapsto-(x_1+\cdots+x_6)\,dx_1\cdots dx_5=-dx_1\cdots dx_5$. (The chart $x_6=1$ gives the same form on the orthant, since $dx_6=0$ and $\Omega=-dx_1\cdots dx_5$.) The region $\{x_i\ge0,\ \sum_{i\le5}x_i\le1\}$ has volume $1/5!$, and sampling $x=-\log u$ normalised by its sum gives the uniform distribution on it.∎

Why this matters for the chapter: the first non-trivial period of $\varphi^4$ theory is a single zeta value, the same kind of number that appears in $A_2$. At higher loops periods leave the zeta world: Brown–Schnetz (arXiv:1304.5342, abstract only) report that many point-counts of graph hypersurfaces over $\mathbb F_q$ match Fourier coefficients of modular forms of weight $\le8$ and level $\le17$. Proposition 7 · the omitted tail of $\int tI_0K_0^4$ DERIVED

For $T>0$, $$0<\int_T^\infty tI_0(t)K_0(t)^4\,dt\;\le\;\frac{\pi^2e^{-3T}}{12\,T}.$$ For $T=60$ the right side is exactly $\pi^2e^{-180}/720\approx9.204\times10^{-81}$, hence $<9.3\times10^{-81}$.

Integral representations: $I_0(t)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi e^{t\cos\theta}\,d\theta$ and $K_0(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t\cosh u}\,du$ for $t>0$. Since $\cos\theta\le1$, $I_0(t)\le\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi e^{t}d\theta=e^t$. Since $\cosh u\ge1+u^2/2$, $K_0(t)\le e^{-t}\int_0^\infty e^{-tu^2/2}du=e^{-t}\sqrt{\pi/(2t)}$. Hence $tI_0K_0^4\le t\,e^t\,(\pi/2t)^2e^{-4t}=\frac{\pi^2}{4}\frac{e^{-3t}}t$. For $t\ge T$, $1/t\le1/T$, and $\int_T^\infty e^{-3t}dt=e^{-3T}/3$, so the tail is at most $\frac{\pi^2}{4T}\cdot\frac{e^{-3T}}3=\frac{\pi^2e^{-3T}}{12T}$. At $T=60$ this equals $\pi^2e^{-180}/720\approx9.204\times10^{-81}$, which is less than $9.3\times10^{-81}$.∎

The computed tail in the script is $4.7\times10^{-82}$, below this bound. The script integrates to $\infty$ directly; the bound shows that stopping at $t=60$ would also have been harmless at the precision claimed.

Part VI · Brown’s multiple motivic L-functions

Theorem 1.1 of arXiv:1904.00190

Brown builds multi-variable completed L-functions $\xi(s_1,\ldots,s_r)$ as regularised iterated Mellin transforms of theta-kernel one-forms. For the trivial motive the kernel is $\theta_{\mathbb Q}(t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{-\pi n^2t^2}$. The stated theorem (introduction read; Theorems 3.1 and 3.6 are the general statements): ESTABLISHED

  • $\xi(s_1,\dots,s_r)$ is meromorphic on $\mathbb C^r$;
  • it satisfies the functional equation $\xi(s_1,\dots,s_r)=\xi(1-s_r,\dots,1-s_1)$;
  • it satisfies a shuffle-product identity;
  • it has at most simple poles along the hyperplanes $s_1+\dots+s_k=k$ and $s_k+\dots+s_r=0$ $(1\le k\le r)$, with residues expressed by lower-depth $\xi$.

The abstract also states that all periods of mixed Tate motives over $\mathbb Z$ and of motivic fundamental groups of the modular groups occur as special values of these functions. ESTABLISHED Whether the same holds for arbitrary mixed motives is posed as a question. OPEN The portions read say nothing about the zeros of $\xi(s_1,\dots,s_r)$. No Riemann-hypothesis-type statement is in the paper.

Part VII · The geometry of the reversal-reflection

The involution, exactly

Define $R:\mathbb C^r\to\mathbb C^r$ by $R(s_1,\dots,s_r)=(1-s_r,\dots,1-s_1)$. In centred coordinates $z_i=s_i-\tfrac12$ it is the linear map $z\mapsto -Pz$, where $P$ is the reversal permutation matrix. All propositions below are elementary; the script checks each over exact rationals for $r=1,\dots,8$, with controls that must fail.

Proposition 2 · involution DERIVED

$R^2=\mathrm{id}$, and $R$ is complex-linear in the $z_i$ (an affine reflection of $\mathbb C^r$).

$R(R(s))_i=1-R(s)_{r+1-i}=1-(1-s_{i})=s_i$. In $z$: $(-P)(-P)z=P^2z=z$ since reversing twice is the identity.∎
Proposition 3 · fixed locus and eigenspaces DERIVED

$\mathrm{Fix}(R)=\{s: s_i+s_{r+1-i}=1\ \forall i\}$. It is an affine subspace of complex dimension $\lfloor r/2\rfloor$; the $-1$ eigenspace of $z\mapsto -Pz$ has dimension $\lceil r/2\rceil$. (For odd $r$ the middle coordinate is $\tfrac12$.)

$R(s)=s$ iff $1-s_{r+1-i}=s_i$ for all $i$, which is the stated system. It pairs coordinates $(i,r+1-i)$ for $i∎
Proposition 3b · the projectors DERIVED

Let $J$ be the reversal matrix ($J^2=I$) and $A=-J$. Put $P_+=\tfrac12(I-J)$ and $P_-=\tfrac12(I+J)$. Then $P_\pm^2=P_\pm$, $P_+P_-=P_-P_+=0$, $P_++P_-=I$, $AP_+=P_+$, $AP_-=-P_-$, and $$\mathbb C^r=V_+\oplus V_-,\qquad V_\pm=\operatorname{im}P_\pm,\qquad \dim V_+=\lfloor r/2\rfloor,\ \dim V_-=\lceil r/2\rceil .$$ Here $V_+$ is the fixed space of $A$ and $V_-$ its $-1$ eigenspace.

$P_\pm^2=\tfrac14(I\mp2J+J^2)=\tfrac14(2I\mp2J)=P_\pm$. $P_+P_-=\tfrac14(I-J)(I+J)=\tfrac14(I-J^2)=0$. $P_++P_-=I$ is immediate. $AP_+=-J\cdot\tfrac12(I-J)=\tfrac12(-J+I)=P_+$, and $AP_-=-J\cdot\tfrac12(I+J)=\tfrac12(-J-I)=-P_-$. A projector has rank equal to its trace, and $\operatorname{tr}J=r\bmod 2$ (only the middle entry of the anti-diagonal lies on the diagonal, and only when $r$ is odd), so $\operatorname{rank}P_+=\tfrac12(r-(r\bmod2))=\lfloor r/2\rfloor$ and $\operatorname{rank}P_-=\tfrac12(r+(r\bmod2))=\lceil r/2\rceil$. This is a proof for every $r$; the script checks $r=1,\dots,12$ in exact arithmetic as a regression test.∎

Proposition 4 · poles are exchanged DERIVED

$R$ maps the hyperplane $H_k:\ s_1+\dots+s_k=k$ onto $H'_k:\ s_{r-k+1}+\dots+s_r=0$, and conversely. In particular, given the functional equation of Theorem 1.1, the two pole families of that theorem are consistent with each other.

For $s\in H_k$ the last $k$ coordinates of $R(s)$ are $1-s_k,\dots,1-s_1$ (reading in order), whose sum is $k-(s_1+\dots+s_k)=0$. Since $R^2=\mathrm{id}$ the image is all of $H'_k$.∎

The critical line is not the fixed locus

Proposition 5 · where the critical line really lives DERIVED

For $r=1$, $\mathrm{Fix}(R)$ is the single point $s=\tfrac12$, not the critical line. The critical line $\mathrm{Re}\,s=\tfrac12$ is the fixed set of the anti-holomorphic involution $\sigma(s)=1-\bar s$. For general $r$ let $\sigma(s)=R(\bar s)$. Then $\mathrm{Fix}(\sigma)=\{s_i+\overline{s_{r+1-i}}=1\}$ is a real-analytic submanifold of real dimension $r$, and it is totally real: at every point $T\cap JT=0$, with $J$ multiplication by $i$.

Fixed means $s_{r+1-i}=1-\bar s_i$ (middle coordinate: $\mathrm{Re}=\tfrac12$). Parametrise by $s_i\in\mathbb C$ for $i∎
Proposition 6 · reflection symmetry cannot confine zeros to the symmetric locus, for $r\ge2$ DERIVED

Let $f$ be meromorphic on $\mathbb C^r$, $r\ge2$, with a non-empty zero divisor $Z$ (the zero set of its numerator, a complex hypersurface of dimension $r-1$). Then $Z\not\subset\mathrm{Fix}(\sigma)$. Likewise $Z\subset\mathrm{Fix}(R)$ is impossible for $r\ge3$.

At a regular point $p\in Z$, $T_pZ$ is a complex subspace of dimension $r-1\ge1$; so if $v\in T_pZ$, $v\ne0$, then $Jv\in T_pZ$. If $Z\subset\mathrm{Fix}(\sigma)$ then $T_pZ\subset T_p\mathrm{Fix}(\sigma)$ and $0\ne v\in T\cap JT$, contradicting Proposition 5. For $R$: $\mathrm{Fix}(R)$ has complex dimension $\lfloor r/2\rfloor$ while $Z$ has $r-1$, and $r-1\le\lfloor r/2\rfloor$ only for $r\le2$.∎

Consequence. For $r=1$ the zeros are points and the line is a legitimate home (this is the Riemann hypothesis, stated as a hypothesis). For $r\ge2$ any zero-location statement for $\xi(s_1,\dots,s_r)$ must have a different shape: for example a statement about the intersection of $Z$ with a real slice, about specialisations to one variable, or a spectral or positivity statement. “All zeros on the symmetric locus” is not available, and this is a theorem about dimensions, not an observation about $\xi$. No claim is made that $Z$ is non-empty.

Explicit counterexamples to “invariance confines zeros”

  • $r=1$: $g(s)=s(1-s)$ satisfies $g(1-s)=g(s)$; its zeros $0,1$ are not the fixed point $\tfrac12$ and not on $\mathrm{Re}\,s=\tfrac12$.
  • $r=2$: $f(s_1,s_2)=s_1-s_2$ satisfies $f\circ R=f$. Its zero set $\{s_1=s_2\}$ meets $\mathrm{Fix}(R)=\{s_1+s_2=1\}$ only at $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$. The point $(0.3+2i,0.3+2i)$ is a zero with $s_1+s_2\ne1$.
  • $r=1$, inside the strip: $F(s)=(s-\tfrac12)^2-\tfrac1{16}$ satisfies $F(1-s)=F(s)$ and $F(\bar s)=\overline{F(s)}$, yet its zeros are $s=\tfrac14$ and $s=\tfrac34$, inside $0<\mathrm{Re}\,s<1$ and off the line $\mathrm{Re}\,s=\tfrac12$. Proof: $(s-\tfrac12)^2=\tfrac1{16}$ iff $s-\tfrac12=\pm\tfrac14$; invariance under $s\mapsto1-s$ holds because $(1-s-\tfrac12)^2=(s-\tfrac12)^2$, and real coefficients give invariance under conjugation. Reflection symmetry and conjugation symmetry together therefore do not localise zeros.
  • Any $h$: $F(s)=h(s)+h(Rs)$ is $R$-invariant for arbitrary $h$.

DERIVED; each checked in the script. They refute an inference from symmetry alone. They are not statements about $\xi$ and do not bear on the Riemann hypothesis.

Part VIII · The Weil perspective


The one place the analogy is a theorem


Over finite fields, the zeta function of a curve has a rational form, a functional equation $T\leftrightarrow 1/(qT)$, and its zeros lie on the line of absolute value $\sqrt q$: Weil’s theorem (1948), extended to varieties by Deligne (1974). ESTABLISHED (see the Weil chapter of this book, which also records that Grothendieck’s intended route via the standard conjectures is still open.) The functional equation there is a reflection; the proof that zeros are confined goes through cohomology and positivity, not through the reflection.


Brown’s $R$ is the several-variable analogue of the reflection, and Proposition 6 says the reflection alone cannot do the confining even in a single extra variable. The finite-field case supplies what is missing there — a positivity (Hodge-index type) input — and no analogue of that input is known for $\xi(s_1,\dots,s_r)$. ANALOGY / OPEN
