A Bucket of Seawater

A virologist opened a course by saying there are more viruses in a bucket of seawater than people on Earth, and that we swim in it and are fine. This chapter starts there, with the arithmetic, and then runs the author's own fruit-fly swarm model to see what it really does. It turns out to be richer than the paper said: many stable states, then cycles near the coupling the paper predicted. The contraction proof the paper printed could not have worked, and the chapter shows why. Every number is computed by ch-a-bucket-of-seawater-verify.py before it is printed.
1 · A bucket of seawater

More viruses than people

In 2020, as Columbia finished taping its introductory virology lectures, the pandemic arrived and the course went up on YouTube. The author took it there. As he recalls the opening, the lecturer, Vincent Racaniello, said he had always been fascinated by viruses, talked about the microbes we carry and depend on, and said there are more viruses in a bucket of ocean water than people on Earth. We swim in it and we are fine, so they are not all bad. MODEL

The arithmetic holds with room to spare. Suttle's reviews of marine viruses report about 107 viruses per millilitre of typical surface seawater. A 10-litre bucket is 104 millilitres, so it holds about 1011 viruses, 12.5 times the 8 billion people the world passed in 2022. Even at ten times fewer viruses per millilitre, the bucket still wins. Most of those viruses infect bacteria and algae, not people. COMPUTED

From the classroom

The author, who has taught since 2000, tells his students that some people are fascinated by this topic. Students who are repulsed at first soon realise that viruses are all over them. On the board goes a rule that follows from both facts: be wise, sanitize. Most viruses are harmless, and the few that are not are a good reason to wash your hands.

2 · A swarm in a fly's brain

The toy model

Nogueira Grossi's Biological Transitions as Multi-Agent Realisations of the Generative Operator Pipeline (V2, May 2026) takes its motivation from the FlyWire connectome of the adult fruit-fly brain, about 140,000 neurons. It abstracts the brain to N clusters, each a point (x, y) in the plane, each coupled to its neighbour in a ring with strength α. One step applies G = U∘F∘K∘C: C keeps the larger coordinate and zeroes the other, K adds a bias b = 0.1 away from zero and clips to [−1, 1], F mixes in the neighbour, (1 − α)s + αn, and U rescales the result to length 1. The paper says the swarm contracts to a unique fixed point for |α| < 1/2, with Lipschitz constant L(α) = 1/2 + |α|, and that a pitchfork bifurcation, a stress switch like the HPA axis, happens at α = 1/2. For weak coupling every run settles, and every run settles somewhere different: 40 starts, 40 end states. The model is multistable. It has many stable states, and which one the swarm reaches depends on where it began. The first cycles appear at α = 0.42 (period 24), period-16 cycles follow at 0.45, and for 0.5–0.65 more runs cycle than settle; and from α = 0.7 some runs neither settle nor repeat within 400 steps. So something does change close to the paper's threshold of 1/2. It is not the change the paper describes (one state splitting into two); it is a move from many resting states to cycles and irregular motion. Settling is also fast: at α = 0.3 six steps bring each run to within 1.5% of its own end state, far inside the 27% the paper proved it could promise. COMPUTED

4 · Why the printed proof could not work

A jump, four fixed points and a flat line

C jumps. The points (1, 0.999) and (0.999, 1) are 0.002 apart, but C sends them to (1, 0) and (0, 1), which are 2 apart. At α = 0.3 the whole step G sends them 1.32 apart. A map that jumps has no Lipschitz constant at all, so no value of L(α) can bound it. In the V2 Lean file, L(α) = 1/2 + |α| is a definition, and the theorems about it (for example L(0.3) = 4/5) are arithmetic about that definition, never connected to G. COMPUTED

Four fixed points where one was promised. At α = 0 the formula gives L = 1/2, a strong contraction, which would force a single fixed point. A single agent has four: (1, 0.1), (−1, 0.1), (0.1, 1) and (0.1, −1), each divided by √1.01. BioSwarmCheck.four_fixed_points proves all four fixed and distinct, and C_jumps proves the jump. KERNEL-CHECKED

The bifurcation figure is flat. The paper's figure 4 plots the mean of |s|² after six steps against α. But U rescales every state to length 1, so without noise that quantity is exactly 1 at every coupling (U_norm). The 80 plotted values lie between 0.9989 and 1.0010, a spread of 0.0021 that is only the added noise. The figure could not show a bifurcation, and the real transition in section 3 is invisible in it. KERNEL-CHECKED

The V2 Lean file's three "open obligations" contain no sorry: two conclude True, and the third holds because the swarm step is an identity placeholder. 5 · Back to the laws of Book XIV

One fixed point or many

Book XIV, chapter 10 ran the Swarm Simulator and found one fixed point, 0, with shared intent dying out like a chain of steps, It = 0.416t. Its multi-orbit claim of distinct fixed points failed. The fly model is the opposite case: the multi-orbit picture holds here, since separate runs end in separate stable states. Put together, the two papers have the ingredients a living swarm needs, which neither has alone. MODEL

Going viral. In everyday speech things "go viral", and that can be good or bad: a kind idea spreads the same way a rumour does. The swarm model has the arithmetic in its type-propagation step, Mt+1 = m·Mt. If each carrier passes a type on to fewer than one new carrier (m < 1) it fades: with the Swarm Simulator's default m = 0.231 it falls to 4.3 × 10−7 of its size in ten steps. If m is even a little above 1 it grows: at m = 1.1 it is 2.59 times larger after ten steps. Epidemiology calls the same threshold the reproduction number. Whether a given idea or virus has m above 1 is a question for data, and the model does not answer it. COMPUTED

The same chapter's parallel law, 1 − (1 − q)n, ends with a ceiling: if a share of errors is invisible to every checker, adding checkers never catches them. An immune repertoire that shares a blind spot is the same shape, which is one reason a new variant can pass many defences at once. That is an analogy, not a result, and it would need real immune data to test. MODEL

Computer viruses and the Trojan horse. In computing the word is almost always bad. A computer virus is a program that copies itself into other programs, the same multiply-and-spread step as Mt+1 = m·Mt with m above 1. A Trojan horse is named after the wooden horse of the Troy story: a gift the city pulled inside its own walls, with soldiers hidden in it. The program looks useful, so people install it themselves, and it carries something harmful inside. In the language of the parallel law it is built to sit in the shared blind spot: every checker that judges by the outside says yes, so adding more of the same kind of checker does not help. The defence is a different kind of check, one that looks inside. It is the same reason this series runs a script and a proof, not only a reading, before a claim is printed. MODEL
