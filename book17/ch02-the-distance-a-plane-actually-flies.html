The Distance a Plane Actually Flies

Put Brasília and New York on the Distance Formula's own grid and read off what the formula gives you. It is a real number. It is not the distance between the cities — and the gap between the two is the whole lesson, drawn live instead of assumed away.

Part I · The Textbook's Own Moment

Where this picks up

Stitz & Zeager's open-source Precalculus (held in this corpus's source folder, §1.1.2–1.1.3) builds the Cartesian plane, then the Distance Formula, from one right triangle: two points $P(x_0,y_0)$ and $Q(x_1,y_1)$, legs $|x_1-x_0|$ and $|y_1-y_0|$, hypotenuse $d$, Pythagoras. CITED

Equation 1.1 · the Distance Formula

$$d=\sqrt{(x_1-x_0)^2+(y_1-y_0)^2}$$

Its own worked exercise for this (Exercise 35, §1.1.4) is “Sasquatch is 3 miles West and 4 miles South of our current position” — a toy example, on purpose, because the formula only means miles there by declaration: the book sets up the axes in miles before asking the question. CITED Put two real cities' coordinates into the same formula instead, and the units stop being free.

Part II · The Grid This Actually Runs On

Two fixed axes, not one

The formula needs the two-perpendicular-axis grid to work — the fixed picture ch-descartes traces to Frans van Schooten's 1649 edition, not to Descartes' own 1637 single reference axis. Put Brasília (15.7939°S, 47.8828°W) and New York City (40.7128°N, 74.0060°W) on it, reading west longitude and south latitude as negative — the ordinary sign convention, not a new one — and each city is a point $(\text{lon}, \text{lat})$ on exactly the grid the formula assumes.

Part III · Two Numbers, Honestly Kept Apart

What the formula gives you, against what is true

reading the formula literally

$\Delta\text{lon} = -74.0060 - (-47.8828) = -26.1232$°, $\Delta\text{lat} = 40.7128 - (-15.7939) = 56.5067$°.

$$d=\sqrt{(-26.1232)^2+(56.5067)^2}\approx62.25\ \text{degrees}$$

62.25 is a real output of Equation 1.1. It is not a distance in miles, km, or anything physical. The formula does not know its inputs are angles on a sphere; it treats a degree of longitude and a degree of latitude as the same size unit, added under one square root, exactly the way it treats Sasquatch's 3 miles West and 4 miles South. COMPUTED

why they are not the same size

A degree of latitude is 111.20 km everywhere on Earth, because meridians are great circles of equal size. A degree of longitude is not constant — meridians converge toward the poles, so it shrinks with the cosine of latitude: 107.00 km per degree at Brasília's latitude (15.79°), 84.28 km per degree at New York's (40.71°) — a fifth less, from latitude alone. Equation 1.1 has no cosine in it. It cannot know this. COMPUTED

the real distance

The great-circle distance — shortest path over the actual sphere, haversine formula, mean Earth radius 6371.0088 km — is 6837.2 km (4248 mi). That is the number a flight between the two cities is close to. It shares nothing with 62.25 except that both came from a distance formula — a different one, on a sphere, not a plane. COMPUTED

Part VI · The One Formal Claim

What is actually kernel-checked here, and what is not

Lean, and its honest status

Book17Ch02.lean states and proves three small, self-contained real-number facts about the squared-distance function $d^2(x_0,y_0,x_1,y_1) = (x_1-x_0)^2+(y_1-y_0)^2$ that Part I–III lean on: it is symmetric, it is non-negative, and $\sqrt{d^2}^{\,2}=d^2$. Unlike Book17Core.lean and Book17Mathlib.lean, this file has not been run against a real kernel — there is no Lean toolchain in this session. The proofs are hand-checked algebra (ring and positivity should close them; the exact Mathlib lemma name for the third fact is a best guess, not a confirmed one), written the way this corpus's own rule requires: stated plainly as untested, not quietly assumed. UNTESTED

Everything else on this page — the two distances, the km-per-degree figures, the geographic facts, the meeting — is either a live Python computation (book17/ch02-verify.py, standard library, re-run every time this claim is checked) or a cited, dated web source. Neither is a Lean claim, and this page does not dress either one up as one.

What this chapter does not claim

That the great-circle distance is what a real flight covers exactly — commercial routing follows air corridors and wind, not a pure geodesic, so 6837 km is a lower bound on any real flight, not its length.
