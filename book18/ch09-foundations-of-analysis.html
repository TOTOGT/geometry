Foundations of Analysis

The earlier chapters kept saying a limit exists. This chapter asks what makes that allowed: the real numbers have no holes, and the rationals do. It checks that claim, three theorems that depend on it, and the difference between pointwise and uniform convergence, with a control wherever a hypothesis is removed.

Every limit in the earlier chapters is a real number that was not on the list when the sequence began. A sequence of rationals can close in on a number that is not rational, so the claim that the limit exists has to come from somewhere. It comes from one property of the real numbers: a nonempty set with an upper bound has a least upper bound.

§ 1

The rationals have a hole

Take the rationals whose square is less than 2. Any rational u > 0 with u2 > 2 is an upper bound for that set, and it is never the least one: the rational u′ = (u2 + 2)/(2u) is smaller and still an upper bound, because u′2 − 2 = (u2 − 2)2/(4u2) > 0 and u − u′ = (u2 − 2)/(2u) > 0. Starting at 3/2 this gives 3/2, 17/12, 577/408, each smaller than the last and each with square above 2; 17/12 squared is 289/144, which is 2 + 1/144. The same holds from below: for a rational x ≥ 0 with x2 < 2, the rational x′ = (2x + 2)/(x + 2) is larger and still has square below 2, since x′2 − 2 = (2x2 − 4)/(x + 2)2. So the set has no least rational upper bound.

The step that remains is that no rational has square 2: if p2 = 2q2 with p/q in lowest terms, then p is even, say p = 2r, so q2 = 2r2 and q is even too, against lowest terms. This is Lebl’s Example 1.1.4 (p.24), where the property that fails is Definition 1.1.3, the least-upper-bound property. The script checks both update formulas on 1,187 fractions above √2 and 673 below it (denominators up to 30, not reduced, so some values repeat), and finds no solution of p2 = 2q2 with q up to 3,000. The finite checks test the formulas; the parity argument is what proves the claim. Lean: newton_improves and control_no_rational_sqrt_two are proved from field arithmetic and Mathlib’s irrationality of √2; completeness is Mathlib’s Real.exists_isLUB. None of it has been compiled.

§ 2

What completeness gives: three theorems

A bounded sequence has a convergent part (Bolzano–Weierstrass, Lebl Thm 2.3.8, p.78). The sequence xn = (−1)n(1 + 1/n) is bounded by 2 and does not converge, but its even terms satisfy x2k − 1 = 1/(2k) exactly, and its odd terms x2k−1 + 1 = −1/(2k−1) exactly, so one subsequence tends to 1 and the other to −1. Control: xn = n is unbounded and has no convergent subsequence, since any strictly increasing choice of indices has nk ≥ k.

A continuous function crosses every value between two of its values (Bolzano’s intermediate value theorem, Thm 3.3.8, p.133). Take f(x) = x3 − x − 1 on [1, 2], where f(1) = −1 and f(2) = 5. Bisection (Example 3.3.9, p.134), in exact arithmetic, keeps f(a) < 0 < f(b) at each of 40 steps and traps a root in an interval of width exactly 2−40, at about 1.3247179572. The only rational candidates for a root, ±1, are not roots, so the root the bisection finds is not a rational: the theorem is delivering a number that is not on the rational list. Two controls. Over the rationals, the same bisection for x2 − 2 on [0, 2] never lands on a zero in 60 steps: the function changes sign, the interval shrinks to width 2−59, and there is no rational to land on. And a step function that is −1 below 1/2 and +1 from 1/2 changes sign on [0, 1] without ever being zero: continuity is a hypothesis.

A continuous function on a closed interval attains its maximum and minimum (Thm 3.3.2, p.130). For x(1 − x) on [0, 1] the identity 1/4 − x(1 − x) = (x − 1/2)2 shows the maximum is 1/4, attained only at 1/2 (checked exactly on a grid of 201 points). Controls: x on the open interval (0, 1) has no maximum, because (x + 1)/2 is larger; and 1/x on (0, 1] is unbounded, with value n at x = 1/n. Closed and bounded matter.

Lean: bolzano_weierstrass, ivt and extreme_value call Mathlib’s own theorems (tendsto_subseq_of_bounded, intermediate_value_Icc, IsCompact.exists_isMaxOn and exists_isMinOn); once compiled they would check the statements, not Lebl’s proofs.

§ 3

Pointwise is not uniform

A sequence of functions converges pointwise if it converges at each point separately, and uniformly if one index N works for every point at once (Definition 6.1.6, p.229). Take fn(x) = xn on [0, 1). At each point tried (1/2, 9/10, 99/100) the powers fall below 1/1000 eventually, so the pointwise limit is 0. But at xn = 1 − 1/(2n) the value is at least 1/2 for every n, by Bernoulli’s inequality (1 − t)n ≥ 1 − nt; the script checks it exactly for n up to 300. So for ε = 1/2 no N works, and the convergence is not uniform (compare Lebl’s Example 6.1.8, p.230, for x2n on [−1, 1]). Each xn is continuous on [0, 1], yet at x = 1 every one equals 1 while the limit elsewhere is 0: the limit jumps, which Theorem 6.2.2 (p.235) forbids under uniform convergence.

A uniform case: fn(x) = x/(1 + nx2). Since (1 + nx2)2 − 4nx2 = (1 − nx2)2 ≥ 0, we get |fn(x)| ≤ 1/(2√n) for every x, with equality at x = 1/k when n = k2. So for ε = 1/100, any N > 2500 works for all x at once. Lean: uniform_limit_continuous, Mathlib’s TendstoUniformly.continuous, uncompiled.

§ 4

Where the earlier chapters leaned on this

Chapter 7 uses the monotone convergence theorem (Lebl Thm 2.1.10, p.55): a bounded increasing sequence converges. The standard proof takes the supremum of the terms, and that supremum exists by completeness; in the rationals the sequence 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, … of decimal truncations of √2 is bounded and increasing and has no rational limit. That is the sense in which chapter 7’s steps were allowed: they were taken in the real numbers. This chapter checks the property and its consequences on examples; it does not trace each earlier step back to it.

§ 5

What is not here

  • Metric spaces, open and closed sets, and compactness in general. The index row for this chapter mentioned them; they are the subject of Lebl’s later chapters and are not covered here, and the extreme value theorem above is only the interval case.
  • Exchanging a limit with an integral or a derivative (Lebl Thm 6.2.4, p.237, and Thm 6.2.10, p.239), Dini’s theorem, and the Weierstrass M-test.
  • Proofs of the three theorems in § 2. The page gives the examples and the controls, and cites Lebl for the proofs.
  • Compiled status. Foundations.lean has not been compiled. Five of its seven proofs are calls to Mathlib, so it checks that the textbook statements are true as formal sentences, not that any hand argument here is right. The two proofs that are not bare library calls are newton_improves and control_no_rational_sqrt_two, and they are the likeliest to need a fix after the first compile.
  • Line-by-line agreement with Lebl. The statements are confirmed to sit on the cited pages (ch09-verify.py [8]); the chapter’s own wording has not been compared with his proofs. [OPEN]
