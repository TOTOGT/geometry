Latent Capacity

Acquired savant syndrome, and where computation lives. After some brain injuries, people gain skills they did not have before. This chapter follows that question down four levels — a person, neurons in a dish, a mapped fly brain, and matter itself — and at each level asks the same four operator questions, with a status for every answer.

The chapter uses the same guide as every other chapter in Book 3 (see The g-series, “Translation and reintegration”). For each level it names the state and the control, says which of the four operators is claimed, supported or skipped, and gives a status: established, model, demonstration, analogy or open. The operator tests are the ones used throughout:

  • C — is there one slow variable that the rest follows?
  • K — does a driver reach a repeatable threshold?
  • F — near the threshold, does the change accelerate as a square root, slow down as 1/√, and then jump? Do curves from different cases collapse onto one?
  • U — when the driver is reversed, does the system stay changed (hysteresis, a residue)?

1 · The human case (in vivo)

Acquired savant syndrome is the appearance of a new skill — in drawing, music, calculation or memory — after a brain injury, a stroke or a degenerative disease, in a person who did not show it before. Darold Treffert collected the cases and gave the name; they are rare, and most of what is known comes from case reports. The best-documented group is people with frontotemporal dementia who began to paint or compose as the disease progressed, often with damage concentrated in the left anterior temporal lobe (Miller et al., Neurology 1998).

Neuropsychology has a wider name for the pattern: paradoxical functional facilitation — a lesion that improves some function rather than only removing one (Kapur, Brain 1996). The usual explanation is release from inhibition: one region, when damaged, stops suppressing another.

Allan Snyder turned that explanation into a hypothesis that could be tested in healthy people. If the left anterior temporal lobe filters raw detail into concepts, briefly suppressing it should give access to the detail. In a small study using repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, some participants estimated the number of dots on a screen more accurately for a short time after stimulation (Snyder et al., Perception 2006). The effects were small, the samples small, replications mixed, and the change faded within about an hour.

Operator reading

  • C: the slow variable would be the strength of top-down filtering. Claimed, not measured.
  • K: the constraint change is the injury or the stimulation. Supported as a trigger; no threshold has been shown.
  • F: case reports describe skills that appear over months, not a jump. Skipped — there are no time courses to test.
  • U: this is where the two cases differ. After injury or dementia the change persists (a residue); after rTMS it does not (no residue). That contrast is the most useful thing this level offers.

Status: model. The disinhibition account is a reasonable model with thin quantitative support.

2 · Living tissue (in vitro)

DishBrain. Kagan et al. (Neuron 2022) grew human and mouse neurons on a multi-electrode array and connected them to a simulated game of Pong. Stimulation told the culture where the ball was; when the paddle missed, the culture received unpredictable stimulation, and when it hit, predictable stimulation. Over about twenty minutes of play, rallies became longer than in controls. The result is that feedback built on predictability shapes activity in cultured neurons. The paper's use of the word “sentience” was criticised in published responses, and this chapter does not use it.

CL1 and Doom. In March 2026 Cortical Labs showed neurons on its CL1 system playing a version of Doom. It is a company demonstration, not a peer-reviewed result, and sceptics pointed out how much of the play depends on the encoding and decoding around the cells. It shows the interface works; it does not yet show learning.

Organoid intelligence. Smirnova et al. (Frontiers in Science 2023) set out a research programme for computing with brain organoids, together with its ethics. It is a roadmap, not a finding.

A link to regeneration. The substrates studied in Ch Λ · Polylaminin are first tested on the same kind of preparation — cultured neurons on a surface — so the dish is also where repair and computation meet.

Operator reading

  • C: a slow variable such as mean firing predictability could be defined from the recordings. Not yet done.
  • K: the driver is the feedback rule. Supported: changing the feedback changes the outcome.
  • F: open. Does performance jump, or improve gradually? The rally curves could answer it.
  • U: open. Does the change persist once feedback stops?

Status: DishBrain established as a feedback effect; CL1 Doom demonstration; organoid intelligence open.

3 · Wiring (in silico)

The fly connectome. The FlyWire consortium reconstructed the whole brain of an adult female Drosophila melanogaster from electron microscopy — about 139,000 neurons and some 50 million synapses — and published it in Nature in 2024 (Dorkenwald et al.). The map was produced by automated segmentation and then proofread by people; it is a reconstruction, checked by hand, not a formal verification.

A model on the map. Shiu et al. (Nature 2024) built a simple spiking model on the whole connectome, with every neuron a leaky integrate-and-fire unit, and used it to predict which neurons carry taste and grooming signals; several predictions were then confirmed in flies. The code is open, which matters here: it means the proposal below can be run by anyone.

A counterexample. “Less inhibition, more ability” is not a general rule. In the fly's mushroom body a single inhibitory neuron, APL, keeps odour coding sparse. Blocking it makes coding less sparse and impairs the fly's ability to tell similar odours apart (Lin et al., Nature Neuroscience 2014). Any disinhibition account has to say why removing inhibition helps in one place and hurts in another.

A proposal (not a result). Run in-silico lesions of specific inhibitory populations in the whole-brain model, and look for any readout that improves — abruptly, and in a way that persists. A null result would also be informative.

Operator reading

  • C: in the model, the slow variable could be the total inhibitory weight onto a region. Definable.
  • K: the lesion size is the driver. Testable in the model.
  • F: open — is there a lesion size at which a readout jumps?
  • U: in a model without plasticity, removing the lesion restores the original behaviour, so no residue is expected; a residue would need plasticity added.

Status: connectome and model established data; the lesion test open (proposal).

4 · Substrate (matter)

Computronium. Toffoli and Margolus (Physica D 1991) used the word for matter arranged to compute as efficiently as possible — “programmable matter”. The physics that bounds it is well established: erasing a bit costs at least kT ln 2 of energy (Landauer 1961), and Lloyd (Nature 2000) worked out the ultimate limits on speed and memory for a kilogram of matter in a litre.

Tubulin. Ch T read microtubules as a kind of computronium. Its numbers did not pass the checks there (TubulinChecks.lean): the critical curvature it gives is more than a hundred times the measured GDP curvature, and the φ value it cites is off. Microtubule computation in the brain (Penrose–Hameroff) is a minority view and disputed. The reading stays an analogy.

Operator reading

  • C, K: the Landauer and Lloyd bounds are limits, not control variables; there is no fold here.
  • F, U: skipped.

Status: physical limits established; tubulin as computronium analogy.

Read across, the table says what the Coherence Bridge is for in this chapter. It does not claim that a person, a dish and a fly share a mechanism. It gives each field the same questions, so that a neurologist, a cell biologist and a modeller can see which of them could answer which one. The person level has the residue contrast; the dish has the time courses; the fly model has the controlled lesion. None has all four.

6 · Open questions and tests

  • Does DishBrain or CL1 performance jump or improve gradually? Fit the published rally curves for a square-root approach and a jump.
  • Does the change persist once feedback stops? Measure performance after a pause with no feedback.
  • Do in-silico lesions of inhibitory populations in the fly model produce any gain, and does its size change abruptly with lesion size?
  • For acquired savant cases, are there time courses — first appearance of the skill, rate of change — that could be tested against the F signature?

7 · Cautions

  • “Sentience” appears here only as the subject of an ethics debate; no result in this chapter shows it.
  • CL1 Doom is a demonstration, not a peer-reviewed result.
  • Tubulin as computronium is an analogy; its numbers failed the checks in Ch T.
  • There is no theorem here that mind has one structure across substrates. That claim belongs to the holology face of Book 8 (Ch 13), where it is a conjecture.
  • No Lean file accompanies this chapter yet: its claims are empirical, and nothing here is a checkable mathematical statement.
