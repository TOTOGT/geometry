Unit 1 · Introduction to Organizational Theory and Behavior

Organizational behavior (OB) studies how individuals, groups and structures shape what people do at work, and uses that to predict and improve performance. Three levels recur through the whole course: the individual (personality, perception, motivation), the group (teams, conflict, communication), and the organization (structure, culture, change). A good answer to most OB questions says which level the problem lives at, because the fix differs.

OB is an applied social science, so its claims are probabilistic: a theory says what tends to happen on average under stated conditions, and the honest verdict on most theories is “useful in these settings”, not “true”. Expect the course to reward you for naming the conditions under which a theory applies.

Self-check Pick a problem you have seen at work (missed deadlines, a team that will not speak up). At which of the three levels does it live, and what would you check first to be sure?

Unit 2 · Organizational Concepts and Theories

Classical theory treats the organization as a machine to be made efficient. Scientific management (Taylor, 1911) studies tasks and standardizes the one best way. Administrative theory (Fayol, 1916) lists the functions of management and principles such as unity of command. Bureaucracy (Weber) describes rule-based, hierarchical, impersonal organization as a source of fairness and predictability.

The human relations turn came from the Hawthorne studies at Western Electric in the 1920s and 30s, which showed that attention, group norms and social needs moved output, not only physical conditions. Later views add that no single design is best: contingency theory says the right structure depends on the environment, technology and size, and systems theory treats the organization as an open system taking inputs from, and returning outputs to, its environment.

Unit 3 · Employee Motivation and Rewards

Motivation theories fall into two families. Content theories say what people want: Maslow's hierarchy (physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization; 1943), Herzberg's two factors (hygiene factors such as pay and conditions prevent dissatisfaction, while motivators such as achievement and recognition build satisfaction; 1959), and McClelland's needs for achievement, affiliation and power. Process theories say how motivation is produced: Vroom's expectancy theory, Adams's equity theory, and goal-setting theory.

Expectancy theory (Vroom, 1964) models effort as the product of three beliefs: expectancy (effort leads to performance), instrumentality (performance leads to a reward) and valence (how much the reward is wanted). Because it is a product, a zero in any term gives zero motivation. With illustrative values E = 0.8, I = 0.5, V = 6, the motivational force is 0.8 × 0.5 × 6 = 2.4 on that scale; the numbers only show the structure.

Equity theory (Adams, 1963) says people compare their ratio of outcomes to inputs with a reference person's and act to remove perceived unfairness. Goal-setting theory (Locke and Latham) says specific, difficult, accepted goals with feedback raise performance more than “do your best”. Reinforcement theory (Skinner) explains behavior by its consequences. Reward design should match the theory: pay is a hygiene factor for Herzberg, a valued outcome for expectancy, and a fairness signal for equity.

Self-check A bonus is paid for team output, but one member believes her effort does not change the result. Which term of expectancy theory is low, and what change to the reward system would raise it?

Unit 4 · Attitudes, Perceptions, and Behaviors

An attitude has a feeling (affect), a belief (cognition) and a behavioral tendency. Job satisfaction and organizational commitment are the attitudes OB tracks most; they relate to turnover and absence more clearly than to output. Festinger's cognitive dissonance (1957) says people reduce the discomfort of inconsistency between attitudes and behavior, which is why someone who takes a job for the pay may later come to believe in the mission.

Perception is how people select and interpret information, so two people can see the same event differently. Attribution theory asks whether behavior is explained by the person or the situation. Kelley's model uses consistency, distinctiveness and consensus. The fundamental attribution error (named by Ross, 1977) is the tendency to over-credit the person and under-credit the situation when judging others. Other perceptual shortcuts include the halo effect, stereotyping, and the self-fulfilling prophecy (the Pygmalion effect: expectations shape performance).

Personality is usually described with the Big Five: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism (emotional stability). Conscientiousness predicts job performance across many jobs, though the effect is modest, so personality is a poor basis for rigid selection rules.

Self-check A manager sees an employee arrive late twice and concludes the person is careless. Name the bias, and list two situational explanations the manager should rule out first.

Unit 5 · Organizational Communication

The basic model (Shannon and Weaver, 1949) has a sender who encodes a message, a channel, a receiver who decodes it, and noise that distorts it; feedback closes the loop. Most workplace failures are decoding or noise failures, not sending failures.

Communication runs downward (instructions, goals), upward (reports, concerns), and laterally (coordination), through formal channels and the informal grapevine. Channel choice matters: Daft and Lengel's media richness theory says ambiguous, emotional messages need rich media (face to face, video) and routine messages suit lean ones (email, notices). Common barriers are filtering, selective perception, information overload, status differences and cultural differences in directness.

Good practice is concrete: state the purpose, check understanding by asking for a paraphrase, and match the channel to the message. Active listening (paraphrasing, asking open questions, not interrupting) is the main receiver-side skill.

Self-check A policy change is announced only by email and half the staff misread it. Using media richness, what channel mix would you use next time, and why?

Unit 6 · Collaborative Dynamics: Groups and Teams

A group is two or more people interacting; a team adds interdependent work toward a shared goal. Tuckman's model (1965) gives four stages, with a fifth added later: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning. It describes a typical path, not a law, and teams can loop back.

Group processes cut both ways. Social loafing (Latané; building on Ringelmann's rope-pulling finding) is reduced effort when individual contributions are not visible. Groupthink (Janis, 1972) is a push for agreement that suppresses dissent, helped by cohesion, isolation and a directive leader; remedies include a designated devil's advocate and outside review. Diverse teams can bring more information but also more friction, so the net effect depends on how the team is managed.

Effective teams tend to have clear goals, defined roles, mutual trust, and norms that make it safe to raise problems. Roles, norms and cohesion are the standard group-structure terms.

Self-check A team agrees quickly on every proposal and later misses an obvious risk. Which stage and which group problem does this suggest, and what one change would you make to the meeting?

Unit 7 · Leadership, Power, and Conflict

Leadership theories move from traits (who the leader is), to behaviors (task versus people orientation; the Ohio State and Michigan studies), to contingency views: Fiedler (1967) matches leader style to situational favorableness, and House's path-goal theory (1971) says a leader clears the path to goals by choosing directive, supportive, participative or achievement-oriented behavior to suit the follower and task. Transformational leadership (Burns, 1978; Bass, 1985) inspires followers beyond self-interest; transactional leadership exchanges rewards for performance. Leader–member exchange (LMX) focuses on the quality of each leader–follower relationship.

Power is the capacity to influence others. French and Raven (1959) listed five bases: legitimate, reward, coercive, expert and referent; informational power was added later. Expert and referent power come from the person; the others come from the position.

Conflict can be about tasks, relationships or process; moderate task conflict can help, relationship conflict rarely does. The Thomas–Kilmann model (1974) has five handling modes on two axes, assertiveness and cooperativeness: competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding and accommodating. No mode is always best; collaborating suits important issues with time, avoiding suits trivial ones.

Self-check Two department heads dispute budget priorities and both want a win. Which Thomas–Kilmann mode would you try first, and what would make you switch to compromising?

Unit 8 · Organizational Culture and Change

Schein describes culture at three levels: visible artifacts (dress, rituals, layout), espoused values (stated principles), and basic assumptions (taken-for-granted beliefs that really drive behavior). The gap between espoused values and assumptions is where culture problems hide. Hofstede's research on national cultures proposed dimensions such as power distance, individualism, uncertainty avoidance and masculinity versus femininity; it is widely used and also criticized for sampling and for treating nations as uniform.

Change meets resistance from habit, fear of loss, and poor communication. Lewin's model (1947) has three phases: unfreeze (create readiness), change (move to the new state), refreeze (anchor the new practice). Kotter's eight steps expand it: create urgency, build a guiding coalition, form a vision, communicate it, empower action, secure short-term wins, consolidate gains, and anchor the change in culture. Change that ignores culture usually fades back.

Self-check An organization states that it values candor, but people are punished for bad news. Which of Schein's levels is out of line with which, and what does that predict for a change program?
