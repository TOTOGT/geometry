Unit 1 · Modular Arithmetic

a ≡ b (mod n) means n divides a - b: the two numbers leave the same remainder when divided by n. Think of a clock. Congruence respects addition, subtraction and multiplication, so you may reduce at every step: (a + b) mod n = ((a mod n) + (b mod n)) mod n, and the same for products. Division is the exception and needs an inverse (Unit 2). computed (-7) mod 5 = 3, and 7³ mod 5 = 343 mod 5 = 3, which equals (7 mod 5)³ = 2³ = 8 mod 5 = 3. Modular arithmetic is the arithmetic of finite sets of numbers, which is why computers and ciphers use it.

Self-check Compute 123 × 456 mod 10 by reducing first, then check it against the full product.

Unit 2 · Primes, GCD and Euclid's Algorithm

A prime has exactly two divisors; every integer greater than 1 factors uniquely into primes (the fundamental theorem of arithmetic). The greatest common divisor gcd(a, b) is found fast by Euclid's algorithm: gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b), stopping at remainder zero. The extended form also finds x and y with ax + by = gcd(a, b). computed gcd(240, 46) = 2, with 240 × -9 + 46 × 47 = 2. When gcd(a, n) = 1, x is the modular inverse of a mod n. For example the inverse of 17 mod 3120 is 2753 (17 × 2753 = 46801 = 15 × 3120 + 1). Euclid's algorithm takes about log n steps, so inverses are cheap even for huge numbers, while factoring a product of two large primes is believed to be hard. That asymmetry is the basis of RSA.

Self-check Use the extended Euclidean algorithm to find the inverse of 7 mod 40, and verify by multiplying.

Unit 3 · Fermat's and Euler's Theorems

Fermat's little theorem: if p is prime and p does not divide a, then ap-1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Euler's totient φ(n) counts the integers from 1 to n that are coprime to n; for a prime p, φ(p) = p - 1, and for a product of distinct primes φ(pq) = (p-1)(q-1). Euler's theorem: if gcd(a, n) = 1 then aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n). computed 210 mod 11 = 1, as Fermat predicts. Using that 710 ≡ 1 (mod 11), 7222 = 72 · (710)22 ≡ 49 mod 11 = 5. And φ(12) = 4 (the numbers 1, 5, 7, 11) and φ(3233) = 3120 = 60 × 52 for 3233 = 61 × 53.

Self-check Compute φ(15) and then 2φ(15) mod 15.

Unit 4 · Fast Exponentiation

Computing ae mod n by multiplying e times is hopeless for large e. Square-and-multiply reads the exponent in binary: square the running value at each bit and multiply in a once for each 1-bit, reducing mod n each time. The cost is about log₂ e steps. computed 6517 mod 3233 = 2790, and since 17 = 10001 in binary, it takes 6 modular multiplications (4 squarings and 2 multiplications), not 16.

Self-check Write 13 in binary and count the multiplications square-and-multiply needs for a13.

Unit 5 · Symmetric Ciphers and Why Keys Matter

A symmetric cipher uses one secret key to both encrypt and decrypt. The one-time pad XORs the message with a random key as long as the message, used once; it is perfectly secret but impractical because of key distribution. computed With message bits 01001000 and key bits 10110101, the ciphertext is 11111101, and XORing the key again returns 01001000. Practical ciphers such as AES expand a short key into a long stream-like transformation. Security rests on the key space: a key of k bits has 2k possibilities, so an 8-character password from 94 symbols carries about 52.4 bits (if random), far fewer than a 128-bit key. Old ciphers such as Caesar (26 keys) fall to trying every key, which is why key space is a minimum, not a guarantee. Do not design your own cipher; use vetted libraries.

Self-check XOR is its own inverse. Explain why reusing a one-time-pad key for two messages leaks information about both.

Unit 6 · The Public-Key Idea and Diffie-Hellman

Public-key cryptography splits the key into a public part and a private part, which solves key distribution. Diffie-Hellman lets two parties agree on a shared secret over an open channel. They fix a prime p and a base g, each picks a private exponent, publishes g raised to it mod p, and raises the other's public value to their own exponent; both reach gab mod p. computed With p = 23, g = 5, Alice's private 6 and Bob's private 15: Alice publishes 8, Bob publishes 19, and both compute the shared secret 2 (2 = 2). An eavesdropper sees p, g, 8 and 19 and must solve the discrete logarithm problem to recover the exponents, which is believed hard for large p. These numbers are far too small to be secure; real systems use primes of thousands of bits or elliptic curves, and DH alone does not authenticate who you are talking to.

Self-check Why does gab equal (ga)b and (gb)a mod p? Which rule of exponents is used?

Unit 7 · RSA

RSA uses two large primes p and q. Publish n = pq and an exponent e coprime to (p-1)(q-1); the private exponent d is the inverse of e mod (p-1)(q-1). Encryption is c = me mod n and decryption is m = cd mod n. It works because ed = 1 + k(p-1)(q-1) for some integer k, and by Euler's theorem med ≡ m (mod n). computed Toy values: p = 61, q = 53 give n = 3233 and (p-1)(q-1) = 3120. Take e = 17; then d = 2753. The message m = 65 encrypts to c = 6517 mod 3233 = 2790, and 27902753 mod 3233 = 65, recovering 65. Security rests on the difficulty of factoring n without knowing p and q. This toy is trivially breakable; real RSA uses primes of 1024 bits or more and padding (such as OAEP), because the plain textbook form is deterministic and malleable. The same math signs messages: sign with d, verify with e.

Self-check With p = 5, q = 11 and e = 3, find n, (p-1)(q-1) and d, then check that encrypting and decrypting 7 returns 7.

Unit 8 · Hashes, Signatures and Limits

A cryptographic hash maps any input to a fixed-size digest and is designed to be one-way, to make it hard to find two inputs with the same digest (collision resistance), and to change unpredictably when the input changes. computed SHA-256 of password1 and password2, which differ in one character, differ in 124 of 256 digest bits. Collisions are governed by the birthday bound: with an n-bit hash, collisions appear after about 2n/2 inputs. The probability that two among 23 people share a birthday is 0.5073 (above one half), and among 57 people 0.9901.

A digital signature signs a hash of a document with a private key, and anyone can verify it with the public key. Passwords should be stored using a slow, salted password hash, not a fast one. The limits of cryptography are important: it protects data in transit and at rest but does nothing about weak passwords, phishing, bad key handling or software bugs. Use standard, well-reviewed algorithms and libraries.

Self-check Explain why an attacker who can find collisions in a hash used for signatures can forge a signature.

What this chapter is not

It is not a course in applied cryptography or protocol design, and it says nothing about elliptic curves beyond a mention. It prepares the vocabulary: modular arithmetic, inverses, Euler, exponentiation, key space and hashing, and the book's own Examples 1.6, 1.28, 2.7 and 3.8 were re-run by b27-verify.py as a cross-check.
