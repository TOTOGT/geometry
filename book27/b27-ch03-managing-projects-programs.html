Unit 1 · Introduction to Project Management

A project is temporary, creates something unique, and has a defined end; a program is a group of related projects managed together for benefits none would give alone; operations are ongoing and repetitive. Project management applies methods to deliver within the triple constraint: scope, time and cost, with quality and risk as the pressures between them. Change one and at least one other moves.

The standard life cycle has five process groups (as in the PMI's PMBOK Guide): initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing. Two broad approaches are predictive (waterfall: plan fully, then execute in sequence) and adaptive (agile, from the 2001 Agile Manifesto: short iterations and feedback). Predictive suits stable scope; adaptive suits uncertain scope. Many real projects blend them.

Self-check A client wants a fixed date, a fixed price and extra features. Which side of the triple constraint must give, and how would you say that to the client?

Unit 2 · Initiation Phase

Initiation justifies the project and authorizes it. Typical outputs are a business case (why do this, expected benefits, options), a project charter (objectives, high-level scope, summary budget and milestones, the sponsor and the project manager's authority), and a first stakeholder register. A feasibility check asks whether it is technically, economically, legally and operationally possible.

Stakeholders are classified with a power–interest grid (Mendelow): high power and high interest means manage closely; high power and low interest, keep satisfied; low power and high interest, keep informed; low and low, monitor. Choosing among projects often uses simple measures such as payback period or net present value; the point is to compare options on stated criteria, not to trust one number.

Self-check List the sections of a one-page charter for a small software migration, and name the two stakeholders you would place in the “manage closely” cell.

Unit 3 · Project Planning, Part 1: Scope and Schedule

Planning turns the charter into a baseline. Scope is defined by requirements and decomposed into a work breakdown structure (WBS): the project split into deliverable-oriented pieces down to work packages that can be estimated and assigned. The 100% rule says the WBS must contain all the work, and only the work, in scope.

Scheduling sequences the activities and estimates their durations. The critical path method (CPM) finds the longest path through the dependency network; its length is the shortest possible project duration, and activities on it have zero float (slack). The method: a forward pass gives early start (ES) and early finish (EF = ES + duration); a backward pass gives late finish (LF) and late start (LS = LF − duration); float = LS − ES.

Unit 4 · Project Planning, Part 2: Time Estimates, Cost and Resources

Estimating combines expert judgment, analogy to past projects, and bottom-up totals from the WBS. Three-point (PERT) estimation uses an optimistic (O), most likely (M) and pessimistic (P) duration. The expected duration is (O + 4M + P)/6 and the standard deviation is about (P − O)/6. computed For O = 2, M = 5, P = 14 days, the expected duration is 6 days, the standard deviation 2 days and the variance 4 days squared.

The cost baseline sums activity costs plus a contingency reserve for known risks and, separately, a management reserve for unknown ones. Resource planning assigns people and equipment and checks for overallocation; resource leveling shifts work to remove overload, which can lengthen the schedule, while crashing adds resources to shorten it at higher cost and fast tracking overlaps activities at higher risk. Only crashing or fast tracking activities on the critical path shortens the project.

Unit 5 · Project Planning, Part 3: Risk, Quality, Procurement and Communication

A risk is an uncertain event that, if it occurs, affects objectives. A risk register lists each risk with probability, impact, owner and response. Quantitatively, expected monetary value is probability × impact; computed a 30% risk of a $20,000 loss has an expected value of $6,000. Responses are avoid, mitigate, transfer (insurance, fixed-price contract), accept, or exploit for positive risks. Residual and secondary risks should be recorded.

Procurement chooses contract types that allocate risk: fixed-price puts cost risk on the seller, cost-plus puts it on the buyer, and time-and-materials sits between. A communications plan says who needs what information, how often and by which channel (a RACI chart assigns who is responsible, accountable, consulted and informed), and a quality plan defines acceptance criteria and checks.

Unit 6 · Project Execution

Execution carries out the plan: directing the work, managing the team, managing stakeholder engagement, procuring and managing suppliers, and producing deliverables. The project manager's work is mostly people and communication: setting expectations, removing obstacles, resolving conflicts and keeping the team's attention on the critical path. Tie this back to BUS 5113: the team stages, motivation and conflict-handling modes all apply here.

Executing also generates the data that control uses: work performance information, issue logs and change requests. Scope changes should go through a defined change control process, so that each is assessed for impact on schedule, cost and risk before approval.

Unit 7 · Control

Monitoring and controlling compares actual progress against the baseline and corrects course. Earned value management (EVM) puts schedule and cost on one scale. Let BAC be the budget at completion, PV the planned value of the work scheduled by now, EV the earned value (budgeted cost of work actually done), and AC the actual cost.

  • Cost variance CV = EV − AC, schedule variance SV = EV − PV. Negative is bad.
  • Cost performance index CPI = EV / AC, schedule performance index SPI = EV / PV. Below 1 is bad.
  • Estimate at completion, if the current cost efficiency continues, EAC = BAC / CPI. Estimate to complete ETC = EAC − AC; variance at completion VAC = BAC − EAC.

Worked example computed. BAC = $100,000, PV = $40,000, EV = $35,000, AC = $42,000. Then CV = -7,000, SV = -5,000, CPI = 0.8333, SPI = 0.875. If this efficiency continues, EAC = $120,000, ETC = $78,000 and VAC = -20,000. The project is over cost and behind schedule, and to finish on the original budget the remaining work would have to run at a to-complete performance index of 1.1207.

Control also covers scope verification, quality control and risk reassessment, and ends in either corrective action or a formal change request. A trend over several periods says more than one status report.

Unit 8 · Closing and Project Success Factors

Closing finalizes all activities: formal acceptance of deliverables, completing procurement and contracts, releasing resources, archiving records, and capturing lessons learned. A post-project review compares outcomes with the business case, not only the plan, because a project can meet its triple constraint and still fail to deliver its benefit.

Frequently cited success factors are clear objectives, active executive sponsorship, realistic planning, engaged stakeholders, skilled team and manager, effective communication, change control and risk management. Failure patterns mirror them: unclear scope, scope creep, optimistic estimates and weak sponsorship. Distinguish project success (delivered to scope, time and cost) from product success (the result is used and valued).

Self-check Describe a project that met its schedule and budget but still failed. Which success factor was missing, and when could it have been caught?
