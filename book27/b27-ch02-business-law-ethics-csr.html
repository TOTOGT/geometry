Unit 1 · Introduction to Ethics in Business

Ethics asks what people ought to do; law says what they must do. The two overlap but differ: some legal acts are unethical, and some ethical acts (civil disobedience) are illegal. A working toolkit has five lenses.

LensAsksSource
UtilitarianWhich option gives the greatest net good for all affected?Bentham, Mill
Duty (deontological)Could everyone follow this rule, and does it treat people as ends, not only means?Kant
Rights and justiceAre rights respected and burdens fair, even to the worst off?Rawls (1971), rights theories
VirtueWhat would a person of good character do?Aristotle
CareWhat do relationships and vulnerability require of me?Gilligan, Noddings

Rest's four-component model (1986) shows why good people still fail: moral awareness (seeing the issue), moral judgment (deciding what is right), moral motivation (prioritizing it), and moral character (following through). A decision procedure to practice: state the facts, identify the stakeholders, name the competing values, run the lenses, check the law, choose, and test the choice by asking how it would look reported publicly.

Two corporate-responsibility positions frame the course. Friedman (1970) argued that a business's social responsibility is to increase profits within the rules. Freeman's stakeholder theory (1984) says managers owe duties to everyone affected, not only shareholders. Carroll's pyramid (1991) layers economic, legal, ethical and philanthropic responsibilities.

Self-check State the same dilemma (a supplier offers a gift before a contract decision) under two lenses. Do they agree? If not, which would you defend and why?

Unit 2 · Ethics in Accounting

Accounting ethics rests on honest reporting, because investors, lenders and regulators rely on the numbers. Typical pressures are earnings management (timing revenue or expenses to hit a target), aggressive estimates, and hiding liabilities. Professional norms stress integrity, objectivity, competence and confidentiality, and auditor independence.

Enron (bankruptcy 2001) and WorldCom (2002) are the textbook failures; the first involved off-balance-sheet entities and the second improperly capitalized expenses, and Arthur Andersen's role as auditor and consultant raised the independence question. The US response was the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, which tightened auditor oversight, made executives certify financial statements and required management to assess internal controls. These are widely reported cases; when you write about them, cite primary sources such as SEC releases, court records and official reports rather than summaries.

Self-check An analyst is asked to book next quarter's sale in this quarter to meet a target. Which of Rest's four components is most at risk, and what would you do?

Unit 3 · Ethics and Environmental Concerns

Environmental harm is the standard example of an externality: a cost imposed on people who are not party to the transaction. Hardin's “tragedy of the commons” (1968) describes how shared resources are overused when each user gains privately and the cost is spread. Responses include regulation, taxes or tradable permits that price the externality, property rights, and voluntary standards.

Elkington's triple bottom line (1994) asks firms to track people, planet and profit. Disasters such as Bhopal (1984) and the Deepwater Horizon spill (2010) are used to ask whether risk was known and who bore it. Questions for a case: who is harmed, is the harm reversible, was it foreseeable, and did the firm follow only the letter of the law while ignoring its purpose? Greenwashing, claiming environmental virtue the evidence does not support, is also a deception issue under advertising law.

Self-check A factory can meet the legal limit at low cost or cut emissions well below it at higher cost. Using stakeholder theory and Friedman's view in turn, how does the argument for each option go?

Unit 4 · Ethics in Finance

Finance concentrates conflicts of interest because advisers, brokers and managers act on behalf of others. A fiduciary duty means putting the client's interest first; a lower “suitability” standard is weaker. Core issues include insider trading (trading on material non-public information), market manipulation, misleading disclosure, and compensation that rewards risk taken with other people's money.

Frequently studied cases are Madoff's Ponzi scheme (exposed 2008), where returns to old investors were paid from new deposits, and the 2008 financial crisis, which raised questions about mortgage underwriting, rating agencies and incentives. Regulation covers disclosure and anti-fraud rules in securities law; the Dodd–Frank Act (2010) added reform and whistleblower provisions after the crisis. Again, use primary sources for any specific claim.

Self-check A fund manager is paid a share of gains but bears none of the losses. Which ethical problem does this incentive create, and what rule or contract term would reduce it?

Unit 5 · Ethics in the Law

This unit joins the legal toolkit to ethics. Core areas a manager should recognize: contract law (offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, legality), tort law (civil wrongs such as negligence), agency (when an employee's acts bind the firm), employment law (discrimination, wages, safety), intellectual property, antitrust (Sherman Act, 1890) and anti-bribery rules (US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, 1977). Corporations are legal persons, which limits shareholders' liability but not the firm's.

The key lesson is that legal compliance is a floor. Whistleblowing shows the tension: employees may owe loyalty to the firm and a duty to the public, and statutes such as Sarbanes–Oxley and Dodd–Frank give some protection from retaliation. This course content is general education, not legal advice, and rules differ by country and state.

Self-check An employee reports an accounting irregularity internally and is then demoted. Which legal and which ethical questions does this raise, and where do they differ?

Unit 6 · Ethics in Marketing

Marketing ethics covers honesty in claims, respect for consumers, and fair treatment of vulnerable groups. Deceptive or unsubstantiated advertising is regulated (in the US, mainly by the Federal Trade Commission). Issues include misleading pricing, hidden fees, targeting children or vulnerable consumers, product safety disclosures, and data privacy, especially behavioral targeting and consent. Truthful but manipulative techniques sit in the gray zone, where law is silent and the lenses of unit 1 do the work.

A practical test is the reasonable consumer: would an ordinary buyer, fully informed, still make the purchase? A second is reversibility: would you accept this practice if you were the customer?

Self-check An ad is literally true but leads most readers to a false belief. Is it ethical? Is it lawful? Answer separately.

Unit 7 · Ethics in Management

Managers shape ethical climate more than codes do. Tone at the top, how decisions and rewards actually treat misconduct, and whether speaking up is safe all matter. Typical issues are fair treatment, discrimination and harassment, surveillance of employees, conflicts of interest, pressure to meet targets, and layoffs. Ethics programs combine a code of conduct, training, reporting channels, and consequences that apply to senior people too.

Wells Fargo's sales practices (the 2016 account-opening scandal) are often used to show how aggressive targets plus weak controls can drive misconduct. As elsewhere, rely on regulators' findings and court records for specifics. The OB link: reward systems (BUS 5113 unit 3) can create the incentive that the ethics program then has to fight.

Self-check A sales quota is unreachable without bending rules. Whose responsibility is the resulting misconduct, the employee's, the manager's, or the system's? Argue one position and give the strongest counterpoint.

Unit 8 · Ethics in Corporate Planning

Strategy and ethics meet in decisions about markets, products, suppliers, mergers and risk. Questions to build into planning: who bears the downside, is the risk disclosed honestly, and how do the plan's incentives interact with safety and compliance? Cases often cited include Volkswagen's emissions defeat device (disclosed 2015) and Boeing's 737 MAX crashes (2018 and 2019), where questions arose about schedule and cost pressure versus safety and candor with regulators.

Good practice is ethics by design: integrate stakeholder analysis and a risk review into planning, define escalation paths, and set metrics that include non-financial outcomes. The course's final task is to turn the earlier frameworks into a personal ethical perspective, so keep a short written statement of the principles you would apply and the lens you trust least.

Self-check Write three sentences you could defend as your personal ethical rule for management decisions, then name one case from this chapter where it would be hardest to follow.
