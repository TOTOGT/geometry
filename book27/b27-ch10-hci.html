Unit 1 · Introduction to HCI

Human-computer interaction studies how people use computing and how to design it for them. It rests on human capacities: perception (what we see and hear), cognition (attention, working memory of roughly a handful of items, learning) and motor skill. Social factors, culture and context shape use as well. The design cycle is to understand users, design, prototype, evaluate with users and iterate. Norman's ideas of affordances (what an object invites you to do), signifiers, feedback and mental models frame most usability problems.

Self-check Name an everyday object with a poor signifier and say what a better one would be.

Unit 2 · User Interface Design

Interface design applies principles and heuristics: Nielsen's ten usability heuristics (visibility of system status, match with the real world, user control, consistency, error prevention, recognition over recall, flexibility, minimalist design, error recovery, help), plus Shneiderman's eight golden rules. Interaction styles include command line, menus, forms, direct manipulation and touch. Layout uses grouping, hierarchy, contrast and alignment. Accessibility is a design requirement: sufficient colour contrast, keyboard operation, text alternatives and clear structure serve people with disabilities and everyone else.

Evaluation methods are heuristic evaluation by experts, cognitive walkthroughs and usability testing with real users performing tasks. Test with a handful of users per round and fix what you find, then test again.

Self-check Apply two heuristics to a form that clears all fields when one field has an error.

Unit 3 · Smart Interfaces

Smart interfaces adapt to the user: recommendations, predictive text, personalization, context-aware behavior and adaptive layouts. Their problems are the ones intelligence brings: opaque behavior the user cannot predict, loss of control, mistakes that look confident, and privacy cost from the data that drives them. Good practice is to show why something was suggested, keep a manual override, and let users correct the system so it learns.

Self-check Give one example where adaptation helps and one where it frustrates, and the principle behind each.

Unit 4 · Virtual Worlds

Virtual reality immerses users in a synthetic world; augmented reality overlays digital content on the real one; mixed reality blends the two. Presence depends on tracking latency, field of view and frame rate, and failures cause discomfort (“cybersickness”). Applications include training, design, therapy, education and games. The outcomes ask for societal implications: privacy of body and gaze data, addiction and escape, identity, harassment in shared spaces, equitable access and the blurring of real and virtual.

Self-check Name two societal risks of persistent shared virtual worlds and one design measure for each.

Unit 5 · Brain Interfaces

Brain-computer interfaces read neural activity (non-invasively by EEG, or invasively by implanted electrodes) and translate it into commands, or stimulate the brain to restore function. Present uses are communication and movement for people with paralysis and clinical therapy. Signals are noisy and slow compared with keyboards, so systems need calibration and error tolerance. Ethical questions are acute: neural data privacy, consent, agency over actions, and fair access.

Self-check Why is information transfer rate low for EEG interfaces, and what does that imply for design?

Unit 6 · Input/Output

Input devices include keyboard, mouse, touch, pen, voice, gesture and sensors; output devices include displays, audio and haptics. Choice depends on task, accuracy, speed, fatigue and context. Two laws give quantitative estimates. Fitts's law models pointing time: MT = a + b · log₂(D/W + 1), where D is distance to the target and W its width. Hick's law models choice time: T = b · log₂(n + 1) for n equally likely options.

Worked example computed. With illustrative constants a = 0.2 s and b = 0.1 s per bit, a target 160 px away and 20 px wide gives MT = 0.2 + 0.1 × log₂(9) = 0.517 s. Doubling the width to 40 px gives 0.432 s, so bigger targets are faster. For Hick with b = 0.15 s per bit, a menu of 7 items takes 0.15 × log₂(8) = 0.45 s to choose from. These constants are made up to show the arithmetic; real values are measured per device and user group.

Self-check Use Fitts's law to say why a menu bar at the top edge of the screen is easy to hit.

Unit 7 · Digital Assistants

Digital assistants combine speech recognition, language understanding, dialogue management and speech synthesis, or text chat. Design issues are discoverability (users do not know what to ask), error recovery when recognition fails, turn-taking, and calibrated confidence. Always-listening devices raise privacy concerns, and accents or dialects can degrade recognition unevenly. Evaluate with task completion, error rate and satisfaction, not only recognition accuracy.

Self-check List three ways a voice assistant can signal that it did not understand, ranked from best to worst.

Unit 8 · The Internet of Things (IoT)

IoT connects everyday objects (thermostats, wearables, vehicles, factory sensors) to networks. HCI concerns are interfaces with little or no screen, setup and pairing, notification overload, and what happens when the network fails. Security and privacy matter most: devices are numerous, hard to update, and collect intimate data. Standards, firmware updates and secure defaults are design requirements, and users need clear control over what is collected.

Self-check A smart lock loses its network connection. State what the user should still be able to do and why.
