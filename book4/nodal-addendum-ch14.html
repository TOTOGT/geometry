A Local Nodal Theorem at $z = i$

What the zero set of the completed Eisenstein series looks like at the elliptic point $i$, at heights where $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}(i)}(\tfrac12+it)=0$, and what it does not tell us.

STATUS · DRAFT 2026-11-26LOCAL THEOREM · PROVED, NO RH ASSUMEDCOUNTING · OPEN (≡ RH-HARD)
Read this first

This page proves a local statement: near $z=i$, the nodal set of $E^*(z,\tfrac12+it)$ has a specific, simple shape exactly at the heights $t$ where $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}(i)}(\tfrac12+it)=0$. It does not prove, imply, or reduce the Riemann Hypothesis. The step that would matter (Step 4, counting) is open and is as hard as the problem itself.

Correction to an earlier figure title. The nodal lines cross at $i$ exactly when $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}(i)}(\tfrac12+it)=0$, that is, at zeros of $\zeta(\tfrac12+it)$ or of $L(\tfrac12+it,\chi_{-4})$. The first such crossing is at an $L$-zero, $t\approx 6.0209$, not at a zeta zero. A crossing at $i$ is not a zeta-zero detector by itself.

Labels: KNOWN classical; HAND-DERIVED proved here by hand, not machine-checked; NUMERICALLY CHECKED computed in nodal_addendum_checks.py; OPEN not done; CONJECTURE believed, not proved.

Setting

KNOWN Let $s=\tfrac12+it$ with $t$ real, $z=x+iy$ in the upper half-plane. Set

$$E(z,s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_\infty\backslash\Gamma}\operatorname{Im}(\gamma z)^{s},\qquad \Gamma=\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}),$$ $$\xi(2s)=\pi^{-s}\,\Gamma(s)\,\zeta(2s),\qquad f(z)=E^*(z,s)=\xi(2s)\,E(z,s).$$

The functional equation $E^*(z,s)=E^*(z,1-s)$ and the conjugation rule $E^*(z,\bar s)=\overline{E^*(z,s)}$ give, on the critical line where $1-s=\bar s$, that $f$ is real-valued. With $\Delta=y^2(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2)$,

$$\Delta f=-\left(\tfrac14+t^2\right)f .$$

Define the value at the elliptic point: $F(t)=f(i)$. Fourier expansion used for every computation below:

$$\begin{aligned} f(x+iy)={}&\xi(2s)\,y^{s}+\xi(2s-1)\,y^{1-s}\\ &+4\sqrt{y}\sum_{n\ge1} n^{s-1/2}\,\sigma_{1-2s}(n)\,K_{s-1/2}(2\pi n y)\cos(2\pi n x). \end{aligned}$$

Convention note (found while re-running): $\xi(w)$ here means $\pi^{-w/2}\Gamma(w/2)\zeta(w)$ evaluated at $w=2s$ and $w=2s-1$.

Step 1 · $F$ is a completed Dedekind zeta

KNOWN Classical Epstein factorization: $\sum_{(m,n)\ne(0,0)}(m^2+n^2)^{-s}=4\,\zeta(s)\,L(s,\chi_{-4})$, so $E(i,s)=2\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-4})/\zeta(2s)$ and

$$F(t)=2\,\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}(i)}\!\left(\tfrac12+it\right),\qquad \Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}(i)}(s)=\pi^{-s}\,\Gamma(s)\,\zeta(s)\,L(s,\chi_{-4}),$$

the completed Dedekind zeta function of $\mathbb{Q}(i)$.

NUMERICALLY CHECKED Draft record: Fourier series at $z=i$ (30 terms) and the factorization agree to better than $10^{-25}$ relative at $t=6.02,\,14.1347,\,20$; $f$ is real on the line to $|\operatorname{Im}f|/|f|<10^{-27}$. Independent re-run in this session (14 terms, 20 digits): $F(6.0209)=-1.2835064\times10^{-8}$ and $2\Lambda=-1.2835064\times10^{-8}$; at $t=20$ both give $-2.1193265\times10^{-13}$; at $t_1$ both are $\approx-9.57\times10^{-25}$ (zero to working precision). The values of $F$ are tiny only because $|\Gamma(\tfrac12+it)|$ decays like $e^{-\pi t/2}$.

Step 2 · Which zeros $F$ sees

KNOWN Since $\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)$ has no zeros, the zeros of $F$ on the line are the zeros of $\zeta(\tfrac12+it)$ together with those of $L(\tfrac12+it,\chi_{-4})$, multiplicities adding.

NUMERICALLY CHECKED (draft record, not re-run here) Scanning $F$ on $(0,40]$ with step $0.01$ gives 20 sign changes: 6 at zeta zeros, 14 at zeros of $L(\cdot,\chi_{-4})$, each classified by checking $|\zeta|$ and $|L|$ at the root. No common zero was seen; common zeros are not known to be impossible.

Step 3 · The local theorem at $i$

Three facts that hold for every $t$

HAND-DERIVED No hypothesis on zeros is used.

  1. (a) $\nabla f(i)=0$ for every $t$. $\sigma(z)=-1/z$ lies in $\Gamma$, fixes $i$, and has differential $d\sigma_i=-\mathrm{Id}$ (since $\sigma'(i)=1/i^2=-1$). From $f\circ\sigma=f$: $\nabla f(i)=d\sigma_i^{\top}\nabla f(i)=-\nabla f(i)$.
  2. (b) $f_{xy}(i)=0$. $f(-x+iy)=f(x+iy)$ (only $\cos 2\pi n x$ appears), so $f_x$ is odd in $x$ and $f_{xy}$ vanishes on $x=0$.
  3. (c) If $f(i)=0$, then $f_{xx}(i)+f_{yy}(i)=0$. Evaluate $\Delta f=-(\tfrac14+t^2)f$ at $y=1$.

Hence at any $t_0$ with $F(t_0)=0$, the Hessian at $i$ is

$$\operatorname{Hess} f(i)=a\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad a=f_{xx}(i)=-16\pi^2\sum_{n\ge1}n^{s+3/2}\,\sigma_{1-2s}(n)\,K_{s-1/2}(2\pi n).$$
Theorem (local nodal structure at $i$) · HAND-DERIVED

Let $F(t_0)=0$ and $a(t_0)\ne0$. Then near $i$ the nodal set $\{f=0\}$ consists of exactly two smooth curves crossing orthogonally at $i$, with tangent lines $y-1=\pm x$.

If moreover $F'(t_0)\ne0$, then as $t$ passes through $t_0$ the nodal set near $i$ undergoes the standard Morse saddle reconnection: for $t\ne t_0$ close to $t_0$ the two curves separate into the two branches of a hyperbola-like pair avoiding $i$, and the sign of $F(t)/a$ decides which pair of opposite quadrants is joined (left/right when $F/a>0$, top/bottom when $F/a<0$).

Proof. By (a)–(c), $i$ is a nondegenerate saddle critical point of $f$ with value $0$, so the Morse lemma gives coordinates with $f=\tfrac a2(u^2-v^2)$, $u,v$ tangent to $x,y$; the zero set is two transverse smooth curves with tangents $y-1=\pm x$, orthogonal because the Hessian is trace-free. For the second part, (a) holds for every $t$, so $i$ stays an exact critical point; the family is locally $f_t\simeq F(t)+\tfrac{a(t)}2(u^2-v^2)$ up to a $t$-dependent change of coordinates fixing $i$, so the unfolding is trivial except for the constant $F(t)$, which crosses $0$ transversally when $F'(t_0)\ne0$. $\square$

Honest remark

Because $i$ is a critical point for every $t$, "$i$ lies on the nodal set" is equivalent to $F(t)=0$. The theorem is a geometric restatement of $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}(i)}(\tfrac12+it_0)=0$ plus the local shape there. It carries no new arithmetic information about where the zeros are.

Open item · OPEN

Proving $a(t_0)\ne0$ at every zero $t_0$ of $F$ is not done. It is checkable zero by zero with interval arithmetic; this has not been done, only floating-point evaluation at $t_1$. A degenerate zero ($F(t_0)=a(t_0)=0$) is a codimension-2 coincidence in a one-parameter family, so one does not expect it, but that is not a proof.

Step 4 · Counting (not proved; the hard step)

OPEN To get anything about the Riemann Hypothesis one would need: the crossings at $i$ for $0<t\le T$ account for all zeros of $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}(i)}$ with $0<\operatorname{Im}\rho\le T$. That is RH for $\zeta$ and $L(s,\chi_{-4})$ together, equivalent in difficulty to a 100% Levinson-type theorem. Nothing in Steps 1–3 provides it.

HAND-DERIVED Hypothetical off-line zero (not computed). If $s$ is off the line, $f$ is complex-valued. (a) and (b) still hold for $\operatorname{Re}f$ and $\operatorname{Im}f$ separately. If $f(i)=0$ there, then $\Delta f=s(s-1)f$ gives $\Delta f(i)=0$, so each has a trace-free diagonal Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(b,-b)$ and, when $b\ne0$, an orthogonal nodal pair along the same lines $y-1=\pm x$. The local picture does not distinguish on-line from off-line zeros, so it places no constraint on where zeros are. A floating-point check at the arbitrary value $s=0.7+3i$ (not a zero) confirmed $\nabla f(i)=0$, $f_{xy}(i)=0$ and $\Delta f(i)=s(s-1)f(i)$ (draft record).
