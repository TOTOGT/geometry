Modular Equations and π

Ramanujan wrote a formula for $1/\pi$ that gains eight correct digits every time you add a term. This chapter shows you where it comes from, and how to build your own.

Here is the formula. Everything in this chapter is about where the numbers in it come from.
$$\frac{1}{\pi} \;=\; \frac{2\sqrt 2}{9801}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(4n)!\,(1103 + 26390\,n)}{(n!)^4\,396^{4n}}$$

Take one term and you have $1/\pi$ to eight decimal places. Take twelve and you have it to a hundred. It is the most efficient formula for $\pi$ anyone had found for seventy years, and Ramanujan published it in 1914 without saying where it came from.

Look at the two odd numbers in it: 9801 out front, and 396 underneath. Hold on to those. They are the same number twice.

1 · A function that has no business being nice

Start somewhere that looks unrelated. There is a function called the elliptic modulus, written $k(q)$. It is built as a ratio of two infinite sums. Nothing about it is tidy.

Now evaluate it at a very particular kind of place — at $q = e^{-\pi\sqrt N}$, for a whole number $N$. Write the answer as $\alpha_N$, which by convention means $k$ squared.

Something strange happens. The infinite sums collapse. At $N = 58$ you get:

$$\alpha_{58} = (13\sqrt{58} - 99)^2\,(99 - 70\sqrt 2)^2$$

Square roots. Whole numbers. No infinite sum anywhere. Compute it both ways at 120 digits — grinding through the theta series, and evaluating those radicals — and the two agree to eighty places.

And there is 99 again, twice over, in a formula written down for a completely different reason.

One convention, and it will bite you $\alpha_N$ is $k$ squared. If you compute the theta ratio and forget to square it, everything downstream is wrong by a factor you will not spot for a while.

This is not a hypothetical. It is the oldest live bug in this material. Ramanujan's second letter to Hardy gives a value for $k_{210}$ that turns out, when you compute it, to be $\alpha_{210}$ — the square of what it is labelled. That discrepancy is still unresolved. Whichever way it goes, the moral holds: write down which one you mean.

2 · The dial

From $\alpha_N$ you build one more quantity, the class invariant:

$$g_N^{12} = \frac{(k'_N)^2}{2k_N}, \qquad k' = \sqrt{1-\alpha}$$

And from that, the number that actually drives the series:

$$x_N = \frac{1}{64\,(g_N^{12} + g_N^{-12})^2}$$

At $N = 58$ this comes out to exactly $1/396^4$.

That is where the 396 comes from. It is not a choice. You picked $N = 58$ and the formula handed you 396.

This is the useful part. $N$ is a dial, and it sets your convergence rate. Bigger $N$ means smaller $x_N$, which means a bigger number downstairs, which means more digits per term. Ramanujan turned it to 58 and got 8 digits a term. The Chudnovsky brothers turned it to 163 and got 14. You can read the rate off before computing a single term.

3 · Why you add before you invert

This next step looks like housekeeping. It is the heart of the thing.

Compute $g_{58}^{12}$ numerically and you get:

$$g_{58}^{12} = 19601.99999489847\ldots$$

It is almost 19602. It is a bit closer to 19602 than to 19601. It is neither. If you round it, you have made something up.

What it actually is:

$$g_{58}^{12} = \varepsilon^6 = 9801 + 1820\sqrt{29}, \qquad \varepsilon = \frac{5+\sqrt{29}}{2}$$

There is 9801 a third time. And $\varepsilon$ is not a random number — it is the fundamental unit of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{29})$, the smallest building block of a whole system of numbers.

Units have norms. This one has norm $-1$. So $\varepsilon^6$ has norm $+1$. Written out, that statement is:

$$9801^2 - 29\cdot 1820^2 = 1$$

Pell's equation. Exactly 1, in whole numbers.

Now, norm $+1$ means something very concrete: the conjugate is the inverse. So

$$g^{-12} = 9801 - 1820\sqrt{29}$$

and when you add the two together, the $\sqrt{29}$ parts cancel:

$$g^{12} + g^{-12} = 19602$$

Exactly. Not 19601.99999. Thirty zeros after the decimal point, and you can prove it rather than measure it.

That is why the series has whole numbers in it. An irrational quantity plus its own inverse, where Pell's equation guarantees the halves cancel.

And the loop closes $19602 = 2 \times 99^2$. Put it back into the dial: $$64 \times 19602^2 = 24{,}591{,}257{,}856 = 396^4$$ Both sides are $256 \times 99^4$. The 9801 in front of the series and the 396 underneath it are the same 99, arriving by two different roads.

4 · Building your own

The procedure, so you can run it at an $N$ nobody has worked:

Recipe
  1. Pick $N$. Compute $\alpha_N$ from the theta series at high precision. Don't look it up — computing it is the check.
  2. Form $g_N^{12} = (1-\alpha_N)/(2\sqrt{\alpha_N})$.
  3. Add: $S = g_N^{12} + g_N^{-12}$.
  4. Is $S$ a whole number? If not, stop. Either your precision is too low, or $N$ is not one of the values where this closes.
  5. $x_N = 1/(64S^2)$. That is your denominator, and $\log_{10}(1/x_N)$ minus the growth of $(4n)!/(n!)^4$ is your digits per term.

Step 4 is the whole safety net. If $S$ comes out ragged, something is wrong and you find out immediately instead of after building a series that does not converge.

Two smaller things. Step 4's test tells you when you have failed, not when you will succeed. And nothing here proves the series converges to $1/\pi$ — it is checked to 104 digits, which is good evidence and is not a proof.
