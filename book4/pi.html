Principia Orthogona · Book 4 · Chapter 3½ · Between the third and fourth dimension

π: a set whose dimension is 3.14159…

Chapters 1½ and 2½ met sets between one and two dimensions and between two and three. This one asks the question from the other end: can a dimension be a number like π, and what has to be true of the space around it? The answer is yes for a set and no for a vector space, and the construction fits on one line.

1 · Two meanings of "dimension"

A vector space has a whole-number dimension: the number of independent directions. A set has other dimensions that measure how it fills space, and those take any real value from 0 up to the dimension of the space it sits in. The similarity dimension of Chapter 1½, log N / log(1/r), is one of them; the tent curve came out at exactly 4/3.

That upper limit is the whole constraint. A set with dimension 3.14159… is bigger than anything that fits in ordinary three-dimensional space, so it needs at least four dimensions around it. Four is enough, because 3.14159… is less than 4. Chapter 4, the hypercube, is exactly the four-dimensional container this set lives in.

2 · The construction, by hand

Let s = π − 3 = 0.14159…, and let C be the Cantor set made of two pieces, each a copy of the whole scaled by r, one at each end of [0, 1]. Choose r so the similarity equation holds:

2 · rs = 1, so r = 2−1/s = 0.0074812… (that is 1/r = 133.67…)

Since r is less than 1/2 the two pieces do not touch, so the set is a genuine Cantor dust, and its dimension is log 2 / log(1/r) = s. The step from the similarity equation to the Hausdorff dimension is a standard theorem about self-similar sets with non-overlapping pieces (the open set condition; Moran and Hutchinson). It is quoted here, not derived.

Now take C × [0, 1]³, a Cantor dust times a solid cube, which sits in R⁴. The box-counting argument is elementary and worth seeing, because the number π − 3 does all the work. The level-k picture of C has 2k intervals of length rk. Cover C × [0,1]³ by cubes of side ε = rk: each interval needs one cube across, and the cube [0,1]³ needs (1/ε)³ of them, so the count is

N(ε) = 2k · (r−k)³ = (r−k)s · (r−k)³ = ε−(3+s) = ε−π

using 2 = r−s. So at the scales ε = rk the box count grows exactly like ε−π. That gives the box-counting dimension at those scales. That the Hausdorff dimension equals it is the standard product theorem for Hausdorff dimension (the product of a set with R³, where both Hausdorff and box dimension of the cube are 3, adds the dimensions). That step is the product formula for Hausdorff dimension found in standard texts on fractal geometry (Falconer's, for example); it is quoted, not derived.

At the level-aligned scales ε = rk the exact count log N / log(1/ε) equals s at every level checked (1 to 6). Between those scales the count wobbles, which is what a very sparse set does: at ε = 10−1 … 10−10 the estimate ranges from 0.30 down to 0.162, and a straight regression over those decades gave 0.158 against the true 0.142. That is the expected behaviour of box counting on a gappy set over a finite range, and it is the reason to prefer the exact level count to a fitted slope.

3 · The lattice trap

The constructions in Chapter 1½ cut a unit square into a grid and keep N of m² cells, so their dimension is log N / log m with whole numbers N and m. In the unit 4-cube the same recipe allows any N up to m⁴. That gives dimensions that are logarithms of whole numbers, and it can get as close to π as you like: the verifier found m = 37 with N = 84,460 pieces giving 3.141593, an error of about 4 × 10−8.

It does not give π exactly. Hitting it would need mπ to be a whole number, and this check does not settle whether that can happen. The construction above sidesteps the question by using a ratio that is not a reciprocal of a whole number (1/r = 133.67…), which a lattice cannot do. The honest statement is: the lattice route approximates π, and the non-lattice ratio realises it exactly.

4 · Can mπ be a whole number?

The lattice route would hit π exactly only if mπ = N for whole numbers m and N. As far as the author knows this is an open question; even "is 2π rational?" is listed as unknown. Below is every route considered, with what it actually gives.

RouteWhat it givesStatus
1 · Certified bracketingmπ lies strictly between two consecutive integers for every m from 2 to 1,000,000 (999,999 values, none undecided). By hand: 8 < 2π = 8.82497… < 9 and 31 < 3π = 31.54428… < 32.Proved for those m, by interval arithmetic (pi-integer-power.py). Nothing beyond the limit.
2 · Six exponentials theoremFor any three distinct primes, at least one of pπ is transcendental, hence not a whole number. So at most two primes p can have pπ algebraic. (Take xi = ln pi and y = (1, π). The theorem is usually stated with two x's and three y's; the set of products xiyj is the same with the roles swapped.) The four exponentials conjecture would sharpen this to at most one prime.Quoted theorem (see M. Waldschmidt's survey slides, Hyderabad 2003); the consequence is one step. Speaks about primes, not directly about composite m.
3 · Gelfond–Schneider, BakerNothing. They need an algebraic exponent, or algebraic coefficients in a linear form of logarithms, and π is neither.Does not apply.
4 · Schanuel's conjectureWould, as far as the author knows, settle whether mπ is algebraic.Conditional on an unproved conjecture; the implication is not derived here.
5 · HeuristicFractional parts of mπ behave like random numbers, so an exact whole-number hit has probability zero. The nearest approach for m up to 106 is 2.9 × 10−7 (at m = 973,492), about what a random sample of that size would give.Evidence only, not proof.

Each route stalls at a known place. Route 1 is finite and cannot reach every m. Route 2 constrains primes and leaves up to two of them open, and a composite mπ is a product of prime powers of π that could in principle be algebraic even if none of the factors is. 5 · What this is, and is not

  • This is a construction, not a natural object. A Cantor dust times a solid is a clean way to realise a dimension. It says nothing about whether any physical or dynamical set has dimension near π.
  • Where natural 3–4 dimensional sets might appear. As far as I know, a chaotic flow in four variables can have a Kaplan–Yorke dimension between 3 and 4, as the three-variable flows of Chapter 2½ have values between 2 and 3. None is computed in this chapter, so it makes no claim about any 4D system.
  • π is not special. The same recipe gives e, 3.2 or 3.9. A dimension equal to π would need its own reason to matter, and none is given here.
  • Machine-checked: the algebra only. PiDimension.lean proves, for every s > 0, that 2·rs = 1 for r = 2−1/s; that the pieces are disjoint for 0 < s < 1; and the box-count identity 2k·(ε−1)3 = (ε−1)3+s, also with 3 + s = π. It compiles on Lean v4.32.0 with only the standard axioms. It does not prove that the Hausdorff dimension equals π; that step is the quoted theorem. The certificates for mπ in §4 are computed, not Lean-checked.
  • Not a link to the series' other constants. The π here is just a target value for the dimension. The chapter does not claim any connection to the π that appears elsewhere in the dm³ chapters.

Exercises

  • Build a dimension-3.5 set: show r = 1/4 works for the two-piece Cantor set, and check that 2 · (1/4)1/2 = 1.
  • Why can no set of dimension 3.14 sit in R³? Say it using the container rule.
  • Replace two pieces by three. For d = π, find r from 3 · rs = 1 and check the pieces are disjoint (you need r < 1/3).
  • Find r for d = e. Is it larger or smaller than for π, and why?
